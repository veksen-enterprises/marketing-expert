## Start from where the reader is (Schwartz, Breakthrough Advertising, 1966) [practitioner]

Awareness determines where the headline starts:
- **Most aware**: knows the product and wants it. Lead with the offer, price, deadline.
- **Product-aware**: knows the product, not yet convinced. Lead with proof, comparison, risk reversal.
- **Solution-aware**: knows the kind of solution, not you. Lead with your differentiated mechanism.
- **Problem-aware**: feels the pain, doesn't know solutions exist. Lead with the problem in their words, then the category of answer.
- **Unaware**: lead with a story, identity or surprising insight. The hardest; usually a brand or content job, not a landing page job.

(The "problem/solution/product aware" labels are later relabelings of Schwartz's descriptions.)

Market sophistication (how many similar claims the market has heard) determines the claim type:
1. First in market: state the claim plainly.
2. Competitors copied: enlarge the claim (bigger, faster, more specific).
3. Claims are tired: introduce a new mechanism (how it works).
4. Rival mechanisms: elaborate the mechanism.
5. Market disbelieves claims: shift to identification with the prospect.

Most B2B SaaS categories are at stage 3–5. "Save time" no longer lands. Show the mechanism or get specific.

## Use the customer's words [practitioner]

- **Review mining** (Joanna Wiebe / Copyhackers): collect reviews of you and competitors (G2, Capterra, Amazon, app stores). Bucket them into pains, desired outcomes, alternatives tried, purchase triggers, objections. Count frequency. Lift exact phrases. 3-star reviews hold the most balanced detail. Results reported are single self-reported tests: useful method, no evidence of the size of effect.
- **Switch interviews** (see customer-research) give you the trigger moments and anxieties.
- **Sales call recordings and support tickets**: the objections you must answer on the page.

LLM-clustered reviews are fine for sorting; always check phrases against the raw text.

## What makes copy work (rules with mechanisms)

- **Specific beats general.** "Close your books in 3 days, not 10" vs "faster close". Specific claims are checkable, which makes them more credible, and they tell the reader whether it applies to them.
- **One reader, one job.** Copy written to everyone is read by no one. Name the role and situation.
- **Benefit tied to a mechanism.** A benefit with no "how" sounds like every competitor; a mechanism with no benefit makes the reader do the translation.
- **Proof next to the claim.** Numbers, named customers, screenshots, third-party ratings, guarantees. A claim on line 1 and proof in the footer doesn't connect.
- **Answer the objections that actually block purchase** (price, switching cost, risk, "will it work for us"). Get them from sales calls, not imagination.
- **Reader-centric framing.** Count "you" vs "we". Company-centric copy talks about the mission; reader-centric copy talks about the reader's problem.
- **Clarity before cleverness.** If a 5-second test can't recover what you do and for whom, wordplay is costing you.
- **CTA states what the reader gets** ("Get my audit"), not what they do ("Submit").

## Headline and subhead

A homepage or landing-page hero should answer, in about 10 seconds: what is it, who is it for, why is it better than the alternative, and what do I do next.
- Headline: the value or outcome, in the reader's terms.
- Subhead: what the product is (category) + for whom + how (mechanism).
- Supporting visual that shows the product doing the thing, not an abstract illustration.

## Critique checklist

Run analyze_copy first, then:
1. Can a stranger state what it is and who it's for after one read?
2. Which competitor could publish this exact sentence? If any could, it isn't differentiated.
3. Every adjective: is there a number or proof that could replace it?
4. Does each claim have proof within one scroll?
5. Are the top three objections answered?
6. Is the awareness level right for the traffic source (cold paid social vs branded search)?
7. Is there one primary CTA, and does it say what you get?
8. Read aloud: anything you wouldn't say to a customer face to face?

## Message testing [research] / [practitioner]

Evidence hierarchy for copy decisions, strongest first:
1. A properly powered A/B test on real traffic against a business metric (see experimentation).
2. Panel message tests (e.g. Wynter-style: verified target buyers answer open questions on clarity, relevance, value, differentiation). Diagnostic, not predictive: stated reactions from paid panelists reading cold. Saturation for themes typically at roughly 9–17 responses in qualitative research literature; this finds themes, not their prevalence.
3. 5-second tests: measure first impressions and recall only. Peer-reviewed work (Gronier 2016; Kuric et al. 2024) supports validity for first impressions, but results are affected by visual complexity and participant cognitive abilities.
4. Internal opinion.

Low-traffic B2B sites usually can't A/B test copy (see experimentation); panel tests plus sales-call feedback are the realistic option.

## Common mistakes

- Writing copy before positioning is settled.
- Hype words (revolutionary, seamless, cutting-edge): unbelievable and indistinguishable.
- Feature lists without the "so what".
- Clever headlines that require context the reader doesn't have.
- Copying a competitor's structure and therefore their positioning.
- Declaring a copy "winner" from a test without enough traffic.
