## Base rates

- **No exit is the most common outcome.** In a CB Insights cohort of 2008–2010 US tech seed companies, ~30% exited by 2018 [vendor]. Ghosh (HBS): of ~2,000 VC-backed companies (2004–2010), ~75% never returned cash to investors and 30–40% liquidated [research, unpublished dataset].
- **Acquisition is the most common good exit.** US VC-backed exits in 2025: 995 acquisitions ($112.7B) vs 62 IPOs ($119.4B) (PitchBook-NVCA) [first-party]. Roughly 75–95% of exits by count are acquisitions, depending on year and definition.
- **Most acquisitions are small.** Carta 2022: deals under $5M were 44–55% of startup M&A by quarter; over $100M, 3–11%. Most acquired startups are early-stage (63.7% of H1 2025 acquisitions were pre-seed to early-stage, PitchBook). [first-party]
- **Shutdowns are rising**: Carta counted 966 US startup shutdowns in 2024, up from 769 in 2023. [first-party]

## What founders actually receive

Sale proceeds flow through the **liquidation waterfall**: debts and costs, then preferred shareholders up to their preference, then common. If the price is below the preference stack, common stock gets nothing.
- 1× non-participating preference is the norm; > 1× appeared in ~8% of rounds (Carta Q1 2024).
- In trade sales, VCs gave the team "carve-outs" in **45%** of deals, averaging **9%** of deal value (Broughman & Fried) [research].
- **Acqui-hires** typically split consideration: a small amount buys the company (goes through the waterfall to investors) and a larger amount goes to hired people as buyer equity and bonuses.

Run the waterfall at 0.5×, 1× and 2× of total preferences before any conversation. If common is underwater, your outcome depends on carve-outs and retention packages: negotiate those explicitly.

## Why acquirers buy

- **Technology and talent, to save time**: Gautier & Lamesch (175 Google/Apple/Facebook/Amazon/Microsoft acquisitions 2015–2017) found acquirers mainly integrated functionality, technology, talent and IP; many acquired products stopped existing under their own brand. M&A worked as a substitute for in-house R&D. [research]
- **Removing a competitor**: in pharma, ~6% of acquisitions are "killer acquisitions": overlapping projects 23.4% less likely to be developed after purchase, clustered just below merger-review thresholds (Cunningham, Ederer & Ma 2021) [research]. In tech, evidence of burying is rare; evidence of absorbing is common.
- **Does Big Tech buying chill investment?** Theory says it could ("kill zone", Kamepalli, Rajan & Zingales 2020); evidence found the opposite short-term: Big Tech acquisitions were followed by *more* VC investment in that sub-industry (Prado & Bauer 2022, 392 acquisitions). [research]
- **Revenue and customers**: no study split by motive was found; most targets are early-stage with little revenue, which suggests technology and team dominate by count. [inference]

Small acquirers buying private targets earn positive announcement returns on average (Moeller, Schlingemann & Stulz 2004; Betton, Eckbo & Thorburn). Buying startups is the part of M&A that tends to work for buyers, which is a negotiating point. [research]

## After the deal: people leave, products get absorbed

- Acquired employees are about **twice as likely to leave** as similar regular hires; the gap shrinks when the founding team stays together and the acquired unit is kept separate (Kim 2024, US Census data). [research]
- Acquired employees turn over faster than matched direct hires, especially senior and technical staff (Ng & Stuart 2022, 30M+ résumés). [research]
- Of 454 founders acquired by Google and Facebook, **77% left**; mean tenure 3.7 years; ~30% gone in 2 years, 27% remaining at year 5; serial founders left earliest (Seitz & Lehmann). [research]
- Assume your product will be absorbed or shut down unless the contract says otherwise.

## Regulatory climate (2023–2026)

- US: 2023 Merger Guidelines kept by the new FTC/DOJ leadership (Feb 2025). The expanded merger filing form (Feb 2025) was vacated by a court (Feb 2026); the FTC appeal is pending. [regulator / law-firm alerts]
- Blocked or abandoned deals: **Adobe–Figma** abandoned Dec 2023 ($1B termination fee to Figma; Figma later IPO'd, Jul 2025). **Amazon–iRobot** abandoned Jan 2024 ($94M fee); iRobot laid off 31% the same day and filed Chapter 11 in Dec 2025. **Microsoft–Activision** closed Oct 2023 after restructuring for the UK CMA.
- **AI license-and-hire deals** ("reverse acqui-hires"): Microsoft–Inflection (Mar 2024, ~$650M, mostly a licence; UK CMA treated it as a merger and cleared it), Amazon–Adept (Jun 2024; investors roughly repaid; FTC inquiry), Google–Character.AI (Aug 2024, ~$2.7B; DOJ review reported). Investors got repaid through licence fees, hired staff got buyer compensation, and **remaining employees were left with a hollowed-out company**. Google–Windsurf and Meta–Scale AI (2025) followed similar structures [unverified this session].

If the likely buyer is a dominant platform overlapping its core business, expect long reviews or a block. Negotiate a reverse break fee, and ask whether you could survive a failed deal (Figma did; iRobot didn't).

## Should you build for acquisition?

For:
- Acquisition is the most likely good exit.
- A product that's an obvious missing piece of a large platform has a clear buyer set.
- Buyers of small private companies tend to do well, so they keep buying.

Against:
- Base rates are low: most companies never exit. A plan whose only payoff is a sale has a fragile base case.
- Buyer appetite is cyclical and regulator-exposed.
- **Single-buyer risk**: if the one natural buyer builds it instead, your value goes to zero (see platform-and-feature-risk).
- Signalling a sale worries customers (will it be shut down?) and employees.

Rule: build a company that works on its own, and make it **acquirable**, not dependent on being acquired. Only build "a feature to sell" if at least two credible buyers exist and you can survive if the platform builds it.

## Staying acquirable (useful even if you never sell)

- Clean cap table; simple, 1× non-participating preferences.
- Signed IP assignment from every founder, employee and contractor.
- Diligence-ready finances: monthly close, correct revenue recognition, contracts without change-of-control traps.
- Real partnerships (integrations, co-selling) with likely buyers: they create the internal champion who argues "buy, don't build." [practitioner]
- Be the best option in a niche a large company needs.
- A founding team that stays together (it lowers post-deal turnover, which buyers know).

"Companies are bought, not sold" is common banker advice: relationships 12–24 months before any process, competitive tension from two or more interested buyers as the main price lever. No original source was found. [practitioner]

## Numbers to stop quoting

"70–90% of acquisitions fail" (HBR 2011) has no primary source in the article; it likely descends from KPMG 1999 (17% of large deals added value by one-year share price). It's about large public acquirers, not startup acquisitions. [vendor]

## Decision rules

1. Plan for no exit first, acquisition second, IPO third.
2. Run the waterfall before any conversation; negotiate carve-outs and retention if common is underwater.
3. Don't build a feature-to-sell company with only one possible buyer.
4. With a dominant-platform buyer, price in regulatory time; get a meaningful reverse break fee.
5. In a license-and-hire offer, get in writing what happens to remaining employees and common shareholders.
6. Expect to leave within ~4 years; negotiate team integrity and structural separation, or price the retention accordingly.
7. Earn acquirability through hygiene and partnerships, not pitching.
