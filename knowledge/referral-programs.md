## What the research shows about referred customers

**Referred customers are worth more, on average** [research]
- Schmitt, Skiera & Van den Bulte (2011, *Journal of Marketing*) followed about 10,000 customers of a German bank for almost three years. Referred customers had a higher contribution margin (the gap shrank over time), a higher retention rate (the gap stayed), and were more valuable in both the short and long run. Lifetime value was **at least 16% higher** than for similar non-referred customers acquired at the same time.
- The value gap **differed across customer segments**, so the authors recommend being selective about whom you push to refer.
- The follow-up study (Van den Bulte, Bayer, Skiera & Schmitt 2018, *Journal of Marketing Research*; ~1,800 referred customers at the same bank, €25 reward to the referrer) tested two reasons: **better matching** (referrers know who will fit) and **social enrichment** (the referrer helps the new customer use the product). Treat the exact mechanism results as [not re-verified]; the direction is that both help explain the gap.
- Limits: one bank, one country, one reward level. Expect the direction to hold for products where fit matters; don't plan on exactly 16%.

**Your biggest buyers are not always your best referrers** [research, HBR summary]
- Kumar, Petersen & Leone (2007, *Harvard Business Review*, telecom and financial-services data) separated **customer lifetime value** (what a customer buys) from **customer referral value** (the value of the customers they bring). Many high spenders refer little and some low spenders refer a lot. Ask both groups, and give different asks: big spenders to refer, strong referrers to buy more.

**Why people talk** [research]
- Berger (2014, *Journal of Consumer Psychology*) reviews word of mouth (WOM) research and finds it serves the sender: managing how others see them, regulating emotion, getting information, social bonding and persuading others. Practical reading: make sharing make the sender look good or helpful, and give them something specific and easy to say (see messaging-and-copy).

**Rewards and tie strength** [research, lab experiments]
- Ryu & Feick (2007, *Journal of Marketing*, four experiments): rewards increase stated referral likelihood, most of all for **weak ties** (acquaintances) and **weaker brands**. With strong ties and strong brands, giving at least part of the reward to the **receiver** worked better than rewarding only the sender. Measured as intention, not behaviour.

## Net Promoter Score: useful question, weak growth predictor

- NPS asks "How likely are you to recommend us?" (0–10); % of 9–10 scores minus % of 0–6. Reichheld (2003, HBR) called it "the one number you need to grow".
- Critics noted the original analysis correlated NPS with **past**, not future, growth [research, secondary].
- Keiningham et al. (2007, *Journal of Marketing*, 21 firms, 15,500+ interviews, Norwegian data) **failed to replicate** NPS's claimed superiority over other satisfaction measures such as the American Customer Satisfaction Index. A second Keiningham et al. (2007, customer-level panel of 8,000+ US customers) found recommend intention helps predict recommending behaviour but should not be used alone. [research]
- Use: NPS can track sentiment and find candidates to ask for referrals and reviews. Do not use it as proof that word of mouth is happening; measure actual referrals.

## Program design

**Prerequisites** [practitioner]
- Referral programs amplify existing satisfaction; they do not create it. Check retention cohorts and organic "a friend told me" answers in self-reported attribution first (channel-strategy).
- The product must be easy to explain in one sentence and the new user must reach value fast; otherwise referred sign-ups churn.

**Double-sided rewards** [practitioner, with research support for receiver rewards]
- A **double-sided** reward gives something to both the referrer and the new customer. It removes the awkwardness of "I'm earning money from you" and gives the friend a reason to act now. Ryu & Feick's result supports a receiver reward for close ties.
- **Reward in product value where possible** (more storage, credit, free month, upgraded tier). It costs you less than cash at face value, attracts people who want the product, and is less attractive to fraudsters. [practitioner]
- Cash or gift cards suit products with infrequent purchase (finance, insurance, energy) where there's no product credit to give.

**Timing: ask at moments of value** [practitioner]
- Trigger the ask right after the user experiences success: first successful project, a completed delivery, a saved amount shown, a high satisfaction score, a renewal. These are the same triggers lifecycle email uses (email-and-lifecycle).
- Put the invite where sharing is natural in the product (inviting a collaborator, sharing a file or report). Product-based sharing often beats a separate "refer and earn" page.
- Remind referrers when a friend joins and when they earn; show progress.

**Reward size** [practitioner]
- Set the ceiling from unit economics: total reward (both sides) ≤ the CAC you accept for a customer of that value. Referred customers' higher value (above) can justify a somewhat higher ceiling than paid channels, but test.
- Bigger rewards raise volume but also attract people who refer for the money, which can lower the matching benefit and raise fraud. Test two or three reward levels with a randomized split (experimentation) and compare **retained** referred customers per dollar, not sign-ups.
- Tiered or capped rewards (e.g. up to N rewards per year) control cost and abuse.

**Fraud and abuse** [practitioner]
- Common: self-referral with second accounts, coupon-site posting of referral codes, fake sign-ups, refund after reward.
- Controls: pay the reward only after the new customer pays and passes a refund window; device and payment-method matching; caps per referrer; block public posting in terms if you want referrals between people who know each other; manual review of top referrers.

## The Dropbox case: what is verified

- **First-party, filed with the SEC** (S-1, 2018): Dropbox said it acquires users "efficiently" through word of mouth, in-product referrals and content sharing, and that over 90% of revenue came from self-serve channels. [first-party, snippet-only]
- **From Drew Houston's 2010 "Startup Lessons Learned" talk**, as reported by secondary sources: the two-sided referral (500MB extra storage for both people) "permanently increased signups by 60%"; users grew from about 100,000 (Sep 2008) to 4 million (Jan 2010); referrals made up about 35% of daily sign-ups; users sent about 2.8 million direct invites in 30 days (April 2010). The slides could not be opened this session. [anecdote; first-party talk via secondary relay, not re-verified]
- The often-quoted "3900% growth" is a marketing-blog calculation (100K → 4M), not a Dropbox metric. Don't quote it.
- What generalizes: storage was a reward that cost Dropbox little, made the product better for the referrer, and was useful only to people who wanted the product. What doesn't: Dropbox had an unusually shareable product (shared folders pull in new users without any reward).

## Measuring incrementality of referrals

Referral tracking credits every sign-up that used a referral link, but some of those people would have joined anyway (they heard about you and the link was just convenient). [practitioner]
- **Holdout test**: randomly withhold the program (or the reward) from a share of eligible users for a period, and compare total new customers brought in per user, including those who arrived without a link. See experimentation for sample sizes.
- **Reward-level test**: randomize reward size; if volume barely changes, most referrals were not caused by the reward.
- **Geo or time switch-off**: turn the program off in one market for some weeks and watch total sign-ups (metrics-and-measurement).
- **Quality**: track referred cohorts' 3-, 6- and 12-month retention and margin against other channels; if they don't beat other channels, the matching benefit isn't present.
- **K-factor** (invites per user × conversion per invite) describes viral spread; a value above 1 means each user brings in more than one new user. It is rarely sustained; don't plan on it. [practitioner]

## What usually works by stage

- **Pre-product-market fit**: no formal program. Ask happy users personally for introductions; record "how did you hear about us?" at sign-up. [practitioner]
- **Early traction**: a simple double-sided reward in product value, triggered at the first moment of value; manual fraud checks.
- **Scaling**: dedicated referral tooling, reward-level tests, holdout measurement, fraud rules, referral asks in lifecycle email.
- **B2B**: replace rewards with customer references, case studies, review-site asks and introductions; many buyers' employers forbid personal rewards. See partnerships-and-affiliates for paid referral partners.

## Common mistakes

- Launching a referral program to fix weak retention.
- Rewarding sign-ups instead of paid, retained customers.
- Asking at a random time (e.g. day one) instead of after a success.
- Treating NPS as a measure of word of mouth.
- Quoting the Dropbox "3900%" figure or assuming Dropbox-level results.
- Counting all referral-link sign-ups as incremental.
