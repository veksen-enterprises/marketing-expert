This playbook is for a business that sells something about **one event** to the people who took part in it or watched it, and puts it in front of them **within days**. The plain example is a framed print of a marathon's course with the buyer's name and finish time, advertised to runners in that city the week after the race. The same pattern works for concerts, finals, festivals and graduations, and, without a physical product, for any business whose audience lives through a shared moment: a framework release, a price change, a deadline, a public outage. For year-round seasonal demand, see **seasonal-and-hobby-products**. For paid ads in general, see **paid-acquisition**. For message and consent law, see **privacy-and-marketing-law**.

## What an event-moment product is

_In short:_ One event, the people who lived it, something about that event, sold or shown while they still care. All four parts must be there.

- **One event, with a date.** Not a season or a topic: this race, this concert, this release.
- **The people who lived it.** Participants (runners, graduates, players) or attendees and followers (fans, users of the released software). Participants usually care more because the moment is about them [practitioner].
- **Something about the event.** A keepsake, a recap, a report, a check, a fix. It should show the event's **end** or its **peak**: the finish time, the final score, the setlist, the version number.
- **While they still care.** Prepared before the event, shown in the first days after it.

**Why the end matters.** People judge an experience they remember mostly by its worst or best moment and its end, not by how long it lasted. In a lab test, 69% of people chose to repeat a longer cold-water trial because it ended slightly better (Kahneman et al. 1993) [research; lab experiment, n=32]. A finish time or final score is that end moment. That a print of it sells better than other designs is our inference, not tested.

## Which events work

_In short:_ Keep an event only if it passes all five checks. Most events fail at least one.

| Check | Passes | Fails |
|---|---|---|
| A peak people are proud of | A marathon, a graduation, a final won | A commute, a routine meeting |
| Enough people | Tens of thousands of finishers or attendees | A few dozen |
| You can reach them | They live in one city, or follow one interest | Scattered, with nothing in common to target |
| Data you may use | Date, place, distance, score, the route drawn from open map data | The organiser's logo, map graphic or photos |
| A version you can make in advance | One template per event type, filled per buyer | A design that needs days of work after the event |

- **Field sizes are large and public.** The 2025 New York City Marathon had 59,226 finishers (preliminary) [first-party; NYRR press release]; the 2026 London Marathon had 59,830 [first-party; London Marathon Events]. Montréal's 2026 edition expected 35,000 participants across all distances [first-party; city pre-event figure, not finishers].
- **Longer races buy more.** A race-photo platform says purchase rates are high for marathons, ultras and long triathlons, good for halves and trail races, and lower for 5 km and 10 km [vendor; Sportpxl, no numbers given].
- **Runners spend on running.** In one North American survey, respondents spent an average of $1,748 a year on running, and 57% agreed "I wish races offered something other than a finisher t-shirt" [first-party; Running USA 2022, n=5,500+, convenience sample of keen runners].

## Why people buy

_In short:_ People value experiences, and a keepsake is a way to hold on to one. Putting their own data on it probably helps, but that is not proven.

- **Experiences make people happier than things.** In a US phone survey, 57% said an experiential purchase made them happier, against 34% for a material one; the authors link this to identity and to retelling the experience (Van Boven & Gilovich 2003) [research; 2 surveys + 2 experiments, survey n=1,263]. A keepsake is a thing that stands for an experience; the study did not test such objects.
- **Self-designed products earn a higher price.** People bid over 40% more for a T-shirt they designed themselves than for the same shirt off the shelf, and the gap grew when they liked the result (Franke, Schreier & Kaiser 2010) [research; 5 auction experiments, n≈20–129 each, mostly students]. The effect came from the feeling of having made it. Typing your name and time into a template is very little making, so **expect a smaller effect, if any**; nobody has tested it.
- **Souvenirs keep a trip memorable when they are unique and usable** [research; qualitative interviews, one Finnish destination, abstract only].

## Three ways to run it

_In short:_ Partner with the organiser, sell without them using only generic facts, or (for any business) make something useful about the moment. Each has a different risk.

**1. Official partner.** You make the product; the organiser promotes it and takes a share.
- One US company makes a custom print per race, builds the shop, ships the orders and handles support; the race promotes the prints to finishers and earns a share of each sale (share not stated). It signed 20 races in about nine months and sells most prints at $60 [vendor; Trackstar's own post and site].
- A French race sells a course-map poster with name, time and bib number as an option when you register, €30 or €35, shipped 5 days after the race, non-refundable [first-party; Marseille-Cassis FAQ]. Selling at registration removes the timing problem: the buyer has already paid.
- A UK organiser has a partner print framed name-and-time pictures in the race village in about 10 minutes [first-party; Run For All].
- Cost: a sales conversation per race and a revenue share. Gain: the event's name, the finisher email list and no legal risk.

**2. Unofficial seller.** You sell without the organiser.
- Unofficial custom race prints and setlist prints exist, sold under the event's or artist's name with no licence stated (a London Marathon print from £13; a custom setlist print at €25) [vendor; listings read 2026-10-13]. Their existence shows they sell; it does not show they are safe (see the law section).
- Cost: no permission needed, but you cannot use the event's name or marks freely, and the organiser may sell an official version at the same time.

**3. Any business: something useful about the moment.** No physical product.
- A developer tool can publish an upgrade check the day a major version ships; a tax tool a checklist the week before a deadline; a monitoring tool a plain write-up of a public outage. The audience is the people the event touched, and the asset is about that event [practitioner; no study measured this].
- This is the "Make something about an event your audience just lived through" small bet; see **small-bets**. Run it on at least three events before judging, because most single tries do nothing.

## Timing

_In short:_ Build before the event, show it in the first days after. Interest fades fast, but nobody has published how fast for keepsakes.

- A race-photo platform says "participants' enthusiasm quickly wanes after the event" and that faster photo availability sells better [vendor; Sportpxl, no numbers].
- A claim that each day of delay loses 10–15% of email opens comes from another photo vendor [vendor; seen via search snippets only]. Treat it as a hint, not a rate.
- Public attention to topics now rises and falls faster than a decade ago: a Twitter hashtag stayed in the top 50 for 17.5 hours on average in 2013 and 11.9 hours in 2016 [research; observational, several datasets; public topics, not personal events].
- So [practitioner]:
  - **Before:** the template, the product listing, the ads and the landing page are ready and approved.
  - **Day 0–1:** results appear; ads go live; the page asks for the buyer's name and time.
  - **Days 1–7:** the main window. Watch cost per sale daily.
  - **After 14 days:** stop the ads. Keep the page up for late buyers and gifts.
- **No published sales-by-day curve exists** for event keepsakes. Measure your own on the first event.

## Reaching them with ads

_In short:_ On Meta you can set a city with a radius and add interests, but under Advantage+ audience the interests are only suggestions. The ad's wording does most of the filtering.

- **Location.** A city radius can be 17–80 km; a dropped pin can go down to 1 km. Only "people who live in or were recently in" the place can be chosen, not "people there right now" [first-party; Meta Marketing API docs].
- **Interests are suggestions.** Advantage+ audience is applied to new campaigns by default. It treats interests as a starting point and may show ads beyond them. The hard limits are location, minimum age, language and excluded custom audiences [first-party; Meta API docs; Help Center seen via search snippets only] [practitioner; Jon Loomer guide]. For link-click and landing-page-view campaigns, interest expansion cannot be turned off since 2024 [first-party; Meta developer blog].
- **Some interests disappear.** Meta removed sensitive-topic options in 2022 and targeting exclusions in 2025 [first-party; Meta announcements]. Ad sets using a further round of removed options stopped on 15 January 2026; which options went was not published in what we read [practitioner; Social Media Today]. Check that "marathon" or "running" still exists before you plan around it.
- **In the EU**, people who chose "less personalised ads" see ads based only on what they are viewing plus age, location, gender and ad engagement [first-party; Meta, 2024]. Interest targeting does not reach them.
- **What follows** [practitioner]: put the event, city and date in the image and the first line ("Montréal, 11 Oct 2026, 42.2 km: your finish, framed"). People who were not there scroll past, so loose targeting costs less than it seems.

**Ad rules that matter here**
- **Don't imply you know the viewer.** Meta bans ads that assert or imply personal attributes, including someone's **name**. Its own example allows "We print customizable t-shirts and stickers with your name" and bans "Billy Taylor, get this t-shirt with your name in print!" [first-party; Meta Advertising Standards]. "Add your name and time" is fine; "Sarah, your 3:41 deserves a frame" is not.
- **Don't suggest you are official.** Ads may not be "likely to confuse people about the source, sponsorship or affiliation" of a product, and are often removed when the rights holder reports them [first-party; Meta Advertising Standards].
- Run the numbers before spending: paid_media_math gives the cost per sale you can afford from your price and margin. **No source publishes the share of finishers who buy a keepsake**, so treat any conversion rate as an assumption and label it.

## Making it

_In short:_ Print-on-demand order APIs let each order carry its own artwork. Draw maps from OpenStreetMap with its credit. Let buyers type their own data.

- **Printing.** Printful and Prodigi both accept orders by API with a per-order image URL, so each print can be generated for its buyer [vendor; API docs read]. Printful lists framed posters from $19.95 (8×10") to $81.25 (24×36") before shipping, and shipping a medium frame costs more to Canada (C$49.39) than to the US (C$18.29) [vendor; Printful catalog and shipping page]. Gelato's pages were blocked, so its framed-print and API details are unchecked.
- **Maps.** You may sell printed maps made from OpenStreetMap data. Each print must credit OpenStreetMap ("Map data from OpenStreetMap" or "© OpenStreetMap contributors") and print the address openstreetmap.org/copyright [first-party; OSM Foundation guidelines]. Don't render prints from OpenStreetMap's public tile server: its policy bans bulk downloading and warns commercial users that access "may be withdrawn at any point" [first-party; OSM tile policy]. Render from the data yourself or use a paid map provider.
- **Results.** Let the buyer type their own name and time. Matching buyers to a scraped results list adds legal and privacy risk for little gain [practitioner]. In France, the data regulator says organisers may publish results only if runners were told at registration and could easily object [first-party; CNIL 2022]. No regulator has addressed third parties reusing results for marketing.
- **Event lists.** Event-data APIs restrict commercial use: PredictHQ's trial forbids it and requires deleting the data when a contract ends; Ticketmaster's developer terms forbid earning revenue from its API, with an exception not identified [first-party; terms read]. A hand-kept calendar of the events you serve is often enough at the start.

## The law: names, marks, maps, photos and people

_In short:_ Facts are free; an event's name, logo and look are not. Selling goods that call up a famous event without its permission is the main risk. Not legal advice.

- **Using a race's name on goods.** A US appeals court held that a seller who put "Boston Marathon" on shirts, which people bought because of that reference, was presumed to confuse buyers about who sponsored them, and had "an affirmative duty" to avoid that. The ban also covered a design with only "Boston", runners and the year, without the word "Marathon" [legal; BAA v. Sullivan, 1st Cir. 1989; US].
- **Calling up an event without its name can still infringe.** Shirts referring to a college bowl game in the teams' colours, with no names or logos, infringed because the seller meant to create the association [legal; LSU v. Smack Apparel, 5th Cir. 2008; US].
- **Generic words stay open.** The US trademark board let "Marathon Monday" be registered over the Boston race organiser's objection [legal; TTAB 2015, read via a law-firm summary]. Naming an event to describe something can be fair use, but only when nothing suggests sponsorship [legal; New Kids on the Block, 9th Cir. 1992; newspaper polls, not merchandise].
- **Olympic words are a special case.** In the US, "Olympic" is protected without any need to show confusion, and the usual trademark defences don't apply [legal; 36 U.S.C. § 220506; SFAA v. USOC 1987]. In Canada, using "Olympic", "Olympian" or "Olympics" in business is banned permanently; the wider ban on suggesting an association only applies during periods set by regulation [legal; Olympic and Paralympic Marks Act, current 2026].
- **FIFA World Cup.** FIFA says items with generic football terms, country names or flags "do not create an infringement", but its marks ("World Cup", "Mundial" and others) on merchandise are reserved to its licensees [first-party; FIFA WC26 IP Guidelines]. Mexico made ambush marketing an administrative offence in 2026 [legal; EU IP Helpdesk summary].
- **Major-event laws in the UK expired** after the London 2012 and Birmingham 2022 games [legal; statutes].
- **Canada in general.** The Trademarks Act bans directing attention to goods in a way likely to cause confusion with another business, and using a registered mark in a way that lowers its goodwill; accurate descriptions of your goods are allowed [legal; Trademarks Act ss. 7, 20, 22].
- **Facts are not owned.** Facts, scores and game results are not protected by copyright [legal; Feist 1991; NBA v. Motorola 1997], and song titles and band names are not either [first-party; US Copyright Office]. Applying this to finish times and setlists is our inference. Lyrics are protected.
- **Maps.** An organiser's designed course map can be protected as artwork; the streets and turns of the route are facts [first-party; US Copyright Office Compendium § 919]. Draw your own from open data.
- **Photos and people.** The photographer owns a race photo [legal; 17 U.S.C. § 201]. Using a real person's name or likeness on merchandise without consent is barred in California [legal; Civil Code § 3344] and can be an invasion of privacy in Quebec [legal; Civil Code art. 36]. A buyer putting their own name on their own print raises none of this; no source addressed it.

**A safer design rule for unofficial sellers** [practitioner; built from the cases above, not tested in court]:
- Use the city, date, distance, the route drawn from open data, and the buyer's own name and time.
- Leave out the event's name, logo, typeface, colours, mascot, sponsor names and official photos.
- Say "not affiliated with the organiser" on the page.
- Skip Olympic and FIFA events entirely unless you hold a licence.
- Or ask the organiser: the partner model removes all of this.

## Measuring it

_In short:_ Judge each event on its own: cost, sales and margin in its window, against a normal week.

- One tagged link per event and per ad (build_utm_link).
- Per event: ad spend, visits, orders, contribution margin (price minus printing, shipping, fees and refunds), and days from event to order.
- Judge after 7–14 days. Three events with no profit after ad spend means change the event type, the offer or the timing, not just the creative.
- For the no-product version (a release-day check, an outage write-up), compare sign-ups in the event window with a normal week.

## Common mistakes

- **Putting the event's name or logo on the product** because other sellers do. Their survival is not permission.
- **Ads that sound personal**, such as using a first name or "your 3:41". Meta's rules ban implying you know the viewer.
- **Designing after the event.** By the time the template is ready, the window is closing.
- **Small or casual events.** A few hundred 5 km runners rarely cover the ad cost.
- **Shipping frames across borders without checking cost.** Framed prints are heavy; shipping can exceed the print price.
- **Scraping results to target people by name.** It adds privacy risk and the buyer can type their own time.
- **Judging on one event.** Results vary a lot by event; run at least three.

## Folklore

_In short:_ Purchase rates, the "day-after" decay rate and the personalisation premium for keepsakes are repeated without evidence.

- **"About 10% of runners buy their photos."** From a 2010 forum post seen in search snippets [weak; not used].
- **"You lose 10–15% per day of delay."** From a photo vendor's blog, seen in snippets only [vendor].
- **"Personalisation doubles willingness to pay."** The research is on self-designed products, not data-filled templates [research applied beyond its test].
- **"Interest targeting finds exactly the people who were there."** Interests are suggestions under Advantage+ audience, and no interest means "ran this race" [first-party].
