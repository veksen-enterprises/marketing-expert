## When positioning is the problem

Fix positioning before copy, channels or pricing when you see:
- Prospects ask "so what is it?" or compare you to the wrong competitors.
- Sales cycles are long because every call starts with education.
- Win rates differ wildly by segment and nobody knows why.
- Messaging tests all come back flat: no wording wins because the underlying frame is wrong.
- The homepage could belong to three competitors if you swapped the logo.

Copy tweaks can't fix these. Changing the words around a confused frame produces a differently confused page.

## The components (April Dunford, Obviously Awesome, 2019) [practitioner]

Positioning "defines how your product is the best in the world at delivering some value that a well-defined set of customers cares a lot about."

1. **Competitive alternatives**: what customers would do if you didn't exist. Usually not a named competitor: a spreadsheet, an agency, an intern, doing nothing.
2. **Unique attributes**: capabilities you have that the alternatives lack. Literal and checkable.
3. **Value and proof**: what those attributes enable for the customer ("so what?"), with evidence.
4. **Target market characteristics**: what makes a buyer care a lot about that value, and how to recognise them.
5. **Market category**: the frame of reference that makes the value obvious and sets the buyer's assumptions (competitors, price, features).
6. (+1) **Relevant trend**: optional, layered on carefully; a trend nobody connects to the value is noise.

## Order matters

Dunford starts with competitive alternatives because differentiation only exists relative to something. Attributes are defined as "what we have that the alternatives don't", value as "so what for customers", target customers as "who cares most about that value", and category last, to frame the value. Starting from category ("we're a CRM") forces you into that category's feature checklist before you know whether it helps.

Her 10-step process:
1. List customers who love the product (fastest to close, longest retained, most referrals).
2. Form a cross-functional positioning team. Marketing alone gets it wrong.
3. Let go of the positioning baggage from how the product was first conceived.
4. List true competitive alternatives (from the customer's view).
5. Isolate unique attributes.
6. Map attributes to 2–4 value themes.
7. Determine who cares a lot.
8. Find a market frame that puts your strengths at the centre.
9. Layer on a trend, if one genuinely fits.
10. Capture the positioning in a document others can use.

## Choosing the category frame [practitioner]

- **Head-to-head**: win an existing category by beating the leader on its own terms. Buyers already know the category; you need to be, or credibly become, the leader.
- **Big fish, small pond**: dominate a subsegment whose needs the leader serves poorly ("CRM for property managers"). The usual right answer for startups: cheaper than fighting the leader, and word spreads inside a community.
- **Create a new game**: new category. You must prove the category should exist, define it, then claim leadership. Most expensive; reserve for when no existing frame makes your value legible.

Test a frame by asking: when a buyer hears this category, do the assumptions they bring (competitors, price range, must-have features) help or hurt you?

## What evidence exists

- No controlled studies validate Dunford's process; support is client case experience. [practitioner]
- Ries & Trout (Positioning, 1981): "positioning is what you do to the mind of the prospect." The buyer's frame of reference idea survives in the category step. "Own one word", ladders and first-mover claims are anecdote-based and survivorship-biased. [practitioner]
- Romaniuk, Sharp & Ehrenberg (2007): across many consumer categories, buyers perceived little differentiation between brands they bought; perceived differentiation was not necessary for a brand to be bought or to succeed. [research] This weakens "unique positioning" claims for mass consumer goods, where distinctiveness (being recognisable) does more work. It says less about considered B2B purchases, where buyers explicitly compare alternatives and Dunford's approach is built for.

Practical reconciliation: in considered purchases, make the differentiated value explicit and provable; in low-involvement categories, prioritise being easy to notice and recall (see brand-and-demand).

## Outputs a positioning exercise should produce

- One-paragraph positioning statement (internal, not copy): for [target], who [situation/need], [product] is a [category] that [key value]. Unlike [primary alternative], it [key differentiator + proof].
- 2–4 value themes, each with proof points (numbers, named customers, demos).
- The segment definition sales can qualify against.
- A homepage headline and subhead derived from it. Run analyze_copy on them.
- The list of assumptions that need customer validation (see customer-research).

## Common mistakes

- Positioning against competitors you track instead of the alternatives buyers actually use (often "do nothing").
- Listing features as differentiators when the alternative has them too.
- Choosing a broad target "to not leave money on the table": the message gets vaguer for everyone.
- Treating positioning as a tagline exercise. The output is a set of decisions; copy comes later.
- Re-positioning every quarter. Positioning should change when the market, product or best customers change, not when a campaign underperforms.
