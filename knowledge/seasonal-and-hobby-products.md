This playbook is for products used by hobbyists, where demand peaks once or twice a year. Examples: a free planner for a holiday craft project, a pattern or template library, a colour or supply database, a gallery of finished projects. For tools that live inside game or Discord communities, see community-and-hobby-products. For general community building and Reddit norms, see organic-social-and-community. For selling physical goods, see ecommerce-dtc.

## Two kinds of user: serious hobbyists and one-project makers

_In short:_ Some users treat the hobby as a long-term pursuit and return all year; others do one holiday project and leave. Plan retention, pricing and messages for each group separately.

Leisure research separates two kinds of user. They behave differently, so plan for each.
- **Serious leisure** means a hobby someone pursues for years, building skill and knowledge (a "leisure career"). Its marks include perseverance, effort to learn, a strong sense of identity, and a **social world**: a shared culture, events and places to meet (Stebbins 1982) [research; conceptual framework, not a measured effect].
- **Project-based leisure** is a short creative project done once or now and then, such as a holiday decoration (Stebbins 2005) [research; conceptual].
- What this means in practice [practitioner]:
  - **Serious hobbyists** come back between peaks, join groups, post work and pay for depth: more libraries, saved projects, advanced features.
  - **One-project makers** arrive just before the holiday, want a quick result, and leave. They will rarely pay monthly. A one-time purchase or a simple kit fits them better.
  - Some one-project makers become serious hobbyists. That move is your main growth path between peaks.
- **Why crafters join communities.** In a survey of 2,608 members of craft and maker sites (Ravelry, Etsy, Instructables and others), the top reasons were "inspiration and new ideas for future projects" (97% agreed), learning new things (97%), meeting like-minded people (79%), and getting feedback and teaching others (77%). Most said job hunting (68% disagreed) and online reputation (60% disagreed) did not motivate them (Kuznetsov & Paulos 2010) [research; survey, n=2,608; self-selected, mostly women from knitting and crochet sites].
- So lead with inspiration and learning, not points or status badges.

## Plan the year back from the peak

_In short:_ Work backwards from the holiday: content and pages ready months before, the product polished before planning starts, email and promotion during the rise, and quiet-season work after.

**Seasonality** means demand that rises and falls on the same calendar every year. For craft commerce the big peak is the end-of-year holidays. Etsy's annual report says its sellers sell more, and Etsy spends most on marketing, in the fourth quarter every year, and expects this to continue [first-party; Etsy 10-K for 2025].

People plan before they buy, and they buy before the holiday:
- About two in five US holiday shoppers start browsing and buying before November [vendor: NRF, retail trade association; survey by Prosper Insights; sample not given on the page].
- Pinterest advises having campaigns "ready to run a couple months ahead of calendar moments" [vendor: Pinterest sells ads; no data shown].
- A planning tool sits before the supply purchase, so its rise likely starts earlier than gift shopping. **No study measured this lead time for craft planning.** Check it with your own terms (next section).

A year plan for one peak [practitioner]:

| When (before the peak) | Do |
|---|---|
| 5–6 months | Look at last year's curve. Fix the product's main flow. Plan new templates, colours or features. |
| 3–4 months | Publish or refresh seasonal pages on their usual URLs. Pin new seasonal pins. Line up creators. |
| 6–10 weeks | Email the list, post in groups, start any paid tests. This is when the curve rises. |
| Peak weeks | Keep the site fast and support quick. Collect gallery posts. Don't launch big changes. |
| After the peak | Ask users to share finished work. Send a thank-you and a "what next" email. Survey users. |
| Quiet months | Build features, serve the serious hobbyists, try a second smaller season. |

- **Keep one URL per season.** Google's John Mueller advised reusing "one single URL" for recurring seasonal content, so the page keeps its links and history. Don't delete and republish it each year [first-party via press; 2018 hangout]. An **evergreen URL** is one without a year in it, such as /christmas-projects rather than /christmas-2026.
- **Paid search:** Google says its automated bidding already handles normal seasonal patterns. Its "seasonality adjustments" are for short events of 1–7 days with a big expected change in conversion rate (the share of visitors who buy or sign up) [first-party; Google Ads Help].
- Look for a **second season** (another holiday, a spring or summer project) to make the quiet months less quiet. Test it small first (see small-bets).

## Reading seasonal demand

_In short:_ Google Trends and Pinterest Trends show the shape of interest, not search counts. Compare the same terms year over year, and see when the rise begins. Never panic over off-season drops.

- **Google Trends** divides each point by all searches in that place and time, then scales it from 0 to 100. It uses a **sample** of searches, drops low-volume terms to 0, and adds small random noise. Google says it "is not a scientific poll" [first-party; Google Trends FAQ].
- **Pinterest Trends** shows up to two years by country. Each curve is scaled so its highest point is 100. Its "Moments" view shows how interest in a holiday "builds, peaks, and cools" [first-party; Pinterest Help].
- How to use them [practitioner]:
  - Look at the **shape**: the week the curve starts rising, the peak week, the drop. Ignore the absolute level.
  - Compare at least two years, because one year can be odd (2020 was).
  - Use broad terms. Small niche terms show as 0 or as noise.
  - Check both: Pinterest users plan with images, Google users often search for a specific answer.
- **Your own traffic drop is often just the season.** Google's guide to traffic drops lists seasonality as a cause. It suggests filtering Search Console to one query, checking it in Google Trends, and comparing the last 3 months year over year [first-party; Google Search Central, updated Dec 2025].
- Judge each season against the same weeks last year, not against the month before (see metrics-and-measurement).

## Between peaks: keep the audience

_In short:_ After the peak, most users leave until next year. Keep their email permission, give serious hobbyists reasons to return, and warm up the list before the next peak instead of mailing everyone at once.

- **Your email list is the asset that survives the off-season.** Pinterest, Facebook and search reach depend on other companies' rules (see platform-and-feature-risk). Ask for email at the moment of value: saving a project, receiving a shopping list, joining a challenge [practitioner].
- **Don't send nothing for ten months, then mail everyone.** Gmail and Yahoo watch spam complaints; a complaint rate of 0.3% or more causes problems, and people who forgot signing up complain more. Send a light monthly or seasonal note. Before the peak, mail your most engaged readers first, then widen (see email-and-lifecycle) [first-party rules; practitioner on timing].
- **Give serious hobbyists a reason to return.** Monthly challenges, a smaller off-season project, new colours or patterns, a "made with" gallery feature, polls on what to add next [practitioner]. Feedback on first posts helps: a study of about 140,000 new Facebook users found that newcomers who got comments on their early photos went on to share more (Burke, Marlow & Lento 2009) [research; log study, one platform; read via summary only].
- **A group or forum keeps people talking without you.** Pick the platform your users already use; for crafters that is often Facebook (next sections). Measure member-to-member replies, not member count (see organic-social-and-community).
- **Re-activate one-project makers next year.** Send a "your project from last year" email with their saved work, 6–10 weeks before the peak [practitioner].

## Pinterest

_In short:_ Pinterest is a planning search engine. Its recommendations lean on which boards a pin is saved to, so clear text, a claimed site and early saves matter. Publish seasonal pins well before the rise.

How it works, from Pinterest's own sources:
- **Related Pins** (the pins shown under a pin) drove over 40% of engagement in 2017. Its main source is **board co-occurrence**: pins that people saved to the same boards as the one being viewed. More shared boards meant more relevant results (Liu et al., Pinterest engineers, 2017) [research; one platform; 2017].
- A new pin saved to few boards has few connections (the "cold start" problem). Pinterest filled the gap with text search on the pin's description and link words [research; same paper]. So a new pin's text matters most until people save it.
- **Claim your website**: Pinterest then shows your profile picture on pins saved from your site, offers people the option to follow you, and gives you analytics [first-party].
- **Rich Pins** pull a product's or article's title, price and availability from your page's markup (Schema.org or Open Graph), updated within about 24 hours [first-party].
- **Idea Pins no longer exist** as a separate format. Since 2023 every pin can hold images, video or both, and an external link [first-party].
- Specs: 2:3 images (1000 × 1500 px); titles up to 100 characters, with the first 40 most visible; descriptions up to 500 characters [first-party; Pinterest ads guide].

What to do [practitioner]:
- Give each seasonal project a page on your site with a good image, then pin it with a plain, descriptive title ("Felt Christmas ornament plan with colour list"), not a slogan.
- Make **boards by project and season**. Boards are how Pinterest groups pins, and users save from them.
- Turn your gallery into pins: one pin per strong finished project, linking back to its page, with the maker's permission.
- Pin seasonal content before the rise you see in Pinterest Trends; consultants say 45–90 days, but no test supports a specific number.
- Video pins showing a process work for how-to content; keep the first seconds clear.

## Facebook groups

_In short:_ Crafters gather in Facebook groups. Join and help in existing ones before running your own; if you run one, you are responsible for what you approve, and the group is rented, not owned.

Rules from Meta:
- Meta removes groups that repeatedly break its Community Standards. Content an admin or moderator **approves** counts against the group [first-party; Meta Transparency Center, Nov 2024].
- A brand **Page** can join a group only if the group allows Pages, and can become an admin only by joining and then being invited by an admin [first-party; Facebook Help].
- Admins can set membership questions, require new members to accept the rules, and approve or decline requests. **Admin Assist** can approve or decline requests and posts automatically by criteria [first-party; Facebook Help; Meta 2022].

How brands use groups [practitioner]:
- **Join first.** Many craft groups ban promotion. Read the rules, ask the admins, help with questions, and share the tool only where it answers a question. Say that you made it.
- **Running your own group** works when there is a shared project: a seasonal challenge, a "show your finished piece" thread, weekly prompts. Post the rules, set two or three membership questions, and recruit moderators from active members.
- A membership question can ask for an email to send the newsletter, but make it optional and say clearly what they will receive. Consent and data rules still apply (see privacy-and-marketing-law).
- **The group is rented.** Reach and rules can change, and Meta can remove the group. Move your most engaged members onto your email list.

## Creators, Reddit, Etsy and craft fairs

_In short:_ Craft creators on YouTube and TikTok can show the tool in use; disclose any payment. Treat Reddit and Etsy by their own rules. Craft fairs are where makers who sell gather.

- **YouTube and TikTok creators.** Process videos are the natural fit: the creator plans a project with your tool, then makes it. Offer free premium access and credit first; pay only after you see fit. YouTube requires creators to tick the "paid promotion" box for branded content, which adds a label to the video [first-party]. US law requires clear disclosure of paid or gifted relationships (see pr-and-influencers).
- **Reddit craft communities.** Each subreddit sets its own rules on self-promotion. Follow them and say you made the tool (see organic-social-and-community).
- **Etsy as a discovery channel.** Etsy's search is where people look for patterns, templates and supplies. To sell there, everything must be made, designed, handpicked or sourced by you. Digital items must be designed by you, and AI-made items must say so [first-party; Etsy Help]. Each listing costs $0.20 and each sale 6.5% of the total, plus payment fees [first-party; Etsy Help, 2026]. A few paid templates on Etsy can test demand and send buyers to your free tool [practitioner].
- **Craft fairs and shops.** In a UK survey of 6,068 adults, people still preferred buying craft directly from the maker at fairs and markets, younger buyers included [research-style survey; Crafts Council 2020; buyers of finished craft]. If your users sell their work, fairs are where to meet them. Bring a printed handout with a QR code, offer a fair-season feature (price lists, booth planning), or partner with an organiser [practitioner]. **Local craft and supply shops** can display a flyer or host a workshop that uses your tool.

## Galleries as content and search pages

_In short:_ A gallery of users' finished projects can bring search and Pinterest traffic, but only with real text on each page, consent from makers, and moderation against spam.

- **UGC** (user-generated content: work posted by users rather than by you) gives a craft site fresh, real examples. Each gallery page can show a project, its colours or materials, and a link to "plan this yourself" [practitioner].
- **Make each page findable.** Google's image guide asks for descriptive alt text, meaningful file names, images placed near relevant text, good titles, and an image sitemap [first-party; Google Search Central, 2026]. A page that is only a photo and a username gives Google little to understand.
- **Group pages by what people search for**: "felt ornament ideas", "beginner snowman projects". Index good collection pages; consider `noindex` for thin single entries (see seo-content-and-architecture).
- **Moderate.** Google recommends manual approval for some user actions, `rel="ugc"` or `nofollow` on user links, `noindex` on new users' posts until they build a reputation, CAPTCHAs, and a clear way to report abuse. It says even basic steps put spammers off [first-party; Google Search Central, Dec 2025].
- **Respond to first posts.** A comment or a feature on a newcomer's first project is cheap and supports more posting (see Burke et al. above).
- **Ask before reusing.** Get the maker's consent before featuring their work in pins, emails or ads; credit them by name [practitioner].

## Making money from a free hobby tool

_In short:_ Charge for depth that serious hobbyists want, sell one-time kits or templates to one-project makers, add affiliate links with disclosure, and take supply-brand sponsorship only if users still trust your choices.

From least to most risk to user trust [practitioner unless tagged]:

1. **Freemium** (a free plan forever, with paid extras). Keep the main planning flow free. Charge for depth: more saved projects, full colour libraries, print-ready exports, advanced tools. Freemium brings many sign-ups and few payers (see pricing). For a one-peak product, monthly plans tend to be cancelled after the holiday, so test an **annual plan** or a **season pass** (one payment for the season) [rule-of-thumb; no public data for seasonal tools].
2. **Kits and templates.** Sell what one-project makers need right now: a printable template, a pattern pack, or a materials kit with a partner. A free knitting community's pattern store passed 100,000 patterns sold (over $500,000) by 2009, with designers keeping most of the money [first-party case; Ravelry, 2009]. Physical kits bring stock and shipping costs; work out the contribution margin (what each order leaves after the costs it causes) first (see ecommerce-dtc).
3. **Affiliate links** (you earn a commission when a user buys through your link). A shopping list linked to suppliers is a natural fit.
   - **Etsy**: Affiliates (sites, blogs) and Creator Collective (social creators with 500+ followers) run through Rakuten. Commission excludes tax and shipping. The cookie window (how long after a click a sale still counts) is 30 days for sites but 7 days for creators and for sales in the Etsy app. Payouts wait for Etsy's return window [first-party; Etsy Help, 2026]. Etsy does not publish the rate on that page.
   - **Etsy API**: you need approval for commercial access and must show Etsy's "not endorsed or certified by Etsy" notice [first-party; Etsy developer docs].
   - **Amazon Associates**: you must state "As an Amazon Associate I earn from qualifying purchases". No links in printed material. Links in email, SMS or direct messages only to people who opted in. A sale counts only if the order comes within 24 hours of the click (or 89 days for items added to the cart in that time) [first-party; Amazon Associates agreement and policies, 2025–2026]. New accounts are reported to close without three sales in 180 days [snippet-only]. A seasonal tool that signs up in the quiet months can miss that.
   - **Disclose next to the link.** The FTC says a disclosure on a separate page is not enough; plain words like "paid link" or "I earn a commission" work [first-party; FTC]. See partnerships-and-affiliates for programme design.
4. **Supply-brand sponsorship.** A paint, yarn or tool brand pays to be featured, run a challenge, or sponsor the newsletter. Label it as sponsored (see pr-and-influencers). Keep sponsored items out of neutral rankings and colour matches; users who think results are paid stop trusting them [practitioner].
5. **Using brand names.** A colour library naming paint brands is usually fine in the US under **nominative fair use** (using a trademark only to name the other company's product): name the product, use no more of the mark than needed, and imply no endorsement [legal; US case law summary, snippet-only; not legal advice; other countries differ]. Don't copy a brand's logo or suggest a partnership you don't have.

## Measuring a seasonal product

_In short:_ Compare each season with the same weeks last year, track cohorts by the season they joined, and count how many users come back next season, not monthly active users.

- **Year over year, same weeks.** Month-to-month changes mostly show the season.
- **Cohorts by season** (users grouped by the season they joined): what share come back the next season, and what share stay active between peaks. This separates serious hobbyists from one-project makers (see retention-and-expansion).
- **Lead time**: the week your traffic starts rising compared with last year. If it moves earlier, move your plan earlier.
- **Channel per season**: sign-ups from Pinterest, groups, creators, search and email. Ask "How did you hear about us?" at sign-up.
- **Money per season**: paid conversions, affiliate commissions and kit sales per active user during the peak, and costs in the quiet months.

## What usually works by stage

_In short:_ First, win one season with one project type and one channel. Then add the email list, gallery and a second season. Add paid plans, affiliates and sponsors once a returning audience exists.

| Stage | Usually works | Usually fails |
|---|---|---|
| First season | One project type for one holiday; post in existing groups with admin permission; a few pins per project; ask every user for email | Launching after the curve has started rising; many crafts at once; building your own group with no members |
| Second season | Evergreen seasonal pages; gallery of last year's work; email warm-up before the peak; a few creators; one second season tested small | Mailing a cold list all at once; judging by month-over-month numbers |
| Returning audience | Freemium extras for serious hobbyists; season pass or annual plan; templates or kits; affiliate shopping lists with disclosure | Paywalling the basic planner; sponsored results presented as neutral |
| Growing | Own group or forum with member moderators; supply-brand sponsorships; fair and shop partnerships; more seasons | Relying on one platform's reach for all traffic |

## Common mistakes

_In short:_ Starting work when the season has already begun, deleting seasonal pages yearly, panicking over off-season drops, mailing a cold list, monthly-only pricing, undisclosed affiliate links and unmoderated galleries.

- Starting seasonal content or features when searches are already rising.
- New URLs each year for the same seasonal page.
- Reading an off-season traffic drop as a site problem without checking last year.
- Sending nothing for ten months, then emailing the whole list at the peak.
- Monthly plans only, for users who need the tool a few weeks a year.
- Affiliate links in unsolicited email, in print, or with the disclosure on a separate page.
- Gallery pages with no text, no consent from the maker, and no spam control.
- Running a Facebook group as an ad channel, or as the only way to reach users.
- Treating all users as serious hobbyists, or all as one-time makers.

## Folklore

_In short:_ Exact Pinterest lead times, a "fresh pin" boost, keyword-placement rules, Pinterest's trend accuracy claims and craft-fair conversion rates are repeated widely but rest on vendor claims or no data.

- **"Pin 45 or 90 days before the holiday."** Pinterest's own advice is "a couple months ahead" for campaigns; no published test compares lead times [rule-of-thumb].
- **"Christmas planning on Pinterest starts in April."** From one 2020 Pinterest post in a pandemic year, with no method [vendor].
- **"Pinterest favours fresh pins."** A 2017 Pinterest paper discusses helping new content get shown; no current Pinterest page states a ranking boost [practitioner].
- **Keyword placement rules** such as "main keyword in the first 35 characters". Pinterest only says the first 40 title characters are most visible [practitioner].
- **"Pinterest Predicts is 80% accurate."** Graded by Pinterest using its own definition [vendor].
- **"10% of craft fair visitors buy."** From vendor-directory blogs with no method [weak; not used].
