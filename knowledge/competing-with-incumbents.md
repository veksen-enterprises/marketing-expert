## The starting fact: big is not slow

- In Chandy & Tellis (2000), incumbents introduced 47% of radical innovations overall (consumer durables and office products, n = 64, since 1851). Before WWII non-incumbents introduced 73%; after WWII incumbents introduced **74%**, and large firms 74%. Their conclusion: the "incumbent's curse may apply, but to an earlier economic period." [research, read in full]
- What predicts radical innovation is **willingness to cannibalize** existing revenue, more than firm size (Chandy & Tellis 1998). [research]
- Incumbents may deliberately wait for an outsider to prove a market, then enter as fast followers with their marketing and distribution. [hypothesis noted in Chandy & Tellis 2000; untested]

So the question is never "are they big?" It's "**what stops them from copying us, in their own P&L terms?**"

## Disruption theory: useful mechanism, weak base rate

Christensen's mechanism [practitioner/theory]:
- **Low-end foothold**: incumbents overserve the low end while chasing their best, highest-margin customers. An entrant offers "good enough" cheaper.
- **New-market foothold**: the entrant serves people who weren't buying at all (non-consumption).
- **Asymmetric motivation**: the incumbent is rationally pulled upmarket and doesn't defend low-margin business. This is a resource-allocation effect, not incompetence.
- **Sustaining innovations** (better products for the incumbent's best customers): incumbents usually win. Christensen et al. (HBR 2015) classed Uber as sustaining, not disruptive.

The evidence is thinner than the fame:
- King & Baatartogtokh (2015) asked industry experts about the 77 cases Christensen used as examples. Only **9%** showed all four conditions the theory requires. Incumbent overshooting of customer needs showed up in only **17 of 77**; incumbents floundered in 48 of 77. [research, snippet-only]
- Gans (2016) separates **demand-side** disruption (incumbent unwilling: listens to its best customers) from **supply-side** disruption (incumbent unable: organised around an old product architecture). Ask which one you're betting on.

**Use**: don't call your product "disruptive." Test the claim: interview the incumbent's low-end customers and see whether they're actually overserved.

## Counter-positioning: the main structural advantage a newcomer can have

Hamilton Helmer (7 Powers, 2016) [practitioner; from secondary summaries]:
- A durable advantage needs a **benefit** (better cash flow) and a **barrier** (something that stops rivals copying even when they understand it).
- **Counter-positioning**: "a newcomer adopts a new, superior business model which the incumbent does not mimic due to anticipated damage to their existing business." The barrier is **collateral damage**: copying you would hurt their existing revenue.
- Examples: Vanguard's low-cost index funds vs active managers whose fees they would cannibalise; Netflix's no-late-fees DVD-by-mail vs Blockbuster, whose late fees were a material revenue line. Blockbuster did respond (Total Access), then scaled back under board pressure: "dabbling, then capitulation."
- Typical incumbent sequence: denial, ridicule, fear, anger, capitulation, often too late.
- **Limit**: it protects you only from *that* incumbent, not from other startups copying your model. You need a second advantage before they arrive.

**When each kind of advantage is available** (Helmer):
| Phase | Available |
|---|---|
| Origination (before takeoff) | Counter-positioning, cornered resource (exclusive access to something valuable: talent, IP, data, licence, relationship) |
| Takeoff | Scale economies, network effects, switching costs |
| Stability | Brand, process power |

Implication: at founding, claiming network effects or scale is a claim about the future. What you can actually have now is a model they won't copy or an asset they can't get.

## Fight on a different axis (the asymmetric-conflict data)

Arreguín-Toft (2001), asymmetric wars 1800–1998 [research, snippet-only]:
- Strong actors won **76%** when both sides used the same approach.
- Weak actors won **63%** when they used the opposite approach.
- Strong-actor win rate fell from 88% (1800–1849) to 49% (1950–1998).
Gladwell popularised it (David and Goliath, 2013). Caveat: "winning" in insurgencies often means the strong side withdrawing for political reasons; alternative explanations exist (mechanisation of armies). Use it as an analogy only:

**Don't compete on the dimension where their money converts directly into results**: ad spend, feature parity, sales headcount, price wars in concentrated markets.

## How incumbents actually respond

- **Before you launch, and on your segment only.** When Southwest became likely to enter an airline route, incumbents cut fares on that route before Southwest flew; over half of Southwest's fare impact came before entry, mostly on routes that were concentrated beforehand (Goolsbee & Syverson 2008). [research]
- **Bundling.** Microsoft Teams, bundled into Office 365, went from launch (2017) to 13M daily users in July 2019, passing Slack, then 145M daily users by April 2021 (Microsoft). Slack's EU complaint (2020) led to binding remedies only in September 2025, by which time Slack had been sold to Salesforce. When an incumbent bundles your category into something the buyer already pays for, your rival's effective price is zero. [first-party]
- **Copying a format onto an existing network.** Instagram Stories (Aug 2016) followed by sharply slowing Snapchat user growth (Snap S-1; correlation, not a causal estimate). [first-party / press]
- **Price response**: Rao, Bergen & Davis (HBR 2000): "the best counterattack does not involve a retaliatory price." If a big player cuts price, respond first with non-price moves, then selective offers to specific segments, and cede segments where fighting costs more than it returns. [practitioner]

## Tactics with some support

- **Contained fire / beachhead**: start with a narrow market you can dominate quickly (Facebook: Harvard only). Its users should be people "doing something lots more people will be doing in the future" (Paul Graham). [practitioner]
- **Do things that don't scale**: recruit users by hand, give service no big company can match (Stripe installing for users on the spot; Airbnb visiting hosts). [practitioner/anecdote]
- **Go where their incentives block them**: business-model conflict, channel conflict, margin structure, cannibalisation. Graham: big companies "have some kind of turf to protect" and "only build one of each thing." [practitioner]
- **Unbundle a component** where your version is far better for a definable segment, then rebundle adjacent jobs once you own the customer. (The "bundling and unbundling" line is attributed to Netscape's Jim Barksdale; no primary source found.)
- **Competition as signal**: Graham: "a crowded market is actually a good sign": demand exists and current solutions fall short. Thiel ("competition is for losers") argues for dominating a small market. They mostly agree: dominate a niche first. The real danger is a competitor whose customers can't leave (switching costs, network effects).

## Decision rules

1. Write the incumbent's reason not to follow you in P&L terms (which revenue or margin it would destroy). If you can't, assume they will copy you.
2. If your product is "better for the incumbent's best customers," you're in a sustaining fight, which they usually win. Only enter that way with an exclusive asset or a business model they won't copy.
3. Test the overshoot assumption with real low-end customers before betting on it.
4. Pick a different axis than theirs. Never feature parity or a price war against deeper pockets.
5. In concentrated markets, expect a targeted price response as soon as your entry becomes credible. Budget for it, or enter where you're not a direct substitute.
6. Plan the second advantage (switching costs, network effects in your niche, brand) you'll build during takeoff, before copycats arrive.
7. Target incumbents whose incentives block them, not just big ones.
8. If your category can be bundled into a suite your buyers already own, serve a segment the suite serves badly, or own a channel the incumbent can't bundle (community, vertical, partners). Don't count on regulators: relief takes years.
