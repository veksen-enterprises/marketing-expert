## Quebec

_In short:_ Quebec customers get French first: website, app, terms, receipts, ads. Prices include every fee except sales tax. Tracking that profiles people stays off until they turn it on. No ads aimed at under-13s.

> Not legal advice; check with a local lawyer. Sources and access levels: research/marketing-law-canada-us.md (Quebec, items 1–21). Federal Canadian law (CASL, Competition Act) also applies in Quebec; see the next section.

**Language (Charter of the French language, as amended by Bill 96)**
- Websites, catalogues, order forms and other commercial publications must be in French. Other languages are allowed if the French version is "available on terms that are at least as favourable". [first-party; Charter s.52]
- Software, including games and apps, "must be available in French unless no French version exists". [first-party; Charter s.52.1]
- Terms of service are a contract of adhesion (a standard contract the customer cannot negotiate). Since 1 June 2023 the customer must be given the French version first; only then can they choose to be bound by English. Online, a French version of the site with the French terms on it meets this. Customers living outside Quebec are exempt. [first-party; Charter s.55; Regulation s.27.3; OQLF guidance]
- Ads, signs, invoices and receipts are in French. When bilingual, French must be "markedly predominant", which on signs means at least twice the space. [first-party; Charter ss.57–58; Regulation ss.27.4–27.6]
- Trademarks, since 1 June 2025: a registered trademark with no registered French version may stay in English, but generic or descriptive words in it (flavour, colour, "app", "tool") must also appear in French on the product. Outdoor signs with an English brand need added French words. Products made before 1 June 2025 can be sold until 1 June 2027. [first-party; Charter ss.51.1, 58.1; Regulation ss.27.7 and transitional s.7]
- Businesses with 25–49 employees must register with the OQLF (Office québécois de la langue française, the language regulator) since 1 June 2025; language-of-work rules apply at any size. [first-party; OQLF]
- Enforcement is complaint-driven. The OQLF works with the business first, then gives 15 days' notice before a formal order; fines ($3,000–$30,000 for a company, doubled for a second offence) apply to breaking the order. Website cases have ended in $1,500 fines (Quality Belt maintenance, 2022) and $3,000 for a repeat offence (Fournitures Hermes, 2024). [first-party; Charter ss.177, 205, 207; OQLF communiqués]

**Privacy (Law 25, Quebec's private-sector privacy act)**
- Every business has a person in charge of personal information, by default the most senior person. Publish their title and contact on the website, along with a plain-language privacy policy and governance rules. [first-party; P-39.1 ss.3.1, 3.2, 8.2]
- Do a privacy impact assessment (PIA: a written review of privacy risks, scaled to how sensitive the data is) before building or replacing a system that handles personal information, and before sending personal information outside Quebec, including to a US cloud host. Sign a written agreement with the recipient. [first-party; P-39.1 ss.3.3, 17]
- Tracking that can identify, locate or profile a person must be off by default and switched on by the user. The CAI's own example: a site whose cookies drive personalised recommendations explains them and tells readers how to turn the cookies on. Browser cookies are excluded from the general privacy-by-default rule. [first-party; P-39.1 ss.8.1, 9.1; CAI Guidelines 2023-1] Reading of this: strictly necessary cookies need no banner. [judgment]
- Consent must be clear, free, informed and asked separately for each purpose. Health data is sensitive and needs express consent. Using data collected for another purpose to market to people ("commercial prospection") is never a "consistent purpose", so it needs consent. Under-14s need a parent's consent. [first-party; P-39.1 ss.4.1, 12, 14]
- Report incidents with a risk of serious injury to the CAI (Commission d'accès à l'information, the privacy regulator) and to the people affected; keep a register of all incidents. Data portability on request since 22 September 2024. [first-party; P-39.1 ss.3.5, 3.8, 27; CAI]
- Penalties reach $10 million or 2% of worldwide turnover (administrative) and $25 million or 4% (penal), plus punitive damages of at least $1,000 for intentional breaches. No published CAI penalty against a business was found; the CAI joined the 2025 TikTok investigation on children's data. [first-party; P-39.1 ss.90.12, 91, 93.1; OPC 2025]

**Advertising, prices and contests (Consumer Protection Act)**
- The advertised price must include "the total amount the consumer must pay", except GST and QST. A service fee added at checkout breaks this. [first-party; CPA s.224]
- Commercial advertising "directed at persons under thirteen years of age" is banned. The test looks at whether the product appeals to children and how, where and when the ad appears; mixed adult-and-child audiences are not a safe harbour. Fines: $5,000–$125,000 for a company. [first-party; CPA ss.248–249, 278; OPC guide]
- No using a person's identity or image, including altered images and AI recordings, in advertising without their consent (2026). [first-party; CPA s.238.1]
- No false regular prices or "was" prices; no omitting important facts. [first-party; CPA ss.225, 228]
- Suggested tips are calculated on the pre-tax price, with a custom-amount option, all shown equally (since May 2025). [first-party; CPA s.225.1; OPC]
- Contests no longer need registration or duties with the RACJ (since 27 October 2023), but all conditions must be clearly disclosed. [first-party; S.Q. 2023 c.24; CPA s.233]

**Online sales and subscriptions**
- Before an online sale, show your identity and address, a full description, an itemised price with all fees, the total, and the cancellation and refund policies. If you don't, the customer can cancel within 7 days of getting the contract copy; refunds within 15 days. [first-party; CPA ss.54.4, 54.8, 54.13]
- Since 12 September 2026, subscriptions sold online must be cancellable through an easy-to-find online button. Send a written reminder 2–10 days before a discounted period ends, with the end date and the new price. Show one-time fees next to the monthly price. Contracts cannot forbid customers from posting reviews. [first-party; Bill 10 (2026, c.20); OPC]
- A free trial cannot require the customer to send a notice to avoid paying full price afterwards. [first-party; CPA s.230(c)]
- Changing terms needs 30 days' notice and a free right to cancel; the price of a fixed-term contract cannot be changed unilaterally. [first-party; CPA s.11.2]

**Delivery and ordering fees**
- Quebec has no delivery-fee cap now. Bill 87 (March 2021) capped fees at 15% for delivery plus 5–10% for online ordering, only for platforms serving 500+ restaurants and only while dining rooms were closed; it ended with the health emergency in June 2022. [first-party; S.Q. 2021 c.4; end date secondary]

**First steps for a small product**
1. Ship French first: site, app UI, checkout, terms, receipts, emails. Make the French version at least as easy to reach as the English one.
2. Name the privacy officer on the site and publish a short, plain privacy policy in French.
3. Turn off ad pixels, profiling and location features by default for Quebec users; switch them on only after the user opts in.
4. Write a one-page PIA for your stack and for each processor outside Quebec, and sign data agreements with them.
5. Show all-in prices, add an online cancel button, and send discount-ending reminders.
6. Fitness or health data: ask for express consent, separately. Games or apps that appeal to children: no ads aimed at under-13s.

## Canada (federal) and Ontario

_In short:_ Marketing email and texts need consent in Canada, B2B included. Advertised prices must be attainable, with no fees added later. Privacy outside Quebec is consent-based but lighter. Ontario's new subscription rules aren't in force yet.

> Not legal advice; check with a local lawyer. Sources and access levels: research/marketing-law-canada-us.md (items 22–45).

**Messages (CASL, Canada's Anti-Spam Legislation)**
- A commercial electronic message (CEM: any email, text or DM with a commercial purpose, even a side purpose) needs consent, B2B included. Express consent states the purpose and who is asking. [first-party; CASL ss.6, 10(1)]
- Implied consent applies after a purchase (two years) or an inquiry (six months). A published work address counts only if it carries no "no spam" note and your message is relevant to the person's job. [first-party; CASL s.10(9)–(10)]
- Every CEM identifies the sender, gives contact details and has a free unsubscribe that works within 10 business days. [first-party; CASL ss.6(2), 11]
- Penalties go up to $10 million per violation for a business. There is no private lawsuit: that section was repealed before coming into force. The CRTC acts on complaints (414,630 in 2024–25). OneClass, an online study platform, paid $100,000 (2020) for CEMs without consent and for installing a browser extension without consent. Gap paid $200,000 (2021) for missing or broken unsubscribe links. [first-party; CASL s.20(4); ISED report; CRTC]
- Transactional and B2B details are in **outbound-and-abm** and **email-and-lifecycle**.

**Privacy (PIPEDA, federal law outside Quebec, BC and Alberta)**
- Consent must be meaningful: the key points are visible up front, not buried in a policy. Uses the service doesn't need must be optional. [first-party; PIPEDA s.6.1; OPC guidelines 2018]
- Health and other sensitive data need express consent. Behavioural ads can rely on an easy opt-out only for non-sensitive data, and child-directed sites should allow no tracking at all. [first-party; PIPEDA Sch.1 4.3.6; OPC OBA position]
- Parents consent for children under 13, in all but exceptional cases. The 2025 joint TikTok investigation (federal, Quebec, BC, Alberta) found age checks "largely ineffective"; TikTok agreed to stop most ad targeting of under-18s. [first-party; OPC]
- Sending data abroad is allowed, but you stay responsible and need contracts giving "comparable" protection. [first-party; PIPEDA Sch.1 4.1.3]
- Report breaches with "a real risk of significant harm" to the Privacy Commissioner and the people affected, and log every breach. PIPEDA has no administrative fines; knowingly failing to report can mean up to $100,000. [first-party; PIPEDA ss.10.1, 10.3, 28]
- Bill C-36 would replace PIPEDA with a new regulator and penalties. It was introduced on 15 June 2026 and is at second reading, so it is not law. [first-party; LEGISinfo]

**Advertising and prices (Competition Act)**
- Drip pricing is illegal: a price "not attainable due to fixed obligatory charges or fees" is misleading, unless the extra is only a government tax. In force since 23 June 2022. [first-party; s.74.01(1.1); date secondary]
- Cineplex was fined over $38.9 million (September 2024) over a $1.50 online booking fee. The Competition Bureau sued DoorDash in June 2025 over service, delivery, small-order and "regulatory response" fees added at checkout; DoorDash disputes it and the case is pending. [first-party; Competition Bureau; status snippet-only]
- Company penalties reach $10 million ($15 million for repeats), or three times the benefit, or 3% of worldwide revenue. Since 20 June 2025, competitors and others can ask the Competition Tribunal for permission to bring these cases themselves. [first-party; s.74.1; date secondary]
- Performance claims ("burns 30% more calories") need "adequate and proper" tests done before the claim. Testimonials need the giver's written permission. "Regular price" claims need real sales or good-faith offers at that price. [first-party; ss.74.01(1)(b), 74.01(2)–(3), 74.02]
- Influencers and employees must disclose any material connection (payment, free product, discount) in every post, even when the review is honest. [first-party; Bureau Digest Vol. 4 (2018); Bureau notice January 2024]
- Contests: disclose the number and approximate value of prizes and anything that affects the odds, and award prizes promptly. [first-party; s.74.06]

**Ontario**
- The Consumer Protection Act, 2002 is still the law: refunds within 15 days after a valid cancellation, refunds for delivery more than 30 days late, no charging for goods nobody ordered. [first-party; Ontario.ca]
- The Consumer Protection Act, 2023 (assented December 2023) adds active consent to renewals, a continuing right to cancel auto-renewing contracts, and a ban on barriers to cancelling. It is not in force yet: it waits for a proclamation date and regulations. [secondary; snippet-only]
- Ontario's delivery cap (15% delivery, 20% total, platforms with 500+ restaurants) applied only where indoor dining was banned during COVID. It no longer has practical effect; its formal repeal date was not confirmed. [secondary; snippet-only]

**British Columbia, in brief**
- Since 1 January 2023, BC permanently caps what a big delivery platform (500+ restaurants) charges for listing, ordering, payment and delivery together: a 20% base tier must be offered, excluding taxes and tips. Optional extras are allowed, and driver pay cannot be cut to fund the cap. A pickup-only app does not provide "delivery services", so it is outside the Act. [first-party; Food Delivery Service Fee Act; B.C. Reg. 249/2022]

**First steps for a small product**
1. Add an unticked consent box with a clear purpose at sign-up; log when and how each person consented, and track two-year (purchase) and six-month (inquiry) clocks.
2. Put your name, contact address and a one-click unsubscribe in every marketing email and text.
3. Show prices that include every mandatory fee; let only government taxes be added at checkout.
4. Put the key privacy points on the sign-up screen; keep health data opt-in and out of ad tools; no trackers on anything aimed at children.
5. Ask employees and creators to disclose their connection in every post; get written permission before quoting a testimonial.
6. Build cancellation to Quebec's and California's standard now; Ontario's rules will likely be covered when they arrive.

## United States and California

_In short:_ California now requires all-in prices, online cancellation for online sign-ups, and renewal reminders. CCPA reaches small sites through ad-pixel "sharing". Health apps may fall under medical privacy law. San Francisco caps delivery commissions.

> Not legal advice; check with a local lawyer. Sources and access levels: research/marketing-law-canada-us.md (items 46–57). Federal rules already covered elsewhere: CAN-SPAM (opt-out email) in **outbound-and-abm**; the FTC fake-reviews rule and Endorsement Guides in **pr-and-influencers**; COPPA, GPC and click-to-cancel status in **privacy-and-marketing-law** and **retention-and-expansion**.

**Prices (California)**
- Since 1 July 2024 it is unlawful to advertise or display a price "that does not include all mandatory fees or charges", except government taxes and real shipping costs. [first-party; Civ. Code §1770(a)(29)]
- Restaurants and cafés may list a separate mandatory charge only if it is shown clearly, with its purpose, wherever the price appears. The exemption "does not apply to a 'third-party food delivery platform' … or any other food delivery platform". [first-party; Civ. Code §1770(a)(29)(D)]
- Whether a pickup-only ordering app counts as "any other food delivery platform" is not settled; all-in prices avoid the question. [judgment]

**Subscriptions (California Automatic Renewal Law, as amended from 1 July 2025)**
- Show renewal terms clearly next to the consent request, get express affirmative consent, and keep proof for three years (or one year after the contract ends, if longer). [first-party; BPC §17602(a)]
- Send a notice 3–21 days before a free trial or promo price longer than 31 days ends; 15–45 days before an annual (or longer) plan renews; 7–30 days before a price change; and an annual reminder for annual plans. [first-party; BPC §17602(b), (g), (h)]
- If people can sign up online, they must be able to cancel online, "at will", through a prominent link or button (or a ready-made cancellation email). One save offer is allowed only with a "click to cancel" button shown next to it. [first-party; BPC §17602(d)–(e)]
- Federal enforcement continues without a click-to-cancel rule: Amazon paid a $1 billion civil penalty plus $1.5 billion in refunds (September 2025) over Prime sign-ups and cancellation. The FTC sued Uber over Uber One (April 2025). [first-party; FTC]

**Privacy (California)**
- The CCPA applies to for-profit businesses above $26,625,000 in revenue (2025 figure), or that buy, sell or "share" personal information of 100,000+ California consumers or households a year, or that make half their revenue from selling or sharing it. Ad pixels and retargeting count as sharing, so a popular free site can cross the line on traffic. [first-party; Civ. Code §1798.140(d); CPPA]
- New CCPA regulations on risk assessments, automated decision-making and cybersecurity audits took effect 1 January 2026, with filings phased from 2028. [first-party; CPPA]
- From 1 January 2027, browsers must offer a built-in opt-out signal, so opt-out signals will become common. [secondary; snippet-only]
- A health or fitness app designed to store medical information so users can manage it, or to manage a medical condition, is treated as a health-care provider under California's medical privacy law (CMIA). Keep health data out of ad tools and get consent before sharing it. Whether step counts and heart rate count as "medical information" is unsettled. [first-party; Civ. Code §56.06(b); judgment]

**Delivery fees (San Francisco)**
- A delivery platform may not charge a restaurant more than 15% per online order, unless it offers every restaurant a "core delivery service" tier (listing plus delivery) at 15% or less with no add-ons required. Contracts must spell out each fee. [first-party; SF Ordinance 164-22, Police Code §5302]
- Platforms need a written agreement before serving a restaurant and must stop within 72 hours if the restaurant asks. Whether pickup-only orders are covered was not checked. [first-party; snippet-only]

**First steps for a small product**
1. Show all-in prices in California; for a café, display any service charge with its purpose on the menu, and don't let your platform add fees on top.
2. Add an online cancel button, renewal and trial-end reminders on the statutory schedule, and a consent log kept three years.
3. Count California visitors whose data your pixels share. Near 100,000 a year, honour GPC, add a "Do Not Sell or Share" link, and suppress pixels for opt-outs.
4. Fitness app: keep health data separate, never send it to ad pixels, and get explicit consent before sharing it.
5. Order-ahead app in San Francisco: sign each café before listing it, and keep any per-order fee at 15% or below unless you offer a 15% basic tier.

## Turkey

_In short:_ Turkey needs opt-in for marketing messages, and consent counts only once it is in İYS, the national consent register. Analytics cookies need explicit consent. Prices are all-in lira. Cancelling must be easy.

> Not legal advice; check with a local lawyer. Sources and access levels: research/marketing-law-turkey-armenia-argentina.md.

**Messages (email, SMS, calls)**
- Commercial electronic messages (email, SMS, calls, autodialers) need the recipient's prior consent. The exception is messages about something they bought, sent after they gave their contact details. [first-party; Law 6563 Art. 1(c), 6(1)]
- İYS (İleti Yönetim Sistemi, the state register of message consents): every business that sends commercial messages registers in it. A consent collected on your own site must be uploaded to İYS within three business days, or it is invalid. [first-party; Regulation on Commercial Electronic Messages Art. 7(11)–(12); registration duty for all senders from secondary sources]
- B2B: merchants and tradespeople ("esnaf ve tacir") can be messaged without prior consent. If they refuse, you must stop, and you check their refusal status in İYS before each send. [first-party; Law 6563 Art. 6(2); Regulation Art. 6]
- Every message must offer a free, easy way to refuse. You must stop within three business days. [first-party; Law 6563 Art. 8]
- Fines for 2026: TRY 2,859–14,309 per breach of the consent rule, and up to ten times that when one send goes to many people. [first-party; Law 6563 Art. 12(1)(a), 12(2); 2026 table]

**Privacy (KVKK, Law 6698)**
- The default legal basis is explicit consent: freely given, specific and informed. Other grounds are listed in Art. 5(2): contract, legal duty, legitimate interest. [first-party; KVKK Art. 3, 5]
- Cookies: analytics, functional and ad cookies need explicit consent. Legitimate interest does not cover them. In Decision 2022/229 (10 March 2022) the Board fined an e-commerce company TRY 800,000 over this. [first-party; KVKK Board decision summary]
- The KVKK cookie guide (June 2022) says consent categories start switched off, and pre-ticked boxes are unlawful. [first-party; snippet-only]
- VERBIS (the data-controller register): Turkish companies are exempt below 50 employees and TRY 100 million balance sheet. If processing health or other special-category data is the main business, the limits are 10 employees and TRY 10 million. [first-party; Board Decision 2025/1572; snippet-only]
- A controller based outside Turkey registers in VERBIS through a Turkish representative, whatever its size. [vendor; primary regulation not read]
- Transfers abroad (for example, to a US or EU cloud) need an adequacy decision or the Board's standard contract. The signed contract must be notified to the authority within five business days. [first-party; KVKK Art. 9 as amended 2024]
- Breaches: notify the Board "within the shortest time". The Board reads this as 72 hours. Yemek Sepeti (food delivery, 21.5 million users) was fined TRY 1.9 million in December 2021 for weak security. [first-party; Art. 12; Decision 2019/10 snippet-only; Decision 2021/1324 summary]
- 2026 KVKK fines reach about TRY 17 million for security and VERBIS failures. [secondary; snippet-only]

**Advertising and prices** (Regulation on Commercial Advertising; changes in force 1 August 2026)
- The advertised price is the total price including all taxes, in "TL" or "₺", with delivery costs or how they are calculated. [first-party; Art. 13]
- A "was" price for goods must be the lowest price in the 10 days before the discount (it was 30 days until 31 July 2026). Each sales channel uses its own reference price. Show the start and end dates of the discount. [first-party; Art. 14]
- Influencers: say "Reklam" or "Tanıtım" plus the brand name. The label must be visible without scrolling, come before other hashtags, and appear in every post of a series. It applies to payment, free products, giveaways and paid events. [first-party; Art. 23/A]
- Reviews: only verified buyers can review. Keep reviews up for at least a year without filtering out bad ones. Never buy reviews. [first-party; Art. 28/B]
- Targeted ads: tell users which criteria picked the ad and how to change them. No profiling-based ads to people known to be children. [first-party; Art. 25/A]
- Comparative ads are allowed, but the claims need proof from a university or an accredited lab. [first-party; Art. 8]
- The Advertising Board examined 12,499 files in the first half of 2025, found 753 violations and fined TRY 141.1 million. [first-party; Ministry of Trade, 14 July 2025]

**Consumer contracts and subscriptions** (Law 6502)
- Withdrawal (the right to cancel and get a refund) is 14 days for distance sales. If you don't inform the buyer, the period runs up to a year longer. [first-party; Art. 48(4)]
- No withdrawal right for instantly delivered digital content, or for a service started with the buyer's approval before the 14 days end. [first-party; Distance Contracts Regulation Art. 15(1)(ğ), (h)]
- Subscriptions: a fixed-term plan can't auto-renew for another full term unless the consumer asks or approves. Cancelling can't be harder than signing up. After the cancellation deadline you can't charge, and you refund the unused balance within 15 days. [first-party; Art. 52(3)–(5)]
- Language: contracts and information in "understandable language", at least 12-point. Turkish companies keep contracts and correspondence in Turkish. [first-party; Law 6502 Art. 4; Law 805 Art. 1–2, snippet-only]
- E-commerce sellers register in ETBİS, the e-commerce information system, before selling. [secondary; snippet-only]

**First steps for a small product**
1. Before your first Turkish newsletter or SMS, register in İYS and upload each consent within three business days.
2. Block analytics and ad cookies until the user gives explicit consent. Keep consent toggles off by default.
3. Sign the KVKK standard contract with your cloud and email providers, then notify it within five business days.
4. Show prices in TL with tax included. Base any "was" price on the lowest price of the last 10 days.
5. Make cancelling a subscription one step, the same as signing up.
6. Fitness or health apps: check VERBIS. If you are abroad, appoint a Turkish representative.

See: privacy-and-marketing-law, email-and-lifecycle, pr-and-influencers, pricing, retention-and-expansion, fitness-and-health-apps.

## Armenia

_In short:_ Armenia requires ads in Armenian; English may sit beside it but never bigger. Treat marketing messages as opt-in. Notify the data authority before handling health data. The competition authority fines misleading claims heavily.

> Not legal advice; check with a local lawyer. Sources and access levels: research/marketing-law-turkey-armenia-argentina.md.

**Messages**
- No statute is written specifically for marketing email or SMS. But the data protection law requires prior express consent. The data authority's advisory decision on phone numbers asks for opt-in, opt-out at any time, and a clear source and purpose. [secondary; Lexology Panoramic, law stated May 2024]
- In March 2025 the Competition Protection Commission fined four bookmakers about AMD 1 billion in total for advertising messages sent without consent. [secondary press; Hetq, 19 March 2025]

**Privacy** (Law on Protection of Personal Data, 18 May 2015)
- Prior express consent is the default. Under-16s need a parent's consent. No data protection officer is required. [secondary; DLA Piper; the law itself not read]
- Notifying the data authority (PDPA, the Personal Data Protection Agency) is voluntary, except before processing biometric or special-category data such as health data. [secondary]
- Transfers abroad: allowed with consent, or to countries the authority lists as adequate. Otherwise you need its permission, which it decides within 30 days. [secondary]
- Breaches: fix unlawful processing within three working days and inform the person. A leak from electronic systems needs an immediate public notice and a report to the police and the PDPA. [secondary]
- There are no cookie rules. Fines are small, up to AMD 500,000. [secondary; Code of Administrative Offences Art. 189.17]
- Reform: the EU visa-liberalisation plan (November 2025) requires an independent data authority. A draft law was being prepared, and nothing had been adopted as of December 2025. [secondary]

**Advertising and language**
- Ads in Armenia must be in standard Armenian. Ads only in a foreign language are banned; registered trademarks are exempt. [first-party; Law on Advertising Art. 5]
- A foreign-language text may accompany the Armenian one. It can't take more space, can't add information the Armenian text lacks, must come after it, and can't be more prominent. [first-party; Art. 5]
- Misleading ads and "undue comparisons" with competitors are unlawful. [first-party; Art. 6, 21]
- Signs, letterheads and labels go in Armenian, with translations where needed. [first-party; Law on Language Art. 4, unofficial translation]
- Prices are set in drams. [first-party; snippet-only]

**Consumer contracts**
- From 1 July 2026: a 14-day withdrawal right for distance purchases. The online order button must state the payment duty (for example "order with payment obligation"). Phone sellers must say who they are and why they are calling. [secondary press; snippet-only]
- Rules on unfair commercial practices and unfair contract terms have been in force since 1 August 2025. The competition authority is being renamed to cover consumer protection too. [secondary; ICPEN, 31 July 2025]

**Enforcement**
- The Competition Protection Commission is the active enforcer. It set a record of over AMD 3 billion in fines in 2024. [secondary press; PAN, 29 April 2025]
- It fined parcel forwarder Globbing about AMD 53 million: the homepage promised 5–10 working days, and at least 26% of air deliveries took longer. [secondary press; snippet-only]

**First steps for a small product**
1. Translate every ad, store listing and promo into Armenian. Keep any English beside it, the same size or smaller.
2. Collect opt-in consent before sending marketing SMS or email, and include an opt-out.
3. Fitness or health apps: notify the PDPA before processing health or biometric data.
4. Check that delivery times and other promises on your homepage match your terms.
5. Before July 2026, add the 14-day withdrawal right and a "pay" label on the order button.

## Argentina

_In short:_ Argentina requires a visible withdrawal button on sites selling online, and a cancel button for subscriptions. Check the No Llame register before phone marketing. Show final prices in pesos. Giveaways can't require purchase.

> Not legal advice; check with a local lawyer. Sources and access levels: research/marketing-law-turkey-armenia-argentina.md.

**Messages**
- Phone marketing (calls in any form, including voice over IP): don't contact anyone on the national "No Llame" (do-not-call) register, and check it every 30 days. The exceptions are existing customers, contacted strictly about their contract, and people who expressly agreed. [first-party; Ley 26.951 Art. 4, 7, 8]
- Email: no opt-in statute was found. The data law requires consent to process data, and anyone may ask at any time to be removed from advertising lists. [first-party; Ley 25.326 Art. 5, 27]
- The AAIP, the data and access-to-information agency, treats contacting people on the register and failing to supervise call-centre contractors as serious offences. [first-party; AAIP Res. 126/2024 Annex I]

**Privacy** (Ley 25.326; AAIP enforces)
- Consent must be free, express and informed, in writing or an equivalent form. [first-party; Art. 5]
- Health data is sensitive: nobody can be forced to provide it. [first-party; Art. 2, 7]
- Databases must be registered with the AAIP. Registration is online and free. [first-party; Art. 21; registration procedure from secondary sources]
- Transfers abroad are banned to countries without adequate protection, subject to the law's exceptions. [first-party; Art. 12]
- Fines are ARS 1,000–100,000 per breach. Repeated identical breaches in one case can be multiplied up to 500 times. Paying within 20 business days halves the fine. [first-party; Art. 31; AAIP Res. 126/2024]
- In February 2025 the AAIP fined a laboratory (Res. 28/2025) for processing data without express consent. [secondary; snippet-only]
- New law: bill 3397-D-2026, introduced 16 July 2026, would replace Ley 25.326. It is at an early stage. [secondary; Allende & Brea, August 2026]

**Advertising, prices and promotions**
- Prices go in pesos and may also be shown in dollars. The shown price is the total final amount. The net price without national taxes appears smaller, labelled "PRECIO SIN IMPUESTOS NACIONALES". [first-party; Res. SIyC 4/2025]
- Misleading ads are banned. Comparative ads are allowed if they compare verifiable features objectively and don't denigrate the competitor. [first-party; DNU 274/2019 Art. 11, 15]
- Contests and draws can't require a purchase. [first-party; DNU 274/2019 Art. 14(b); later amendments not checked]
- An offer binds you for the period it is advertised. In high inflation, update the ad when you change the price. [first-party; Ley 24.240 Art. 7]

**Consumer contracts and subscriptions** (Ley 24.240)
- Withdrawal: 10 calendar days for online purchases, which can't be waived. The seller pays the return costs. [first-party; Art. 34]
- "Botón de arrepentimiento" (withdrawal button): every website selling at a distance shows it "a simple vista, en lugar destacado y en el primer acceso", with no sign-up required. [first-party; Disposición 954/2025 Art. 1, which replaced Res. 424/2020]
- "Botón de baja de servicio" (cancel-service button): required for services contracted at a distance. Within 24 hours, send a tracking code and process the request by the same channel. [first-party; Disp. 954/2025 Art. 4–5; Ley 24.240 Art. 10 ter]
- Since February 2026 you may ask for a reasonable identity check before acting on either button. [first-party; Disposición 3/2026]
- Charges the consumer has to opt out of are banned. [first-party; Art. 35]
- Fines run from 0.5 to 2,100 basic household baskets. [first-party; Art. 47]
- A federal appeals court upheld ARS 4 million against Cencosud (Jumbo) and ARS 2 million each against two appliance retailers in 2022, because their withdrawal links were plain text or buried. [secondary; court reports, November 2022]

**Language**
- Sale documents are in Spanish, and product labels in Spanish. Translations may not be more prominent than the Spanish. [first-party; Ley 24.240 Art. 10; DNU 274/2019 Art. 20]

**First steps for a small product**
1. If you sell from your own website, put "Botón de arrepentimiento" in a visible spot on the first screen. For subscriptions, add "Botón de baja de servicio" too.
2. Answer withdrawal or cancellation requests within 24 hours with a tracking code.
3. Show peso prices as the final total.
4. Register your user database with the AAIP.
5. Put an unsubscribe link in every marketing email and honour it.
6. Run giveaways without any purchase requirement.
7. Before any phone or call-centre campaign, download the No Llame list.
