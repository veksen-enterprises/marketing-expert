## What investors actually ask for

- Sequoia's current business-plan guide: "Identify your customer and your market. Some of the best companies invent their own markets." It no longer prescribes TAM/SAM/SOM; it puts **Why now?** right before market. [first-party, read]
- YC (Ralston): "The (huge) market… TAM > $1B if possible. Include the most persuasive evidence you have that this is real." YC (Livingston): "market sizes are impossible to predict… our advice is not even to try to hit a big market early on." [first-party, read]
- Andreessen: market size is "the number, **and growth rate**, of those customers." [practitioner]

So: customer first, number second, evidence that it's real, and growth.

## Definitions and methods

- **TAM**: total demand for the category. **SAM**: the part your product and channels can serve today. **SOM**: what you can realistically win in a set period.
- **Bottom-up (primary)**: number of target accounts × annual revenue per account. Every factor checkable; account counts must come from a named source.
- **Top-down (cross-check only)**: an analyst or government total, filtered. If it differs from bottom-up by more than ~3×, the category or the per-account value is wrong.
- **Value-based** (when no spend exists yet): value created per customer × share you can capture × customers. Easy to inflate; keep it as an upside scenario.
- **SOM is bounded by capacity**, not a share you pick: reps × deals per rep, or budget ÷ CAC, minus churn. Use market_size.

## Errors that shrink the market (Gurley vs Damodaran, 2014)

- Damodaran valued Uber using the global taxi market (~$100B), 10% maximum share → ~$5.9B. Gurley argued that a much better product expands its own market (shorter waits, coverage, price, new uses: suburbs, rental replacement, kids, car-ownership replacement) and put the potential market at $450B–$1.3T. [first-party, read]
- Outcome: Uber's Mobility segment alone runs ~$110B/year in gross bookings (Q4 2025 annualised), above Damodaran's whole 2014 market. The market did expand. [first-party]
- Kalanick: San Francisco taxi+limo spend was ~$120M in the seed deck; "we're a very healthy multiple bigger than that right now… it's not about the market that exists, it's about the market we're creating."

**Rule**: when a product changes price, convenience or reliability by a large step, size (a) the spend it replaces and (b) a separate expansion scenario with the assumption written down. Damodaran's useful split: **probable** (in the base case), **plausible** (raises growth), **possible** (option value only).

## Errors that inflate the market

- **"1% of a $100B market"**: Thiel calls it a red flag: a large market either lacks a good starting point or is open to competition, so 1% is hard to reach and profitless. [practitioner, read]
- **"Big market delusion"** (Cornell & Damodaran): in big, visible markets each entrant overestimates its chances and underrates competition; collectively they're priced as if all win. [research]
- **Spend through you ≠ budget for you**: Apple's "$1.4T App Store ecosystem" (2025) is $1.1T physical goods and services, $149B digital, $151B advertising. An app developer's pool is near the $149B line. [first-party]
- **Counting non-buyers**: Thiel's PayPal on PalmPilots had "millions of users" who had little in common and didn't need it; the few thousand eBay PowerSellers did, and PayPal reached 25% of them in three months. [anecdote, read] Count accounts with the problem and a budget.
- **Analyst categories** rarely match your product's job. Christensen: experts' forecasts for emerging markets are reliably wrong.
- **Reverse-engineered SOM**: setting SOM to what the plan needs. Airbnb's 2008 deck claimed 15% of its available market pre-launch; the stronger evidence was its validation slide (670k Couchsurfing users, 17k Craigslist listings).

## Small markets that grow

- Thiel: "Every startup should start with a very small market… it's easier to dominate a small market than a large one." Then expand to adjacent markets (Amazon: books → CDs → video → everything). [practitioner, read]
- Graham: a small market is fine if it's a "well" (few people wanting it intensely) with a fast path out (Facebook: Harvard → colleges). Microsoft began with a couple thousand Altair owners. [practitioner, read]
- Christensen: small markets "don't solve the growth needs of large companies", which is why incumbents leave them open (see competing-with-incumbents).

## Timing: "why now"

- Sequoia: "The best companies almost always have a clear why now? Nature hates a vacuum, so why hasn't your solution been built before now?"
- Enabling conditions to name: a cost or capability threshold (YouTube after broadband passed ~50% of US homes and Flash solved video playback), a new platform (smartphones, app stores, foundation models), a regulatory change, a behaviour shift.
- **Bill Gross's "timing explains 42%"** (TED 2015): one person ranking 200 companies from memory, with outcomes known; external examples chosen as extremes; no method for "42% of the difference"; not replicated. A useful prompt, not a measured effect. [anecdote, transcript read]

## First-mover evidence

- **Golder & Tellis (1993)**, ~500 brands in 50 categories, correcting earlier studies that only looked at survivors. In the 36 categories with full data: almost half of market pioneers failed (**47%**); pioneers' mean market share ~**10%** (earlier survivor-only studies found ~30%); pioneers led in only 11% of categories. Early leaders entered on average **13 years** after pioneers, failed only 8% of the time and held ~28% share. [research, read in full]
- **Lieberman & Montgomery** (1988, review 2013): first-mover advantages come from technology leadership, pre-empting scarce assets and buyer switching costs; disadvantages from followers free-riding, uncertainty resolving for followers, technology shifts and incumbent inertia. The 2013 review: advantages to early movers "often exist, but are by no mean[s] inevitable", show up more for market share than for profit or survival, and fade over time. [research]
- **Suarez & Lanzolla** (2005, 2007): early-entry advantage depends on the pace of technology change and of market growth. Both slow ("calm waters") favours pioneers; both fast ("rough waters", e.g. Netscape) disables early-entry advantage. [research]

**Takeaway**: being early isn't the advantage. Name what would make early entry last (switching costs, scarce inputs, network density) and the resources to survive until the market forms.

## Growth, urgency and buyer structure

- A $200M segment growing 40%/year is ~$1B in about 5 years; a $5B segment growing 2% only offers share taken from incumbents.
- Urgency beats breadth early: users "who want it urgently… usually this initial group is small" (Graham); users whose "hair is on fire" will try an imperfect v1 (Seibel, YC).
- **Fragmented buyers** (many small businesses): low ACV, CAC-sensitive, need self-serve or channel distribution. **Concentrated buyers** (a few hundred enterprises): high ACV, long cycles, the TAM is a named list. **Concentrated incumbents**: entrenched switching costs; entry needs a step change.

## Where to get the counts

- US Census **County Business Patterns** (establishments with employees, by industry and size, down to county; Census says it's used "for analyzing market potential… setting sales quotas"), **Statistics of U.S. Businesses** (firms by size class), **Nonemployer Statistics** (solo businesses). Data lags 2–3 years. Count establishments for location-based products, firms for software sold to HQ.
- Others (indicative, deduplicate): LinkedIn Sales Navigator, technographic data (BuiltWith/Wappalyzer), app-store intelligence, association membership, licensing registries, incumbents' 10-K (US annual report) segment revenue (floor for current spend).

## Sanity checks

1. **Sum of incumbents**: top players' revenue should be a plausible fraction of TAM. If TAM is 50× their combined revenue, say the category is new or fix the TAM.
2. **Implied penetration**: plan revenue ÷ SAM in year 5. Above ~10–20% in a contested market needs a reason (network effects, switching costs).
3. **$100M ARR test**: ACV $1k → 100,000 customers; $10k → 10,000; $50k → 2,000; $250k → 400. Compare with qualifying accounts.
4. **Name the budget line** the money comes from.

## Venture-scale or not

- Returns are concentrated: ~6% of deals produced ~60% of returns (Horsley Bridge via a16z); ~65% of financings returned < 1× (Correlation Ventures); Dropbox and Airbnb were ~¾ of YC's portfolio value (Graham); Facebook returned more than all other investments in Founders Fund's 2005 fund combined (Thiel).
- Fund arithmetic: a $300M fund owning ~10% needs a $3B exit to return the fund → roughly $300–600M revenue → a SAM in the billions. Hence "TAM > $1B."
- A $50–300M SAM with urgent demand, little competition and a reachable list can support a profitable $5–30M ARR company that the founders own. That's a "failure" under venture maths and a great outcome otherwise.
- **Choose bootstrapping** when the $100M ARR test fails and no adjacency credibly expands the market. **Choose venture** when the product expands its own market, when network effects allow > 20% share, or when a fast path leads from a niche into a much larger adjacent market.

## Worked example (invented numbers; replace each with a sourced figure)

Scheduling software for independent US vet clinics:
- 30,000 vet establishments (CBP) × 70% independent × 60% with 2+ vets = 12,600 accounts × $6,000 ACV → **SAM $75.6M**.
- 15% of SAM in 5 years → ~1,900 clinics → **~$11M ARR**: a strong bootstrapped business.
- $100M ARR needs 16,700 clinics, more than the whole SAM → not venture-scale unless ACV rises (e.g. payments) or it expands to adjacent verticals (dental, physio).

Run this with market_size: it computes the capacity-bounded SOM and the target penetration.

## Decision rules

1. Lead with the customer and the count source, not the number.
2. Bottom-up first; top-down as a check; explain any > 3× gap.
3. Never "1% of $X billion"; derive share from reachable accounts × win rate or capacity.
4. Size what you replace and the expansion separately; keep expansion out of the base case.
5. Remove money that only passes through you.
6. Start with a small, concentrated, urgent group and show the path to the next one.
7. Report growth rate and its driver alongside size.
8. Make "why now" concrete and dated.
9. Don't present "first mover" as an advantage; name the mechanism that makes early entry last.
10. Run the $100M ARR test before choosing how to fund the company.
