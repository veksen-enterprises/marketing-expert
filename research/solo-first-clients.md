# Where do a new solo practitioner's first clients come from? (checked 2026-10-28)

Question: a person who sells their own expertise (a dietitian, a freelance designer, a coach) starts with zero clients. Do their first clients come from their own network, a former employer, referral partners, directories, platforms or content? Today `match_small_bets` gives such a profile no bet that uses the person's own network. Should it?

## 1. No survey found separates the first client from clients in general

- Every survey found asks where freelancers get work now. None asks where the first client came from, and none splits answers by years in business. [derived from 1–6]
- The one study of first paying customers is about tech startups, not solo services. It sorts 72 ventures by route: strong ties, weak ties, or no ties. The abstract gives no share for each route. It links "only using strong ties" with survival and "no ties" with higher revenue. [research, 7, abstract-only; fetch summary; 72 ventures, one region; configurational, not causal]

## 2. For clients in general, word of mouth and personal networks usually lead

- MBO Partners (2014): word of mouth 75%, agency 12%, "reference from employer" 4%, online job site 3%, social media 3%. [vendor, 2, fetch summary; US online poll, n=2,017 per source 1; press retelling; question wording not seen]
- Qdos (2017): 48% "secure new work through referrals and their personal network"; agencies or job boards 44%; cold email or phone 3%. [vendor, 3, fetch summary; UK, n=641, mostly IT contractors]
- Localogy (2022): word of mouth 65%, freelance platforms 54%, social media 50% (multiple answers). [vendor, 4, fetch summary; sample size not stated] [corrected 2026-10-28: the article does not say multiple answers were allowed; that is inferred from shares summing to 259% (it also lists employment agencies 46% and job boards 44%, later 41%)]
- Freelancers Union and Upwork (2016): "most freelancers find work through their own personal networks"; top methods were friends and family, professional contacts and social media. [vendor, 5, fetch summary; US, n>6,000; figures not seen]
- The exception: freelancermap's 2025 survey put platforms first (34%), personal networks 23%, referrals 14%. Its respondents are mostly German IT freelancers recruited by a platform. [vendor, 6, fetch summary; secondary report]
- So network or word of mouth comes first in four of five surveys. All five are vendor surveys of the freelancers' own recall. None is a record of where paying clients came from. [derived from 2–6]
- "Reference from employer" was small (4%) in the one survey that named it. That is clients in general, not first clients. [vendor, 2]

## 4. Agreement and disagreement with existing notes

- Agrees with Hinge's buyers' side (`services-and-retail.md` item 2): buyers found a new firm by asking someone 71% of the time. The freelancer surveys are the seller's side of the same pattern. Both are vendor data.
- Agrees with `professional-services.md` that referrals are "usually the largest source". But that section lists past and current clients first. A person with zero clients has neither, so their list is former colleagues, employers, friends and complementary practitioners.
- Disagrees a little with `devtools-cases-and-agent-choice.md`: there, founder-driven routes led with 39% of 31, but that group bundles network with cold outreach and the founder's audience. These sources do not let the two be split either.
- Adds nothing to `partners-referral-outbound.md` §2: those studies are about customers referring customers, which needs customers.

## 6. Weak ties (added 2026-10-28, after the owner's point)

- In a 5-year experiment, LinkedIn randomly varied how many weak ties its "People You May Know" suggestions gave over 20 million people. Moderately weak ties (measured by mutual connections) led to the most job moves: an inverted U between tie strength and jobs passed on. Weak ties helped more in digital industries, strong ties in less digital ones. [research, Rajkumar, Saint-Jacques, Bojinov, Brynjolfsson & Aral, *Science* 377(6612), 2022, doi:10.1126/science.abl4476; https://digitaleconomy.stanford.edu/publication/a-causal-test-of-the-strength-of-weak-ties; read 2026-10-28; fetch summary; abstract only; job moves, not clients; one platform]
- So a solo practitioner's network is worth building beyond people who could buy: acquaintances pass work on. The evidence is about jobs moving through ties, not clients, so it supports the direction, not a number. [derived]

## What this changes

The lead's rule was: add the bet only if surveys put the person's own network first **as a first-client source**. No source measures first clients. So, by that rule: **no `warm-network` bet**. A note at most.

**A note on `cold-one-to-one` for one-person services.** In `src/lib/smallBetsCatalog.ts`, add to `cold-one-to-one` a `notes` entry matched on the saved category or product: `match: /\b(dietitians?|nutritionists?|coach(es|ing)?|therapists?|freelanc\w*|consultants?|designers?|bookkeepers?|translators?)\b/i`, note: "For a service you sell yourself, start the list with people who already know your work: former colleagues, a former employer, people in your field who serve the same clients. In vendor surveys of freelancers, word of mouth and personal networks were the most-named source of work in four of five (clients in general, not first clients)". Notes are read from category or product (`src/lib/smallBets.ts` line 358), which `factsFromProfile` passes.

Test (`test/smallBets.test.ts`): profile `{ category: "registered dietitian", audiences: ["consumers"], surfaces: ["service"], launched: true }` → `cold-one-to-one` in fitsNow, reason matches `/already know your work/`. Profile `{ category: "invoicing software", audiences: ["businesses"], surfaces: ["web-app"], launched: true }` → `cold-one-to-one` reason does not match it.

Why a note and not a bet: telling one's network is the obvious first move, and four of five surveys back networks in general. But the catalog grades bets by evidence, and there is no first-client measure to grade. The note says exactly what was measured.

## What this does not settle

- Where first clients come from, by count. A survey that asks "where did your first paying client come from?", by years in business, would settle it. None was found.
- Whether a former employer becoming a client is common. One "reference from employer" figure (4%, clients in general) and one designer's story.
- Dietitians and other health professionals: no client-source data found. Whether a professional order allows asking friends and family for clients, or referral fees, is lead #130's ground.
- Whether a message to one's own network is a "commercial electronic message" under Canada's anti-spam law, and whether the personal-relationship exemption covers former colleagues. `cold-one-to-one` carries `law: "messages"`; the advisor should check.
- A profile that avoids `cold-outreach` loses `cold-one-to-one` and so the note, though telling friends is not cold. Whether that matters is a build question.
