## 1. Monetizing Innovation (Ramanujam & Tacke 2016)

- **Core thesis.** Have the willingness-to-pay (WTP) conversation before building, then design the product around the price. Do not set the price after launch. [1][2] [practitioner, from Simon-Kucher's >10,000 projects; no controlled evidence]
- **Headline stat.** "72% of innovations fail to meet their financial targets or fail entirely." The ~28% that succeed have early WTP conversations and C-level ownership of monetization. [1][2] [practitioner; First Round attributes it to a 2014 Simon-Kucher study of companies under price pressure, method not described; cite it as a book claim] [read 2026-10-05]
- **Four failure types** [1]:
  - **Feature shock**: too many features crammed into one product. It is overpriced for most buyers, and value is hard to communicate.
  - **Minivation**: priced too low, leaving revenue on the table.
  - **Hidden gem**: a valuable innovation never commercialized because it sits outside the core business.
  - **Undead**: a product customers don't want, launched anyway.
- **WTP questions** (a simplified PSM): "What is an acceptable price?", "What is an expensive price?", "What is a prohibitively expensive price?" Acceptable = price for growth. Expensive = what customers will pay but dislike, and true WTP usually sits near here. Prohibitive = the ceiling. Superhuman priced this way: per NFX (https://www.nfx.com/post/the-hidden-world-of-pricing), it ran Van Westendorp's PSM with early users, took the median "expensive but I'd still buy" answer ($29/month) and rounded to $30. [3] [practitioner; Lenny summary not re-read, NFX read 2026-10-05; corrected]
- **Segmentation.** Segment on needs, value and WTP, not on demographics or firmographics. Example from the book: a paper company re-segmented into "Want Price Only", "Want It Now", "Want Product Only" and similar groups. "One size fits none." [1, ch. 5] [practitioner]
- **Packaging: leaders, fillers, killers** [1, ch. 6][2]:
  - Leaders drive purchase and high WTP, so they anchor tiers.
  - Fillers are nice-to-haves that round out a bundle.
  - Killers reduce WTP when bundled, so sell them as paid add-ons, not inclusions.
- **Good-better-best.** Turns a yes/no purchase into "which one" and serves price-optimizers, quality-optimizers and the middle. [1, ch. 6] [practitioner; see §3 for the behavioral basis]

## 2. Value metrics and usage-based pricing (UBP)

- **Value metric** = the unit you charge per (seats, contacts, API calls, revenue processed). Campbell's (ProfitWell) position: tie price to the unit that scales with customer value, so revenue expands with the customer's growth and "per-user" becomes a default only when users really track value. [practitioner; widely relayed in interviews, primary text not read]
- **Claimed ProfitWell data.** Value-metric companies grow expansion revenue faster than feature-differentiated ones. Outcome metrics (money saved or earned) perform best. ProfitWell claimed a dataset of ~37,000 SaaS companies. [vendor data, snippet-only via secondary summaries; no dated primary report located]
- **UBP adoption, OpenView survey series** [17]:
  - 34% of SaaS companies used some UBP in 2020.
  - 45% in 2021 (2021 report, ~600 SaaS companies surveyed). [read 2026-10-05 in OpenView's release: 45%, "up 32% from 2020" (i.e. ~34%), nearly 600 respondents]
  - 61% in 2022, published in the 2023 report.
  - 2023 report breakdown: 23% usage-based subscription tiers, 18% largely usage-based, 17% testing, 42% none. 46% hybrid. [61% and 46% confirmed only via secondary relays (TechCrunch 2023-02-02, DigitalRoute), 2026-10-04; the 23/18/17/42 split not re-checked]
  - [vendor data: OpenView was a VC investor in PLG/UBP companies; self-selected survey respondents]
- **UBP and net dollar retention (NDR), 2021 report.** Top-quartile NDR was 122% for largely usage-based companies vs 110% for usage-based tiers vs 109% for no UBP. Among public software companies, UBP companies grew 29.9% vs 21.7% and had NDR of 120% vs 110% [read 2026-10-05 in OpenView's release; the 122/110/109 top-quartile split not re-seen]. [17] [vendor data; correlational, so survivorship and category mix (infrastructure/API businesses) likely confound]
- **Operational.** UBP aligns price with value but makes revenue forecasting harder and lets a customer's spend drop when its usage falls. Hybrid (platform fee + usage) is the modal 2023 choice. [17] [vendor data]
- **Do not cite.** Numbers circulating on AI-generated SEO sites ("2x expansion and 50% better retention", "38% faster growth 2020–22", "23% higher ARPU", "segmented price increases cause 30% less churn") could not be traced to any dated ProfitWell or Paddle primary report.

## 3. Packaging psychology: decoy, compromise, anchoring

- **Decoy / attraction effect.** Adding an option that is dominated by the target but not by the competitor raises the target's choice share. This violates "regularity", the rule that adding an option can't raise another option's share. [4] [replicated in lab with numeric attribute stimuli]
- **Ariely's Economist example** [9]:
  - With three options (web $59; print $125; print+web $125), 100 MIT students chose 16 / 0 / 84.
  - Without the print-only decoy, they chose 68 web / 32 combo.
  - [single study: a classroom demonstration reported in a popular book, not a peer-reviewed paper; no independent replication with the same stimuli found]
- **Replication concerns** [5]:
  - Frederick, Lee & Baskin (2014) ran 19 studies with more realistic stimuli. The attraction effect was largely confined to stylized, fully numeric attribute tables.
  - It typically vanished when one attribute was perceptual or experienced (e.g., tasted or seen), and sometimes reversed into a "repulsion" effect.
  - [replicated null/limit finding; 19 studies (1a–1s) and the repulsion effect confirmed in the author copy, read 2026-10-05]
- **Rebuttal** [6]. The original authors replied that the effect needs specific conditions: a clear, easily seen dominance relation and weak prior preferences between the core options. Simonson [7] also wrote a critique of the replications. [practitioner/academic debate]
- **Field evidence.**
  - Wu & Cosguner (2020) found real decoys in an online diamond retailer's sales data.
  - Shoppers detected the decoy-dominant relation only 11–25% of the time.
  - When detected, the dominant diamond's sale hazard rose 1.8–3.2×. Estimated gross profit uplift: 14.3%. [8]
  - [single study, observational and structural model; read 2026-10-05: detection 25% / 17% / 11% in the low / medium / high price segments, $2K–$20K diamonds]
- **Operational takeaway for pricing pages.**
  - A decoy tier works best when tiers are compared on clean numeric attributes (seats, GB, price) and dominance is obvious.
  - Expect a weak effect when the value of a tier is qualitative.
  - Always A/B test. Don't assume the textbook lift.
- **Compromise effect.** Options in the middle of a set gain share, and options at the extremes lose it ("extremeness aversion"). This is the behavioral basis for good-better-best, and for adding a premium tier to pull choices to the middle. [10] [replicated in lab literature; metadata only verified]

## 4. Charm pricing / left-digit effect

- **Lab mechanism.** Across five experiments, Thomas & Morwitz (2005) found that people judge $2.99 as meaningfully smaller than $3.00. This happens only when the leftmost digit changes: $3.59 vs $3.60 shows no effect. People encode a number's size anchored on its left digit. The effect also appears for non-price numbers. [11] [single paper, multiple studies; widely cited]
- **Field.** Anderson & Simester (2003) ran three field experiments in women's apparel mail-order catalogs, with the same item at e.g. $34 / $39 / $44 across catalog versions.
  - $9 endings raised demand in all three experiments.
  - In one test, demand rose ~40% at $9 endings, and $39 outsold $34.
  - The effect was stronger for new items, where customers have less price knowledge.
  - The effect weakened when a "sale" sign was also present, which suggests 9-endings signal a deal.
  - [single study, field; strong design]
- **Counter-evidence.** Fenneman et al. (2022) ran an online experiment (n=266, 4,788 purchase decisions on otherwise identical lottery tickets) and found no left-digit or perceptual-fluency effect on purchase decisions. [13] [single study, null; read 2026-10-05]
- **Operational.**
  - Charm endings help most when the left digit changes, the item is new or unfamiliar, and the brand is value-positioned.
  - Round prices (e.g., $100 rather than $99) are often used to signal premium positioning. [practitioner; not verified here]
  - In B2B SaaS, where price is evaluated deliberately, expect small effects.

## 5. Price research methods: PSM vs Gabor-Granger vs conjoint

- **Van Westendorp PSM (1976)** [14]
  - Four questions: at what price is it too cheap (you'd doubt the quality), cheap/a bargain, expensive but still considered, and too expensive to consider?
  - Plot the cumulative curves and read off the intersections: optimal price point, indifference price point, and the acceptable range (from the point of marginal cheapness to the point of marginal expensiveness).
  - Weaknesses:
    - It measures perception, not purchase.
    - It assumes respondents know the category's price levels.
    - It tests one concept at a time.
    - It has no grounding in economic theory for choosing an optimum.
    - It is open to strategic answering.
  - The Newton–Miller–Smith (1993) extension adds purchase-likelihood questions at the "cheap" and "expensive" answers, which yields a demand and revenue curve. [secondary methods literature; not on the Sawtooth page] [practitioner consensus]
- **Gabor-Granger (1966)** [15]
  - Ask "Would you buy at $X?", then step the price up after a yes and down after a no. This traces a demand curve for one product and finds the revenue-maximizing price.
  - The original paper found price-response curves that were not monotone and linked this to price acting as a quality signal.
  - Weakness: it is so obviously a pricing game to respondents that the answers may not predict real purchases, and it gives no feature-level information. [16] [vendor guidance; read 2026-10-05; corrected]
- **Choice-based conjoint (CBC)**
  - Respondents choose among full product profiles in which price varies alongside features.
  - It estimates WTP for each feature and simulates share for many configurations, including competitors.
  - Sawtooth (a vendor of conjoint software) says conjoint suits new products or changes to existing ones, while PSM and Gabor-Granger each test a single product concept; its weaknesses are cost and time. It does not rank it "most realistic"; it says monadic price experiments with competitors shown most resemble real market choices. [16] [vendor data; read 2026-10-05; corrected]
- **When to use which**:
  - Early concept, no price anchor yet: PSM, or Ramanujam's three-question version, to get a range.
  - One defined product needing a price point: Gabor-Granger.
  - Packaging or tier design, feature-level WTP, competitive context: CBC. It needs a larger sample and expert design.
  - Validate all three in market. Survey WTP is hypothetical. [synthesis]

## 6. Price increases, fairness, grandfathering

- **Dual entitlement** [19]:
  - Telephone surveys found that raising prices to pass on higher costs is seen as fair.
  - Exploiting a demand spike is seen as unfair: 82% rated a snow-shovel price rise from $15 to $20 after a storm as unfair or very unfair.
  - [replicated in many fairness surveys]
  - Operational: tie the increase to added value or cost, not to "because we can".
- **Grandfathering.**
  - Campbell's practitioner view: don't grandfather indefinitely. Make the transition easy, explain the added value, and consider a 6–12-month delay for sensitive segments. [practitioner, snippet-only via secondary]
  - No peer-reviewed or dated first-party dataset on SaaS grandfathering outcomes was found.
- **Fairness asymmetry.** Unfairness perception also affects demand in the field, e.g. Anderson & Simester's work on premium prices for large clothing sizes. [title only, not read]

## 7. Free trial vs freemium vs reverse trial

- **Opt-in vs opt-out trials** [25]
  - The Totango 2012 figures as relayed by Chargebee:
    - Card-required (opt-out) trials converted ~50% of trial users to paid. No-card (opt-in) trials converted ~15%.
    - No-card trials drew far more signups and had better 90-day retention: 80% vs 60%.
    - End-to-end conversion of visitors was reportedly about 2× higher for no-card trials (1.2% vs 0.6%); visit-to-trial ~2% (card) vs ~10% (no card).
    - Sample: ~100 SaaS vendors.
  - [vendor data; 50/15, 80/60, 1.2/0.6, 2%/10% and the 100-vendor sample read on chargebee.com 2026-10-05; Chargebee calls the 90-day retention a "positive correlation". A Totango press page "What makes a free trial work (or not)" exists on totango.com but the figures were not seen on it. Original report still not located: treat as directional]
- **OpenView 2022 Product Benchmarks** [18]
  - Per 1,000 visitors, the median freemium company converted ~3 to paid, about 2 of them without sales contact.
  - The median free-trial company converted ~7, about 1 without sales contact.
  - Freemium produced more signups and more product virality.
  - [vendor data; freemium side read 2026-10-05 in OpenView's release (6% visit-to-signup, 5% of freemium signups convert to paid); free-trial side still snippet-only]
- **Freemium conversion.** Median freemium conversion is ~3–5% (attributed to ProfitWell; [vendor data, snippet-only]). Campbell: "freemium is an acquisition model, not a revenue model". [practitioner]
- **Reverse trial**
  - New users get the paid tier for a limited time with no card, then drop to a permanent free tier instead of being locked out.
  - The term is popularized by Elena Verna (neither cited source says who coined it). Verna writes that reverse trials "usually" lift conversion ~20%, if users make heavy use of paid features during the trial. A 10–40% range attributed to her by CXL could not be read (403). [24] [practitioner, no published dataset; read 2026-10-05; corrected]
- **Operational.**
  - Card-required trials give higher trial-to-paid rates but fewer signups. Optimize for paid customers per visitor, not trial-to-paid rate.
  - Freemium fits products with low marginal cost and viral or collaborative use.
  - A reverse trial is a hedge: users experience the premium tier, and the free floor keeps them around.

## 8. Discounting and reference price

- **Reference price** is the internal price standard buyers compare against. It is formed from past prices, promotion history and context. [23] [review article]
- **Promotion frequency and depth both lower expected price.** In Kalwani & Yim's (1992) controlled experiment, prices elicited directly from respondents dropped as the brand promoted more often and more deeply. [22] [single study, consistent with modeling literature in 23]
- **Sale signs wear out**:
  - Sale signs lift demand less when more items carry them, and category sales peak when only some items are signed.
  - Credibility falls as the signs spread. [21]
  - [single paper, multiple data sources: catalog field test, surveys, grocery data]
- **Discounting can alienate existing customers**:
  - In a 28-month randomized field experiment with more than 50,000 customers, people who saw a lower price than they had recently paid bought less across the whole firm, a "boycott".
  - Among customers who had paid a high price for a discounted item in the 3 months before the catalog, orders fell 14.8% and revenue by more than $90 per customer ($90.71) over the 28 months. The effect lasted more than a year but decayed over time. [read 2026-10-05; corrected]
  - The effect was strongest among the most valuable recent, high-price buyers.
  - It replicated at a second firm. [20] [replicated across 2 firms]
- **Operational.**
  - Prefer targeted, time-boxed or conditional discounts (annual prepay, volume) over broad list-price cuts.
  - Protect recent buyers, e.g. with price guarantees or credits.
  - Limit how often discounts appear so the reference price doesn't drop.
