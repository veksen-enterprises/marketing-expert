## 1. Christensen's disruption theory: the mechanism, not the buzzword

- **Definition (2015).** Disruption is "a process whereby a smaller company with fewer resources is able to successfully challenge established incumbent businesses" [read 2026-10-05, src 3]. It is a *process* that starts in a foothold. It is not a product attribute, and the term does not apply to every entrant that wins.
- **Two footholds** [read 2026-10-05, src 3; the typology originates in src 2]:
  - **Low-end foothold.** Incumbents serve their most demanding and profitable customers. They overserve the low end and leave it under-attended. The entrant offers "good enough" at a lower price.
  - **New-market foothold.** The entrant creates a market where none existed and turns non-consumers into consumers.
- **Uber is not disruptive (2015).** Uber did not start at the low end or in a new market. "Uber's service has rarely been described as inferior to existing taxis; in fact, many would say it is better." The authors say "most of the elements of Uber's strategy seem to be sustaining innovations", which "make good products better in the eyes of an incumbent's existing customers" [read 2026-10-05, src 3; corrected: quote wording]. They call Uber an outlier and say "the regulated nature of the taxi business is a large part of the answer" [read 2026-10-05, src 3].
- **Quality trajectory.** Disruptive offerings do not catch on with mainstream customers until their quality catches up to mainstream standards [secondary snippet, src 3]. Early mainstream rejection is therefore expected, not disconfirming.
- **Sustaining innovations: incumbents usually win.** This is a central claim from the 1997 disk-drive studies [background, unverified this session, src 1]. It is consistent with Chandy & Tellis (2000), who cite Christensen (1993): every disk-drive form-factor change (8" to 5.25" to 3.5") was initiated by a non-incumbent and led to the downfall of the previously dominant firm [research, src 12, read in full, as a citation of Christensen].
- **Asymmetric motivation** (src 2) [background, unverified this session]. The incumbent is rationally motivated to move *upmarket*, toward higher margins and its best customers. It is not motivated to defend the low-margin low end. The entrant is motivated to move up. Both firms run toward the same upper tier, so the incumbent tends to cede ground rather than fight. The mechanism rests on resource-allocation processes (margin hurdles, best-customer pressure). It does not rest on incompetence.

## 2. Empirical tests and critiques

**King & Baatartogtokh (2015), MIT SMR** [research, snippet-only, src 4] (re-check 2026-10-05: results text paywalled; the article's survey appendix confirms the four elements were coded from expert answers)
- Method: they surveyed or interviewed one or more industry experts (relays give **79** or **82** experts in total) for each of the **77 cases** Christensen and co-authors used as examples of disruption in *The Innovator's Dilemma* and *The Innovator's Solution*.
- They decomposed the theory into four testable elements. Counts of the 77 cases where experts saw each element:
  1. Incumbents improving along a sustaining trajectory: **53/77**
  2. That improvement **overshoots** customer needs: **17/77**
  3. Incumbents had the capability to respond but failed to exploit it: **47/77**
  4. Incumbents **floundered** as a result: **48/77**
- Only **9%** of the 77 (about 7 cases) showed all four. [77 cases, four element counts and 9% verified-search: rhsmith.umd.edu news item, researchgate.net, digitopoly.org (element counts via relay snippets), 2026-10-04]
- Reported in derivative coverage: 78% of experts thought incumbents were *not* overshooting customer needs, and in 39% of cases incumbents survived or even thrived [secondary snippet]. Both are consistent with the 17/77 and 48/77 counts (48/77 ≈ 62% floundered, matching a relay that says incumbents floundered in about 62% of cases).

**Lepore (2014), "The Disruption Machine," New Yorker** [background, unverified this session, src 5]
- Main lines of critique as commonly summarized: the cases were hand-picked and examined after the fact; several "disrupted" incumbents, disk-drive makers among them, later recovered or persisted; and the theory works as an ideology of inevitability more than as a predictive model. Exact quotes and figures were not retrieved.
- **Christensen's rebuttal.** He responded publicly in mid-2014 in a Bloomberg Businessweek interview, defending the disk-drive evidence and objecting to how he was characterized. The text was not retrieved. With Raynor and McDonald, the 2015 HBR article (src 3) narrowed the definition and conceded that the term had been over-applied [read 2026-10-05 for the 2015 piece].

**Gans (2016), *The Disruption Dilemma*** [read 2026-10-05 via a book review quoting the book, src 6]
- Gans defines disruption as what a firm faces "when the choices that once drove its success become those that destroy its future."
- **Demand-side disruption** (Christensen's low-end case): the incumbent listens to its main customers and underrates entrants serving niche demand.
- **Supply-side disruption** draws on Henderson & Clark (1990) on architectural innovation. Incumbents organized around existing component competencies cannot absorb a change in how components link together, i.e. the product architecture.
- Diagnostic: is the incumbent *unwilling* (demand side) or *unable* (supply side)?

## 3. Helmer's 7 Powers and counter-positioning

**Core definitions** [secondary snippet, src 7]
- *Power* is "the set of conditions creating the potential for persistent differential returns."
- Every Power needs a **Benefit**, a material improvement in cash flow, and a **Barrier**, something that stops competitors from arbitraging the benefit away even when they fully understand it.
- Helmer's value equation: NPV = M₀ · g · s · m. These are market size, discounted growth factor, long-term share and long-term differential margin. Power is what keeps *m* above zero.

**The seven Powers, with benefit and barrier** (assembled from multiple secondary summaries [secondary snippet]. Check against the book before quoting):

| Power | Benefit | Barrier |
|---|---|---|
| Scale economies | Lower unit cost as volume grows | Prohibitive cost for a challenger to gain share |
| Network economies | Value per user rises with users | Challenger must subsidize users to join a smaller network |
| Counter-positioning | New, superior business model | **Collateral damage** to the incumbent's existing business if it copies |
| Switching costs | Ability to charge more to existing customers | Losses customers would incur by switching |
| Branding | Higher willingness to pay (affect plus "peace of mind") | **Hysteresis**: long build time, uncertain payoff for an imitator |
| Cornered resource | Preferential access, on attractive terms, to a coveted asset | Asset is idiosyncratic and **non-arbitraged** |
| Process power | Better product or lower cost from embedded routines | Hysteresis: complex, slow to replicate |

**Power Progression: which Powers are available when** [secondary snippet, src 7]
- **Origination** (before takeoff): **counter-positioning** and **cornered resource**.
- **Takeoff** (rapid growth): **scale economies, network economies, switching costs**.
- **Stability** (growth slows): **branding, process power**.
- Founder implication: at founding, the only Powers available are a business model the incumbent will not copy, or an exclusive asset. Scale and network effects are *earned during takeoff*. Pitching them at origination is a claim about the future.

**Counter-positioning, precisely** [secondary snippet, src 7]
- Definition: "A newcomer adopts a new, superior business model which the incumbent does not mimic due to anticipated damage to their existing business."
- **The incumbent's calculus.** The incumbent asks: "Am I better off staying the course, or adopting the new model?" Counter-positioning covers the cases where the expected damage to the existing business produces a "no." Non-response is often *thoughtful calculation*, not stupidity.
- **Typical incumbent behavior.** "Dabbling": the incumbent puts a toe in the water but will not commit in a way that answers the challenge. Helmer's "Five Stages of Counter-Positioning" are Denial, Ridicule, Fear, Anger, Capitulation ("frequently too late").
- **Scope limit.** Counter-positioning is power *relative to the incumbent only*. It gives no protection against other entrants using the same model. You need a second Power, usually from the takeoff set, before copycat entrants arrive.
- **Vanguard vs active managers (Fidelity).** Low-cost passive index funds. Per Helmer, Fidelity judged that index funds' lower margins would not offset the damage from customers migrating out of its flagship active funds [secondary snippet]. [anecdote]
- **Netflix vs Blockbuster.** DVD by mail with no late fees. Late fees were a material revenue line for Blockbuster; one secondary source reports Blockbuster said it made $800M in late fees in 2000, about 16% of revenue [secondary snippet; not traced to a Blockbuster filing]. Blockbuster did respond: Blockbuster Online and Total Access, plus dropping late fees under CEO Antioco. After board conflict with activist Carl Icahn, the successor CEO Keyes scaled the program back [secondary snippet]. This is the "dabbling, then capitulation" pattern. [anecdote]

## 4. Asymmetric conflict: what the war data says

**Arreguín-Toft (2001)** [research, snippet-only, src 8] (re-check 2026-10-05: article closed access; no repository copy)
- Thesis: outcomes depend on **strategic interaction**. When both sides use the same approach (direct vs direct, indirect vs indirect), the strong actor is favored. When they use opposite approaches, the weak actor is favored.
- Data: asymmetric wars over two centuries, reported as **197 wars** [secondary snippet; the count is still unverified]. Gladwell describes the inclusion threshold as one side at least 10x more powerful in armed might and population [secondary snippet, src 9].
- Strong actors won **76%** of same-approach interactions. Weak actors won **63%** of opposite-approach interactions [snippet-only; still unverified 2026-10-04. One relay of the 2005 book (fs.blog) rounds these to "roughly 80%" and "lose less than 40%", consistent in direction but not exact].
- Trend: strong actors won **88.2%** of the **34** asymmetric conflicts in 1800–1849, **79.5%** in 1850–1899, and only **48.8%** in the last fifty-year period, labelled **1950–1999** [88.2% (34 conflicts) and 48.8% (1950–1999) verified-search: muse.jhu.edu / belfercenter.org-indexed relays, 2026-10-04; 79.5% still snippet-only]. One secondary source gives 65.1% for 1900–1949 [secondary snippet]. The article's period label appears to be 1950–1999, though its data may end in 1998.
- Duration: opposite-approach wars lasted longer, reported as 4.86 vs 2.69 years [secondary snippet]. Mechanism: time lets the weak side's higher resolve wear down the strong side's political will.

**Gladwell (2009 New Yorker essay; 2013 book)** [secondary snippet, src 9]
- Reports that "Goliaths" won **71.5%** of cases. When "Davids" refused to fight on Goliath's terms, their win rate rose from **28.5% to 63.6%**.
- The 63.6% is close to the paper's ~63% opposite-approach figure. Gladwell's framing as the weak side choosing to "amend their strategies" is an interpretation. The paper measures *interaction types*, not deliberate choices.
- **Critiques of using this data for business** (synthesis plus [background, unverified this session]):
  - The rising weak-side win rate is concentrated in 20th-century insurgencies. There, "winning" often means the strong side withdrawing for political reasons, which has no clean business analogue.
  - Alternative explanations exist. Lyall & Wilson (2009) attribute declining counterinsurgency success to mechanization of state militaries, not to weak-side strategy choice (src 10).
  - Use the finding as a **structural analogy only**: do not fight a resource-rich opponent on the dimension where resources convert directly into outcomes.

## 5. How and when incumbents respond

**Goolsbee & Syverson (2008), airlines** [research, src 11; read 2026-10-05: abstract and NBER working-paper PDF]
- Setting: Southwest's route-network expansion. When Southwest begins operating at both endpoint airports of a route, the probability that it enters that route rises sharply. This identifies *threatened* routes before entry.
- Findings (abstract-level): incumbents "cut fares significantly" when threatened. **Over half of Southwest's total impact on incumbent fares occurs before Southwest starts flying.** Cuts happen only on threatened routes, not on routes from competing non-Southwest airports. The abstract adds that "the evidence on whether incumbents are seeking to deter or accommodate entry is mixed." The claim that the largest responses occur on routes that were **concentrated beforehand** is not in the abstract and was not found in the 32-page NBER working paper [read 2026-10-05; corrected: removed from the competing-with-incumbents playbook; the published QJE text was not opened]. In the working paper, seats and flights show no significant change but load factor (passengers per seat) rises significantly when the threat materializes.
- Timing detail from secondary summaries: fares about 7% lower 3–4 quarters before entry, about 10% lower 1–2 quarters before, almost 12% lower when Southwest starts at both ends [secondary snippet]. One snippet's claim of "15–20 percent" pre-entry cuts is inconsistent with these numbers and is not adopted.
- **Founder implications.** (a) Expect incumbents to respond *before you launch*, as soon as your entry becomes credible. (b) The response is targeted at the threatened segment, not market-wide. (c) Price is the first weapon an incumbent reaches for when the entrant is a direct substitute.

**Chandy & Tellis (2000) on fast-following** [research, read in full, src 12]
- They note an argument from the literature (Ali 1994; Conner 1988) that incumbents deliberately wait. Fearing cannibalization, they hold back until an outsider commercializes the innovation, then "rush in" and use marketing and technology resources as fast followers. The authors say their incumbent counts may therefore be "the tip of the iceberg."
- The paper did not test this. Treat it as a hypothesis: in many markets, being first only buys a lead until incumbents copy.

**Consultancy claims** [vendor]
- No Bain or McKinsey figures on incumbent response speed or the "attacker's advantage" could be retrieved and verified this session. None are included. The phrase "attacker's advantage" is associated with Richard Foster's *Innovation: The Attacker's Advantage* (1986, McKinsey). Chandy & Tellis cite Foster (1986) as an S-curve source [src 12, read in full]. Treat any percentages from that tradition as [vendor] until checked.

## 6. Startup tactics, with the evidence behind each

**Start in a narrow segment ("contained fire," beachhead)**
- Graham: "Sometimes the right unscalable trick is to focus on a deliberately narrow market. It's like keeping a fire contained at first to get it really hot before adding more logs." Facebook was Harvard-only at first. "Any startup that could be described as a marketplace usually has to start in a subset of the market." Other startups are usually the best early adopters for B2B [practitioner, read in full, src 15, 2013].
- Graham (2012): "If you have something that no competitor does and that some subset of users urgently need, you have a beachhead." What matters is who is in the beachhead. If it is "people doing something lots more people will be doing in the future," it is probably big enough [practitioner, read in full].
- Moore (1991): concentrate on one pragmatist niche (the "D-Day" beachhead), deliver the *whole product* for it, then expand to adjacent niches ("bowling alley") [background, unverified this session, src 16]. There is no controlled evidence. The case base is tech marketing practice [practitioner].

**Do things that don't scale** (Graham 2013) [practitioner, read in full, src 15]
- "Startups take off because the founders make them take off." Recruit users by hand. Examples: Stripe's "Collison installation" ("Right then, give me your laptop"), Airbnb going door to door in New York, Pinterest recruiting at a design-blogger conference.
- On early fragility: about "30 days of going out and engaging in person with users made the difference between success and failure" for Airbnb [anecdote].
- "You can provide a level of service no big company can" [practitioner].

**Go where the incumbent is structurally unable or unwilling to follow**
- *Turf and cannibalization*: "most big companies have some kind of turf to protect, and this tends to warp their development decisions." Graham's example is Microsoft's ambivalence about web apps threatening the desktop [practitioner, read in full, Graham 2005].
- *Single-bet problem*: "Big companies also lose because they usually only build one of each thing… you can't do anything really risky with it" [practitioner, Graham 2005].
- *Willingness to cannibalize is the variable that matters, not size.* Chandy & Tellis (1998), in a survey of three high-tech industries, found willingness to cannibalize a more powerful driver of radical product innovation than firm size [research, src 13; read 2026-10-05: abstract]. Founder corollary: target incumbents whose *culture and P&L* punish cannibalization, not merely large ones.
- Business-model conflict (Helmer's collateral damage), channel conflict, and margin-structure conflict (Christensen's asymmetric motivation) are the same mechanism viewed from three angles: the incumbent's best response is not to follow.

**Unbundling / rebundling**
- The line "there are only two ways to make money in business: one is to bundle; the other is unbundle" is widely attributed to Jim Barksdale (Netscape CEO), commonly dated to the mid-1990s [background, unverified this session. No primary transcript located; treat the attribution as folklore]. Operational use: attack one high-margin component of an incumbent's bundle where your version is far better (unbundle), then rebundle adjacent jobs once you own the customer.

**Speed.** No peer-reviewed effect size was retrieved. Goolsbee & Syverson cut the other way: incumbents can move *before* the entrant does.

**Thesis about what incumbents overlook**
- Graham (2012): "You have to be able to phrase it in terms of something the incumbents are overlooking. Best of all is when you can say that they didn't have the courage of their convictions" [practitioner, read in full].

## 7. Competition: Thiel vs the "crowded market is a signal" view; Porter for entrants

- **Thiel (2014)** [background, unverified this session, src 17]. Argues that perfect competition drives profits to zero and that durable value comes from monopoly. The monopoly sources he names include proprietary technology, network effects, economies of scale and branding; he advises starting with a small market and dominating it. His WSJ op-ed was titled "Competition Is for Losers." Exact text was not retrieved.
- **Counter-view, Graham (2012)** [practitioner, read in full]: "A crowded market is actually a good sign, because it means both that there's demand and that none of the existing solutions are good enough." "Err on the side of doing things where you'll face competitors… better a good idea with competitors than a bad one without." "It's exceptionally rare for startups to be killed by competitors". The exception is a competitor with lock-in that would prevent users from choosing you.
- **Reconciliation** (synthesis): the two views mostly agree. Thiel's "monopoly" is usually a *small market dominated*, which is Graham's beachhead. Graham's "crowded market" needs a thesis about what the incumbents overlook, which is a route to a differentiated sub-market. Where they truly differ is whether competitor presence is a demand signal (Graham) or a profit warning (Thiel). Graham's own exception, lock-in, matches Helmer's switching costs and network economies.
- **Porter's Five Forces for a new entrant** [background, unverified this session, src 18]. The forces are rivalry, threat of entry, supplier power, buyer power and substitutes. Entry barriers in Porter (2008) include supply-side economies of scale, demand-side benefits of scale (network effects), customer switching costs, capital requirements, incumbency advantages independent of size, unequal access to distribution channels, and restrictive government policy, plus *expected retaliation*. Founder use: rank which barrier protects the incumbent in *your* segment, then pick a model (Section 3) where that barrier does not bind or turns against the incumbent. Goolsbee & Syverson give empirical teeth to "expected retaliation" in concentrated markets.

## 8. Base rates: who actually introduces radical innovations?

**Chandy & Tellis (2000)** [research, read in full, src 12]
- **Sample.** 93 radical innovations were identified in consumer durables and office products. 64 had reliable data on all variables, and 53 of those met the radicalness cutoff (average ≥5 on a 9-point scale across three raters). The earliest product dates from 1851. Radical innovation is defined as "substantially different core technology" plus "substantially higher customer benefits". The radical innovator is the *first commercializer*.
- **Incumbent definition.** A firm that manufactured and sold products in the *preceding* product generation.
- **Overall:** non-incumbents 53%, incumbents 47%. The difference is not significant (p > .40). "The overall results do not support the incumbent's curse."
- **By size:** small and medium firms (<2,500 employees) 58%, large firms 42%.
- **Size × incumbency:** small/medium non-incumbents 42%; large non-incumbents 11%; small/medium incumbents 17%; large incumbents 30%. Size favors incumbents and disfavors new entrants.
- **Over time (the key finding):**
  - Pre-WWII: non-incumbents introduced **73%**, small/medium firms **83%**.
  - Post-WWII: **incumbents 74% vs non-incumbents 26%** (p < .0001); **large firms 74% vs smaller 26%** (p < .0001).
  - In multivariate models, innovations by incumbents were "no less radical" than those by non-incumbents. In recent years, large firms' innovations were *more* radical.
- **Population adjustment.** Large firms never exceeded 0.22% of US manufacturing firms (1909–1972 Census) yet introduced about 42% of the radical innovations.
- **Geography.** The US produced about two-thirds of the sample. The curse is "less prevalent in Western Europe and Japan."
- **Authors' conclusion.** "The incumbent's curse may apply, but to an earlier economic period."
- **Entrant playbooks observed in the sample:** (a) rely on spillovers and off-the-shelf components from resource-rich firms, e.g. MITS's first PC. The authors warn this "leaves the firm vulnerable to imitators." (b) Partner with research institutions and government funding, then buy up key patents, as Haloid/Xerox did with Battelle and a 1948 $120,000 US Signal Corps grant. In the Xerox case, Kodak and other large incumbents turned the technology down first.
- **Limits.** Two categories; n=64; first-commercializer definition only. Do not extrapolate to software or platforms without other evidence.

## Decision rules for a founder (SYNTHESIS, not findings)

1. **Name the incumbent's reason not to follow, in P&L terms.** If you cannot state which existing revenue line or margin the incumbent would destroy by copying you (collateral damage), assume it will copy you, as fast followers often do (src 12 hypothesis, src 11 evidence).
2. **Classify your innovation as sustaining or disruptive honestly.** If it is "better, for the incumbent's best customers," expect a fight you are likely to lose on resources. Only enter that way with a cornered resource or a counter-positioned model.
3. **Test the overshoot assumption.** Only 17 of Christensen's 77 cases showed overshooting (src 4). Interview the incumbent's low-end customers before assuming they are overserved.
4. **Pick the opposite approach.** Do not fight on the dimension where the incumbent's money converts directly into outcomes: price wars in concentrated markets, ad spend, feature parity. Same-approach contests favor the strong (src 8: 76%).
5. **Expect pre-emptive retaliation where you are a direct substitute.** Once your entry is credible, incumbents may cut price on exactly your segment before you launch (src 11). Budget for it, or enter where you are not a direct substitute.
6. **Sequence Powers.** At origination only counter-positioning or a cornered resource is available. Plan which takeoff-phase Power (scale, network, switching costs) will protect you from *other entrants* copying your model, since counter-positioning does not (src 7).
7. **Start with a contained fire.** Choose a beachhead where you can reach critical mass quickly and whose users are where the market is heading (src 15). Do unscalable recruiting and service there.
8. **Do not treat "big incumbent" as "slow incumbent."** Post-WWII, incumbents introduced about 74% of radical innovations in Chandy & Tellis's sample. The variable that matters is willingness to cannibalize (srcs 12, 13). Target incumbents whose incentives, not size, block them.
9. **Competitors are a demand signal, not a death sentence,** unless they hold lock-in your users cannot escape (src 15). Dominate a small market before you widen it. That is where Thiel and Graham agree.
10. **Plan for dabbling, then a late real response.** If you are counter-positioned, expect denial, ridicule, then a token response. Use that window to build a second Power before capitulation turns into real imitation (src 7).
