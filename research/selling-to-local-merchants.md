# Selling to Local Merchants (B2B2C for cafés, restaurants, salons, shops): Research Notes

**Scope.** A startup whose product is used by independent local businesses and by their customers, where the merchant must adopt it before consumers get value (business-to-business-to-consumer, B2B2C). Covers merchant acquisition (field sales, point-of-sale app stores, integrations, trade associations, trials versus commission-only), merchant economics for ordering and delivery platforms (platform fee pages, city fee-cap laws), cold start in one neighbourhood or city, listing merchants before they join and the rules against it, consumer acquisition through maps and in-store prompts, competing with Google, POS vendors and delivery apps, and research on small firms adopting platforms.

Researched 2026-10-05.

## What this means

- **The integration decides the sale.** Toast's founders say their consumer payment app failed because it was too hard to integrate with restaurants' existing systems (9). The FTC's account of Grubhub's unlisted restaurants shows what happens without integration: wrong orders, unpaid food, blame on the restaurant (18). Google will only show an ordering partner with a contract and a merchant-specific orderable menu (23). For a B2B2C product, "works with my POS" is the entry ticket, and the POS vendors control that gate (4, 6–8).
- **POS app stores are gated, slow and paid.** Toast vets applicants and does not accept all of them, then runs certification, a one-week alpha and a several-week beta (4). Lightspeed requires approval and a full demo (8). Clover keeps 30% of app subscriptions and requires payments to run through Fiserv or Clover (6). Square is the most open (any developer can publish after a technical and content review) (1–3). Plan months, not weeks, for Toast and Lightspeed; no vendor publishes review times except Toast's alpha/beta lengths.
- **Field sales is normal for local merchants, even at self-serve companies.** Toast built on local field teams and counts hardware losses as acquisition cost; payback ran 15–30 months (9). Square, the self-serve example, added ~150 field reps in 2025 to win larger sellers (10). No public source gives door-to-door cost per signed merchant or conversion rates for a startup. Evidence is thin.
- **Merchants anchor on 15–30% delivery commissions and 0–7% for pickup or own-channel ordering.** DoorDash charges 6% for pickup and Uber Eats 7%; their first-party ordering costs only payment fees (11–12). Toast Local and Square Go charge no commission (28–29). A pickup or order-ahead product priced above about 5–7% of the order competes against these. In New York City any app arranging same-day pickup needs a licence and is capped at 5% for basic service (13–14).
- **Caps can backfire for the merchants they protect.** In 14 capped places, independents lost 2.5% of orders while chains gained 4.5%, because platforms shifted promotion and raised consumer fees (32). A startup should not build its pitch on fee caps alone.
- **Listing merchants before they agree is now high-risk in the US.** The FTC ordered Grubhub to stop and took $25 million (18); a separate trademark class action settled for about $7.2 million (19); California bars arranging delivery without an agreement and requires removal within three business days (17); New York City bars unauthorised listings (13); Google Maps terms forbid saving business names and addresses from Places and using Places in a directory (20). Facts such as name, address and hours are not copyrightable (21), and naming a business is often nominative fair use (22), but taking orders or implying a partnership is where the cases land.
- **Small merchants try platforms, but returns vary.** In daily deals, only 55–61% of businesses reported a profitable promotion and few deal users returned at full price (35). Delivery platforms help independents mainly through more dine-in visits, and partly replace their own takeout (33). Restaurants also close often (36), so merchant churn is built in.

## Folklore and weak claims

- **"DoorDash listed restaurants without asking, so you can too."** The origin story is real in outline (PDF menus, founders delivering), but no primary source says the restaurants did not agree (39), and the rules have changed since: FTC order, California and NYC laws (17–18, 13).
- **"Integrate once with a POS marketplace and merchants will find you."** No POS vendor publishes install sources; Square's guide says the listing influences conversion, but gives no data (1). Treat listings as credibility and a smoother install, not a lead source. See research/marketplace-and-registry-listings.md for the same pattern in software stores.
- **"Merchants will pay for a free trial if it works."** Delivery apps use 0% introductory periods and "pay nothing if under 20 orders" guarantees (11–12), which suggests merchants resist paying before results. No controlled study compares trial versus commission-only for local merchants.
- **"Trade association endorsement opens doors."** Associations sell supplier memberships with member lists and email blasts (30–31); no data on conversion from these was found.
- **Order-ahead density figures** (such as "X cafés within Y minutes' walk") circulate without sources. No study of minimum density for an order-ahead or queue-time app was found.
