## §1 Platform rules

1. **Apple, App Review Guidelines** (current page). https://developer.apple.com/app-store/review/guidelines/ — [first-party][read 2026-10-05]
   - **5.1.3(i)**: apps "may not use or disclose to third parties data gathered in the health, fitness, and medical research context" (HealthKit, Clinical Health Records, Motion and Fitness, MovementDisorder APIs, research) "for advertising, marketing, or other use-based data mining purposes other than improving health management, or for the purpose of health research, and then only with permission." An app may use health data to give a benefit directly to the user (for example a lower insurance premium) only if submitted by the entity giving the benefit and the data is not shared with a third party. Apps must disclose the specific health data collected.
   - **5.1.3(ii)**: no false or inaccurate data written into HealthKit; no personal health information stored in iCloud.
   - **5.1.2(vi)**: HealthKit data "may not be used for marketing, advertising or use-based data mining, including by third parties."
   - **5.1.2(i)**: apps may not require users to enable system functions (push notifications, location, tracking) to access functionality or receive rewards.
   - **2.5.1**: HealthKit "should be used for health and fitness purposes and integrate with the Health app"; apps should indicate the integration in their description.
   - **1.4.1**: medical apps get greater scrutiny; accuracy claims about health measurements need disclosed data and method; apps that claim to measure blood pressure, temperature, glucose or blood oxygen with phone sensors only are not permitted; apps should remind users to check with a doctor.
2. **Apple Developer Documentation, "Protecting user privacy" (HealthKit)**. https://developer.apple.com/documentation/healthkit/protecting-user-privacy (read via the JSON behind the page) — [first-party][read 2026-10-05]
   - Permission is per data type; "an app isn't aware when the user denies permission to read data. From the app's point of view, no data of that type exists."
   - HealthKit store is encrypted when the device is locked, so an app "may not be able to read data from the store when it runs in the background"; writes are cached.
   - HealthKit may be used only for health or fitness purposes "and this usage is clear in both your marketing text and your user interface."
   - No use of HealthKit data for advertising (ads may still be shown in the app, but not targeted with HealthKit data); no disclosure to a third party without express permission, and even then only to a third party that also provides a health or fitness service; no selling to ad platforms, data brokers or resellers; privacy policy required.
3. **Apple Developer, "Health and fitness"** hub. https://developer.apple.com/health-fitness/ — WorkoutKit lets apps create interval workouts and, with permission, sync them to the Apple Watch Workout app, where they appear with the app's name and icon; restates "may not be used for advertising, marketing, or other use-based data mining purposes, or sold to data brokers." [first-party][read 2026-10-05]
4. **Google Play Console Help, "Android Health Permissions: Guidance and FAQs"** (Health Connect). https://support.google.com/googleplay/android-developer/answer/12991134 — [first-party][read 2026-10-05; no last-updated date shown]
   - Approved use cases include fitness, wellness and coaching; rewards apps ("encourage users to adopt and maintain healthy habits ... in exchange for financial rewards"); corporate wellness; research with ethics approval; and **health-integrated games**: "Games where gameplay mechanics, character progression, or in-game rewards are directly influenced by a user's real-world physical activity or wellness data."
   - Prohibited: "Transferring or selling user health or fitness data to third parties like advertising platforms, data brokers, or any information resellers" and "using user health and fitness data for serving ads, including personalized or interest-based advertising."
   - Each permission needs a justification in Play Console explaining how the data benefits the user; request the minimum data types. Privacy policy must be reachable from the app and the store listing and say what health data is collected. Background-read and history-read permissions are named but their rules were not detailed on the page.
5. **Google Play Console Help, "Health apps declaration"**. https://support.google.com/googleplay/android-developer/answer/14738291 — Announced April 2024; "After August 31, 2024, all apps will be required to have completed an accurate Health apps declaration", including apps with no health features (they certify none) and apps in testing tracks. Categories: Fitness & Wellness (activity and fitness tracking, nutrition and weight management, sleep, stress) and Medical. [first-party][read 2026-10-05]
6. **Google Play Console Help, "Health Content and Services"** policy. https://support.google.com/googleplay/android-developer/answer/16679511 — Health and medical apps must complete the Health apps declaration and post a privacy policy. Apps regulated as medical devices must be declared and give proof of clearance on request; "Other health and medical apps must include a clear disclaimer in their app description indicating that the app is 'not a medical device and does not diagnose, treat, cure, or prevent any medical condition'" and remind users to consult a healthcare professional. This sits in the section on medical functionality; whether a pure step-counting game must carry it is not stated. Health Connect data is personal and sensitive user data. [first-party][read 2026-10-05 via full page text]

"Strava Data provided by a specific user can only be displayed or disclosed in your Developer Application to that user. Strava Data related to other users, even if such data is publicly viewable on the Strava Platform, may not be displayed or disclosed." ## §2 US law: health data and claims

10. **FTC, "Complying with FTC's Health Breach Notification Rule"** (July 2024, edited January 2025). https://www.ftc.gov/business-guidance/resources/complying-ftcs-health-breach-notification-rule-0 — A vendor of personal health records offers a record of identifiable health information that "has the technical capacity to draw information from multiple sources." The FTC's example: a fitness app that collects height, weight and age and syncs with wearable trackers likely qualifies. A fitness-tracker company sending data to a health app can be a "PHR related entity". A breach "is not limited to cybersecurity intrusions" and includes sharing without the person's authorisation. Notify affected people within 60 calendar days; FTC also (within 60 days if 500+ people, else yearly); media if 500+ in one state. Civil penalty up to $53,088 per violation. [first-party][read 2026-10-05]
11. **FTC, Flo Health** (complaint January 2021; order finalised 22 June 2021). https://www.ftc.gov/news-events/news/press-releases/2021/06/ftc-finalizes-order-flo-health-fertility-tracking-app-shared-sensitive-health-data-facebook-google — Period and fertility app with more than 100 million users shared health data with marketing and analytics firms including Facebook and Google despite privacy promises; order requires affirmative consent before sharing health data, notice to users, and an independent privacy review. No money penalty in this order. [first-party][snippet-only: FTC search results; release not opened]
12. **FTC, "Health Products Compliance Guidance"** (December 2022; replaces the 1998 dietary supplement guide). https://www.ftc.gov/business-guidance/resources/health-products-compliance-guidance — Covers health-related apps. "Competent and reliable scientific evidence" means studies done and evaluated objectively by qualified experts, using methods generally accepted to give accurate results. "As a general matter, substantiation of health-related benefits will need to be in the form of randomized, controlled human clinical testing." Testimonials with results more dramatic than users can generally expect are likely deceptive, and "Results not typical" does not cure that. [first-party][read 2026-10-05]
13. **FTC, Lumos Labs (Lumosity)**, January 2016. https://www.ftc.gov/news-events/news/press-releases/2016/01/lumosity-pay-2-million-settle-ftc-deceptive-advertising-charges-its-brain-training-program ; blog https://www.ftc.gov/business-guidance/blog/2016/01/mind-gap-what-lumosity-promised-vs-what-it-could-prove — $2M redress ($50M judgment suspended) for claims that brain-training games improve work, school and athletic performance and delay cognitive decline. The FTC blog asks whether the evidence shows a real-world benefit "Or does it just show that the person got better at playing the game?" Testimonials solicited through contests with prizes (iPad, lifetime subscription, trip) were not disclosed. Subscribers had to be offered an easy way to cancel auto-renewal. [first-party][blog read 2026-10-05; release snippet-only]
14. **FTC, Reebok EasyTone**, September 2011. https://www.ftc.gov/news-events/news/press-releases/2011/09/reebok-pay-25-million-customer-refunds-settle-ftc-charges-deceptive-advertising-easytone-runtone — $25M refunds for claims that toning shoes gave 28% more buttock strength and tone than regular walking shoes. [first-party][snippet-only]
15. **FTC, "Gut Check: A Reference Guide for Media on Spotting False Weight Loss Claims"** (January 2014). https://www.ftc.gov/business-guidance/resources/gut-check-reference-guide-media-spotting-false-weight-loss-claims — Seven claims that are always false, including weight loss of two pounds or more a week for a month or more without diet or exercise, permanent weight loss after stopping use, substantial weight loss for all users, and losing more than three pounds a week for more than four weeks safely. 16. **FDA, "General Wellness: Policy for Low Risk Devices"**, final guidance, January 2026 (supersedes September 2019). https://www.fda.gov/regulatory-information/search-fda-guidance-documents/general-wellness-policy-low-risk-devices — Low-risk products for general wellness (maintaining or encouraging a general state of health, such as weight management or physical fitness, without reference to a disease) are outside active FDA device oversight; claims tied to diagnosing, treating or preventing a disease move a product toward device rules. ## §4 What drives use

22. **Mazeas, A. et al., "Evaluating the Effectiveness of Gamification on Physical Activity: Systematic Review and Meta-analysis of Randomized Controlled Trials"**, *JMIR* 24(1):e26779, 2022. https://doi.org/10.2196/26779 — 16 RCTs, 2,407 participants, 2010–2020. Gamified interventions raised physical activity, Hedges g=0.42 (small to medium); g=0.23 against active non-gamified programmes; at follow-up (about 14 weeks after the end) g=0.15. ## What this means

- **The data rules are a product constraint before they are a legal one.** Apple and Google both forbid using HealthKit or Health Connect data for advertising, and both forbid selling or passing it to ad platforms. A conversion event such as "workout completed" or "10,000 steps" sent to an ad network is health data leaving the app. Google explicitly lists games whose progress depends on real activity as an approved Health Connect use, so a fitness game is a recognised category, not a grey area. (1, 2, 4)
- **Under US law, a fitness app that syncs with a wearable is probably a "vendor of personal health records".** Sharing health data through an ad pixel or SDK without authorisation is a reportable breach, with penalties per violation. (10; see privacy-and-marketing-law note §3)
- **Claims need evidence close to an RCT for health benefits.** "Burn fat", "lose X kg" or "improves your heart" need clinical evidence the founder almost never has. Activity and fun claims ("count your steps", "turn walks into quests") are wellness claims and stay outside FDA device rules. Lumosity shows that "users got better at the game" does not prove a real-world benefit. (12, 13, 15, 16)
- **January is real but short.** Searches for "diet" jump 82% at the start of a new year, and gym visits rise at every fresh start (week, month, semester, birthday), then decline through each period. January 2025 downloads were the highest since January 2022 (Sensor Tower). Weekly and monthly fresh starts are cheaper to use than one yearly spike. (17, 20)
- **Most motivational effects fade when the push stops.** Gamification works (g=0.42) but shrinks to g=0.15 at follow-up; Pokémon GO's step gain was gone in six weeks in one study; only 8% of 54 gym programmes still worked after four weeks; only 23% of active-game interventions lasted. For an app this means engagement must come from ongoing content and social ties, not a one-time novelty. (18, 22, 27, 29, 30)
- **Competition and visible peers beat messages.** In the best trial evidence, competition was the only social design whose effect outlasted the game; anonymous peers beat promotional messages. Exercise spreads through friends. (23, 25, 26)
- **Fitness games reach less-active people.** Pokémon GO reached low-activity users, while leading health apps drew mostly active people. Wellness programmes and health apps tend to enrol people already healthy. (28, 34)
- **Health & Fitness is the strongest subscription category in RevenueCat's data**, with annual plans carrying most revenue but only a quarter renewing. (36, 37)
- **Strava is a distribution partner with tight terms.** Since June 2026: no leaderboards from other users' Strava data above 9,999 athletes, a 7-day cache, no analytics or AI on the data, and no charging for Strava-derived features. Build on Apple Health and Health Connect first. (7, 8)

## Folklore and weak claims

- **"Fitness apps lose 70–80% of users in 30 days" / day-30 retention of 3% / 5% / 8–12%.** Seen on many blogs (UXCam, Business of Apps citing "2023", "AppsFlyer 2025 State of App Marketing" quoted secondhand). The primary pages could not be read (403/429), and the figures differ by source. Use the Adjust D1 figure already in the repo and your own cohorts. [snippet-only]
- **"Quitter's Day" (second Friday of January).** Attributed to a 2019 Strava analysis of 31.5 million January activities. No Strava primary page found this session; repeated by many blogs, with "80% abandon resolutions" attached without a source. The direction fits the fresh-start data; the date does not have a published method. [snippet-only]
- **"Gamification is just novelty."** The meta-analysis finds a smaller effect remains about 14 weeks later (g=0.15), so "just novelty" is too strong, but "lasts" is also too strong. (22)
- **"A wearable will help users lose weight."** The IDEA trial found less weight loss with a wearable added. (32)
- **"Corporate wellness pays for itself."** Two large randomised trials found no effect on medical spending in 18 months or the first year. Employers may still buy for engagement or benefits reasons. (33, 34)
- **"Summer is the second peak."** No primary source found with a measured summer peak for app downloads or subscriptions. Pre-summer "get fit" messaging is common practice, not measured here.
