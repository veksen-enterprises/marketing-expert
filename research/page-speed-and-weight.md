# Page speed and page weight: what causal evidence says, and what one fetch can check (checked 2026-10-27)

Question: does causal evidence (delay-injection experiments, A/B tests) give an effect size for page speed on conversion or engagement that would justify a threshold in `audit_page`? Which numbers can the tool measure from one fetch (HTML bytes; total bytes, request count and LCP in render mode) and are they meaningful next to field Core Web Vitals? Where does the folklore ("53% of mobile visits leave after 3 seconds", "Amazon: 100 ms costs 1% of sales") come from? Today `audit_page` only warns when HTML is over 5 MB (`src/lib/pageAudit.ts`, `TOO_LARGE`), and the Speed section of `knowledge/landing-pages-and-cro.md` cites Core Web Vitals thresholds and Deloitte 2020.

Everything else was read through WebFetch (a model's summary; marked "fetch summary") or search snippets. ## 1. Delay-injection experiments: speed matters, roughly linearly, with small effects per 100 ms

- **Google search, 2009.** Server delay injected for one group of users against a control. Daily searches per user: 100 ms pre-header −0.20%; 200 ms post-header −0.29%; 400 ms post-header −0.59%; 200 ms after the ads −0.30%. Over the 6-week arms the effect grew: −0.22% and −0.44% in the first half, −0.36% and −0.74% in the second half (200 and 400 ms). Five weeks after the 400 ms delay was removed, the group was still −0.21%. [first-party; delay-injection A/B; search; sample size not stated, 1]
- **Bing.** "A slowdown experiment at Bing slowed 10% of users by 100ms, and another 10% by 250ms for two weeks." Result: "every 100ms speedup improves revenue by 0.6%"; 250 ms at the server cost about 1.5% of revenue. The authors say a linear approximation "is very reasonable for Bing" and that they assume the effect of a slowdown mirrors a speedup. [research; delay-injection A/B; Bing search pages; 2, secondary summary]
- The same team's book: in 2017 "every tenth of a second improvement for Bing was worth $18 million in incremental annual revenue", and it recommends "latency as a guardrail metric" (a metric every test must not harm). [research/practitioner, 3, abstract]
- **Financial Times, 2016.** A blocking CSS call added 1, 2 or 3 seconds on top of a baseline of about 2 seconds; each test ran about four weeks. Articles read over 7 days: −4.9% at 1 s, −4.4% at 2 s, −7.2% at 3 s; at 28 days, −5.0% (2 s) and −7.9% (3 s). An earlier 5-second arm was also run. Results under 95% significance were left out of the tables; the post does not say users were randomised or how many there were. Mobile users were "largely unaffected" at 1 and 2 s over 7 days. [first-party; delay test; one publisher, 5]
- **Amazon, "100 ms costs 1% of sales."** It traces to Greg Linden, a former Amazon engineer, describing internal tests from about 2006 on his blog. Kohavi's papers repeat it "as shared by Greg Linden". No document, sample size or test length has been published. [practitioner; unpublished, second-hand, 2, 4]. Treat it as an anecdote, not a measurement.
- **What these share.** All are very large, very fast, high-traffic sites (search engines, a news site, Amazon), where the measured outcome is repeat use. The effects per 100 ms are small (0.2% to 1%) and are detectable only with millions of users. None tests a page that a founder's small site serves. [derived from 1–5]
- **No threshold.** None of these finds a cliff (a point where losses jump). Bing's effect is close to linear; Google's and the FT's grow with the size of the delay. The causal evidence supports "slower is worse, a little per 100 ms", not "under X seconds is safe". [derived from 1, 2, 5]
- No peer-reviewed randomised experiment on an e-commerce or landing page was found. One Springer chapter on load time and conversion on an e-commerce platform could not be read (see Not fetched). [search, 2026-10-27]

## 2. A/B tests of real speed work: one case, bundled changes

- **Vodafone (2021).** One landing page, paid traffic split 50/50, about 34K visits a day per version. Version A had 31% better field LCP and gave 8% more sales, a 15% better lead-to-visit rate and an 11% better cart-to-visit rate. The versions looked the same, but A also moved a widget to server-side rendering and resized and lazy-loaded images. Test length and confidence intervals are not given. [first-party case study, published by Google; one page, 7]. It shows speed work can pay; it does not isolate speed from rendering changes, and it is one selected success.
- **Zalando (2018).** "100 msec loading time improvement led to a 0.7% uplift in revenue per session." The post describes a correlation analysis "confirmed" by an A/B test, but gives no design, metric definition or sample. [first-party; design not stated, 6]
- **Etsy (2014).** Adding 160 KB of images to the mobile page raised bounce rate 12%. Only a second-hand one-line summary was found; design unknown. [first-party anecdote, second-hand, 22]. This is the only page-weight (rather than delay) result found, and it is too thin to carry a byte threshold.

## 3. Large observational studies: weaker than they look

- **Deloitte "Milliseconds Make Millions" (2020): method.** Speed came from Google Lighthouse, a tool that "pulls simulated lab data", run hourly for about 4 weeks (28 Oct–24 Nov 2019). Speed changes "all occurred naturally and were not artificially created". The "0.1 second improvement was the cumulative impact of all four speed metrics" (server latency, first meaningful paint, estimated input latency and a load metric). A log model was fitted; only results at 95% significance were reported, and the rest "were not included in the report". Desktop results were dropped because of "a lot of contradicting parameters". [vendor-commissioned; observational; 37 of 70+ brands approached, 8]
- So the +8.4% retail conversion per 0.1 s already in the playbook comes from lab measurements of natural hour-to-hour swings, a composite speed number, reporting of significant results only, and a dropped desktop analysis. Slow hours may also be busy hours (heavy traffic slows servers and brings different shoppers), which the summary does not say was controlled. It is not causal and should not size a threshold. [derived from 8]
- **"Users are 24% less likely to abandon page loads"** when a site meets the Core Web Vitals thresholds (22% for news, 24% for shopping), from "millions of page impressions". Abandonment here means leaving before the page finishes loading. Method not published. [first-party; observational, 11]
- **"53% of mobile visits leave after 3 seconds."** First form, Sept 2016: "53% of visits are likely to be abandoned if pages take longer than 3 seconds to load." Footnote: "Google Data, Aggregated, anonymized Google Analytics data from a sample of mWeb sites opted into sharing benchmark data", n=3.7K (sites, not visits), global, March 2016. "Abandoned" is not defined, and how load time was measured is not stated. [vendor, 9]. By Feb 2017 the wording became "53% of mobile site visitors leave a page that takes longer than three seconds to load", same footnote. [vendor, 10]. The claim changed from "likely to be abandoned" to "leave" without new data. Verdict: a descriptive figure from an opt-in sample of 3,700 sites, method unpublished. It is not a threshold. [derived from 9, 10]
- **"Bounce probability rises 113% from 1 s to 7 s"** (Google/SOASTA 2017) comes from "a deep neural network" trained on bounce and conversion data, with "90% prediction accuracy"; sample and data source are not stated. [vendor; model output, 10]. The often-quoted 32% (1→3 s) and 90% (1→5 s) figures were not in the document read.
- **Lab tolerance is much higher than the folklore.** In a lab test of 30 people using mobile search, ratings of the experience fell significantly only at the two highest latencies (8.65 s and 12.98 s); the authors put the tolerance at 7–10 seconds. Self-reported ratings, not behaviour, and the summary flags an error in the paper's odds wording. [research; lab, n=30; mobile search; 13]. An earlier study found added delays become noticeable above a threshold, that users of a fast system notice delays more, and, in Yahoo logs, that users click more on identical result pages served faster. [research; lab plus log analysis; 12, abstract]

## 4. Where the Core Web Vitals thresholds come from

- LCP "good" at 2.5 s was not set from conversion data. Google took the human-factors range of "roughly 0.3 to 3 seconds" for a response to feel immediate (Card et al.; Miller; Newell), narrowed it to 1–3 s because LCP comes after first contentful paint, and then picked a value that at least 10% of origins in CrUX (Chrome's field dataset) already meet. [first-party, 16]
- So the thresholds rest on perception research plus achievability. They are reasonable targets, but no study shows that a page at 2.4 s converts better than one at 2.6 s. [derived from 16 and §1]

## 5. Reference numbers: the median home page (HTTP Archive, July 2025)

HTTP Archive loads millions of pages once each in a lab (Chrome via WebPageTest). Page weight is bytes transferred over the network. [descriptive, 14]

| Home pages, July 2025 | Mobile | Desktop |
|---|---|---|
| Total bytes, median | 2,559 KB | 2,862 KB |
| Requests, median | 75 | 80 |
| Requests, 90th percentile | 187 | 192 |
| HTML, median / 90th percentile | 33 KB / 155 KB | 35 KB / 154 KB |
| JavaScript, median | 632 KB | 697 KB |
| Images, median | 911 KB | 1,058 KB |

- Home and inner pages together: total bytes at the 90th percentile 8,337 KB mobile and 9,179 KB desktop; home pages alone (both devices) 11,406 KB at the 90th percentile and 2,710 KB at the median. [descriptive, 14]
- The chapter is not fully consistent: its headline stat says "2,164 KB, the median weight of a mobile home page", which matches the chart for all mobile pages, not home pages; its text gives 2,362 KB and 2.6 MB in different places. Use the chart values above and say "about 2.5 MB". [14, read]
- "HTML" in these figures is the sum of all HTML responses on the page (the median page loads 3 HTML files, so iframes count). Whether these are compressed or decoded sizes was not confirmed from the query. [14, SQL read]
- Home pages are heavier than inner pages, mainly from images (911 KB vs 354 KB on mobile), and are about 45.8% heavier on average. [descriptive, 14]
- Field status (CrUX, 2025): 48% of mobile and 56% of desktop websites have good Core Web Vitals; at page level, 45% of mobile home pages pass, against 56% of mobile inner pages. 62% of mobile sites have good LCP, 13% poor. [descriptive, 15]

## 6. Lab numbers from one run mislead; field data usually doesn't exist for small sites

- Lighthouse's own docs: scores change "even if there hasn't been a code change". Sources of variance include page nondeterminism, network, server load, hardware and other processes on the machine. "The median Lighthouse score of 5 runs is twice as stable as 1 run"; set thresholds on medians or percentiles of several runs, "instead of single test results". Avoid shared or burstable servers. [first-party, 18]
- Lab LCP differs from field LCP because of device and viewport, network, cache (the lab loads cold; returning users have files cached), personalisation, user interaction and the back/forward cache. Google: "field data is what you should use to prioritize your efforts." [first-party, 17]
- Lab and field disagree at scale. Across 7.3M mobile URLs, the Lighthouse score correlated with the share of good field LCP at 0.49, "not very strongly". [first-party analysis, 19]. Of pages scoring 90+ in Lighthouse, 43% failed at least one Core Web Vital in the field (29% among pages with a viewport tag). [first-party analysis; Sept 2021; FID era, 20]
- In 2025 lab and field moved in opposite directions: median mobile Total Blocking Time (lab) rose 58% to 1,916 ms while field INP improved. [descriptive, 15]
- **Field data (CrUX) needs traffic.** A page or origin enters CrUX only if it is publicly discoverable and "sufficiently popular"; the visitor threshold is not disclosed. Only Chrome users on desktop and Android who sync history and share usage statistics are counted; iOS is excluded. The CrUX API needs a Google Cloud API key, allows 150 queries a minute, and returns 28-day p75 values by phone, tablet and desktop. [first-party, 21]. Most founders' new sites will have no CrUX data.
- `audit_page` render mode is weaker than Lighthouse for timing: one unthrottled run of headless Chromium, desktop-sized by default, from wherever the MCP runs, with every request sent through a local guard proxy that adds its own delay (`src/lib/pageAudit.ts`, render mode). A render-mode LCP is not comparable to the 2.5 s field threshold. [derived from code and 17, 18]

## What this changes

1. **No speed threshold from conversion evidence.** The causal studies show a small, roughly linear loss per 100 ms on very large sites (Bing 0.6% revenue, Google 0.2–0.6% searches), and no cliff. Nothing justifies a "flag above N seconds" rule on conversion grounds. Grade: [research; delay-injection A/B; search engines and one publisher].
2. **`audit_page` fetch mode: report HTML size, compare with the web, flag only far outliers.**
   - Input: the bytes read (already counted by `readCapped`), plus `Content-Length` and `Content-Encoding` when present.
   - Output fields: `htmlBytes` (decoded) and `htmlTransferBytes` (only when the header gives it; otherwise `null` and say "compressed size not known").
   - Rule: info flag when decoded HTML is over 1 MB: "The HTML is N KB before compression. 9 in 10 home pages send at most about 155 KB of HTML (HTTP Archive, July 2025). Large HTML usually means inlined data or scripts. No study links HTML size to conversion; this is a comparison with other sites, not a measured cost." The 1 MB cut-off is the project's choice: it sits well above the 155 KB 90th percentile even if those figures are compressed sizes, so it won't fire on normal pages. Keep the 5 MB warning, which is about the audit being partial.
   - Must say it did not check: images, scripts and other files (fetch mode reads the HTML only), and real-user speed.
3. **`audit_page` render mode: report totals, don't flag timing.**
   - Count requests and transfer bytes through the existing local proxy (it already sees every request). New fields: `requests`, `transferBytes`, both labelled "one load, cold cache".
   - Info flag only above the HTTP Archive 90th percentile for home pages: requests > 190 or total bytes > 11 MB: "This page loads more than 9 in 10 home pages (HTTP Archive, July 2025)…". Same "comparison, not a measured cost" wording.
   - LCP: if added, report it as `labLcpMs` with the note "one unthrottled desktop run through a proxy; not comparable to Google's 2.5 s field threshold; use PageSpeed Insights or CrUX for real users". No flag. Reason: Lighthouse's own docs say single runs are unstable, and 43% of pages with a 90+ lab score failed in the field [18, 20].
4. **Field data as an optional source.** A check that queries CrUX (or the PageSpeed Insights API, which serves CrUX) could give real p75 LCP, INP and CLS by device and compare them with the published thresholds. It needs an API key, and most small sites will return no data, so the tool must say "no field data: the site has too little Chrome traffic" rather than stay silent. That would be a new fetch target and needs the security look `CLAUDE.md` requires.
5. **Playbook `knowledge/landing-pages-and-cro.md`, Speed section.**
   - Add a causal line with its own tag: "Delay-injection tests at Bing and Google found small, steady losses per 100 ms (Bing: 0.6% of revenue per 100 ms) [research; delay-injection A/B; search engines]." The current section tag `[first-party / vendor]` then becomes mixed; tag each line.
   - Change the Deloitte line to say the speed data was lab data from natural hour-to-hour swings, and that only significant results were reported. Keep "associated with".
   - Add folklore lines: "53% leave after 3 s" is from an opt-in sample of 3,700 sites with an unpublished method [vendor; rule-of-thumb]; "Amazon: 100 ms = 1% of sales" was never published [rule-of-thumb].
   - Add: the 2.5 s LCP threshold comes from perception research and what 10% of sites achieve, not from conversion data.
   - Keep "the best evidence for your site is a delay-injection test", and add that it needs very large traffic to detect effects of this size, so a small site should fix clear outliers and not try to measure the effect.

## Open questions

- The Bing figures come from a secondary summary; the KDD 2014 PDF and Kohavi's 2013 KDD paper were blocked. Re-read them (and check whether Bing found smaller effects for delays to content lower on the page) before quoting exact wording in a playbook.
- Are HTTP Archive's HTML byte figures compressed or decoded sizes? Check `bytesHtml` in the HTTP Archive schema docs. This decides whether a lower HTML cut-off is fair.
- Does any peer-reviewed field experiment randomise page speed on an e-commerce or landing page? None was found; the Springer chapter could not be read.
- How often do small, founder-stage sites have CrUX data at all? A sample of the evaluation cases' domains through the CrUX API would answer it.
- Does a heavy page (bytes or requests) predict poor field LCP among pages that have CrUX data? HTTP Archive has both per URL; a correlation by page weight decile would show whether byte flags point at real user pain.
