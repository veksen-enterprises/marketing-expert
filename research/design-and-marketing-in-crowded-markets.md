# Design and Marketing in Crowded, AI-Built Categories — Research Notes

Scope: whether cheap AI-assisted building has flooded app and software categories, whether outcomes are concentrating, and what the evidence says about design and marketing as the factors that separate winners. Dating apps are the test case for the rival claim that liquidity (enough active users nearby), not design, decides who wins. Researched 2026-10-27.

## 1. Supply: is building cheaper flooding categories?

Yes, on every count found. No source proves AI tools caused it; the timing fits.

- **iOS App Store new releases:** 1M+ (2016) → 424K (2023, lowest since 2012) → 448K (2024) → **557K (2025, +24%)** [1] [vendor, estimated].
- **Both stores, Q1 2026: +60% YoY; iOS alone +80%.** April 2026 to date: +104% across both stores. Productivity entered the top five release categories; Utilities rose to No. 2 [2] [vendor via press].
- **Subscription apps on RevenueCat:** monthly new launches rose from about 2,000 (early 2022) to **over 14,700 (January 2026)**, about 7× [3] [vendor]. Sample is apps using RevenueCat, so part of the rise may be RevenueCat's own growth.
- **Product Hunt:** about 800–1,600 launches a month for most of its history, then **2,900–4,900 a month from about February 2024** [4] [practitioner; method not given]. Share of launches featured on the home page reportedly fell from 60–98% (2020–2023) to about 10% (September 2024) [5] [secondary; original Tetriz.io analysis not found].
- **Steam (long-run analog, cheap engines rather than AI):** 1,713 new titles in 2014, 18,602 in 2024 (SteamDB, via [6]). About 20% of 2025 releases disclosed generative-AI use [6] [vendor via press].

## 2. Outcomes: are they concentrating?

Yes. The pattern is "far more entrants, roughly fixed attention, falling median".

- **New app cohorts earn little.** Apps launched before 2020 make 69% of subscription revenue; apps launched in 2025 or later make about 3% [3].
- **Median app one year after launch earns about $72 a month; the top 10% earn $2,500+.** Top 10% grew MRR 306% YoY, bottom 25% shrank 33% [3].
- **Share of new apps reaching $10K MRR within two years fell from 5.3% to 4.6%**; reaching $1K fell from 19% to 17% [3]. Caveat: with 7× more entrants, a falling share is expected even if the count of winners rose. The report does not give the count.
- **AI-powered apps earn 41% more per payer but churn 30% faster** [3]. Read: novelty sells the first month; it does not keep users.
- **Steam:** 1% of games earn 84.6% of revenue, 10% earn 98.8% [7]. Median revenue for 2026 releases is $1,728, against about $15,000 for 2016 (that older figure may be a mean) [7]. In 2025, 40% of 12,732 tracked releases earned under the $100 listing fee [6]. Both are estimates.

**Mechanism** [synthesis]: when building cost falls, supply rises until the scarce input is attention (store rank, feed slots, Product Hunt home page, a buyer's shortlist). Products then compete on getting noticed and on being trusted at first glance. That is marketing and visible design. This is consistent with the data above, but no source here tests it directly.

## 3. Design: what the evidence shows

Strong lab evidence that looks drive first judgments of quality. One good field study that visual quality moves demand. **No study found that shows visual design quality predicts which apps win in an AI-era crowded category.** That gap is the main weakness of the thesis.

- **First impressions are fast and stable.** Visual appeal is judged in 50 ms, and 50 ms ratings agree with 500 ms ratings [9] [research; secondary].
- **Simple, familiar layouts win the first impression.** Low visual complexity and high prototypicality (looking like what people expect for that kind of site) were rated most appealing; both act within 50 ms, complexity as early as 17 ms [10] [research; abstract]. Implication: looking generic is not the failure. Looking cluttered or unfamiliar is.
- **Looks override actual usability in users' judgments.** In a simulated ATM (n=124), the aesthetics level changed post-use ratings of usability; deliberately broken usability (delays, dead buttons) did not, though it slowed task times [11] [research; lab, students, one interface]. So users blame ugly products for being hard to use, and forgive pretty ones.
- **Credibility comments centre on looks.** Of 2,684 people judging live sites, "design look" was the most-mentioned feature, in 46.1% of comments [12] [research report, 2002]. This counts what people wrote, not what drove their judgment.
- **Field evidence: better photos raise bookings.** Airbnb listings with verified professional photos had **8.98% higher occupancy** than those with host photos (difference-in-differences, 7,423 properties, 16 months) [13] [research; abstract]. This is the closest causal evidence that visual quality moves demand in a crowded marketplace. It is about listing images, not product UI.
- **Firm-level "design pays" claims are correlational and vendor-made.** McKinsey's top-quartile design scorers had 32 points higher revenue growth and 56 points higher shareholder return growth over five years (300 listed firms) [14] [vendor; correlation only, as McKinsey itself says]. Do not cite it as proof design causes growth.
- **AI UI generation is weak on design.** Apple researchers: "most LLMs are unable to reliably generate well-designed UIs" [8] [research WP]. They cite earlier work (UICoder) showing "numerous design-related errors" after training; they give no measure of how often. This supports JP's observation but does not measure the share of shipped AI-built apps with bad UX. That share is **unmeasured**.

**What "design" means in the cases that moved markets** [synthesis from §4]: Hinge, Bumble and eHarmony differentiated through product rules (prompts instead of swipes, women message first, a short list of matches), not visual polish. The evidence supports "design decisions about how the product works" more strongly than "the product looks better".

## 4. Marketing and distribution

The repo already holds the main evidence: distribution decides outcomes when a rival can bundle (Teams vs Slack, IE vs Navigator; `competition-and-defensibility.md` §6), and smaller brands have fewer buyers who are slightly less loyal (double jeopardy; `brand-growth-channels.md`). New here:

- **Being first rarely wins.** In 500 brands across 50 categories, almost half of market pioneers failed; lasting leaders entered on average 13 years after the pioneer [15] [research; secondary]. In a crowded category, execution and marketing after entry matter more than timing.
- **Launch platforms saturate.** Product Hunt's featured share reportedly fell to about 10% as launch volume tripled [4][5] [unverified]. A launch-day channel that worked in 2022 now reaches a fraction of the audience per launch.

## 5. Test case: dating apps — liquidity or design?

Both, at different stages. Liquidity decides whether an app survives. Product design and positioning decide which liquid app grows.

- **Theory: smaller platforms survive by design choice, not size.** A matching platform can win by showing fewer candidates and charging more. Users with weak outside options prefer less competition, so a restricted platform and a large one coexist without the market tipping [16] [research; eHarmony (7 matches, 25%+ higher price) vs Match]. Size is not destiny when the design choice sorts users.
- **Hinge: a product redesign inside a liquid market.**
  - In 2016 Hinge was "just another swiping app"; it removed swiping and built around prompts. Its like rate fell from 56% to 10% while dates per user rose [20] [founder's account, unverified figures].
  - About $5.2M worldwide revenue in 2018 (Sensor Tower) [19]; **$167.5M direct revenue in Q2 2025, +25.4% YoY**, while Tinder fell 3.9% to $461.2M [17] [first-party]. Hinge revenue +22% and MAU +13% in Q2 2026; Tinder DAU still down 4% [18] [first-party].
  - Confound: Match took 51% in June 2018 and 100% by early 2019 [19]. Hinge's growth ran on Match's money, marketing and know-how. This case does not separate design from distribution.
- **Downloads are spread out; money is not.** The top 10 dating apps took only 31.6% of downloads among the top 500 in 2025 [21] [vendor]. Many small apps get installs; revenue sits with a few (Match Group alone reported $840M direct revenue in Q2 2026 [18]). Installs are cheap; staying long enough to find a match needs density.
- **Design differentiation fades.** Bumble's women-first rule built a brand; its downloads fell 19% in 2025 while Hinge rose 25.4% [21] [vendor].
- **New entrants launch city by city.** Sitch (AI matchmaking, $6.7M raised) runs in three cities with "tens of thousands" of users [22] [trade press]. The source does not state why; city-by-city launches fit the liquidity view (density per city matters, not total users). Unchecked.

**Verdict** [synthesis]: the coordinator's claim holds for survival (an empty local pool kills any design). JP's claim holds for share shifts among apps that already have users, but the best case (Hinge) is confounded by Match's backing. For a founder, liquidity is the outcome; marketing that concentrates users in one place, and a product rule that attracts one group, are how you get it. The repo's `liquidity_math` tool already covers the density side.

## 6. What this implies for the MCP

1. **Detect crowding, then name the lever.** Signals a tool can check: count of lookalike competitors the founder lists or that a search finds; whether the category grew fast in release counts [1][2]; whether the core feature is easy to build. When crowded, the advisor should say that build quality alone will not separate the product, and that first-impression design, store assets and distribution are the levers [9][10][13]. It should not grade the design.
2. **Point to cheap human checks, never an AI verdict.** Each maps to a finding above:
   - *5-second test:* show the home page or first screen to 5 target users for 5 seconds; ask what it is and whether they would trust it [9][10].
   - *Side-by-side:* put the product's first screen next to the top 3 competitors'; ask 5 target users which they would try first and why [10][11].
   - *Correction (2026-10-27):* a pick between pages does not predict which page converts: judged picks matched live winners about as often as chance (`preference-vs-live-results.md`). Use the side-by-side only to find a page people misread or pass over. Drop "whether they would trust it" from the 5-person test: glance judgements of credibility are less consistent than of appeal (`design-lever-checks.md` item 6).
   - *Asset swap:* when listing images or store screenshots are home-made, test professional ones, since that is where field evidence of a demand effect exists [13].
   - *Paid design review:* a few hours of a working designer's time on the first-run flow is cheaper than a month of features in a crowded category [practitioner; no cost study found].
3. **Separate "how it works" design from "how it looks".** In the dating cases, the winning moves were product rules [16][20]. The advisor should ask what rule makes the product different, not only how it looks.
4. **Warn about launch platforms.** Product Hunt-style launches reach much less per launch than before [4][5]; do not plan a launch as the main channel.
5. **Do not add instruction text for this** (repo rule). If evals show the advisor ignores crowding, make a check catch it.

## Open questions / could not verify

- No source measures how much of the 2025–2026 release growth is AI-built, or what share of AI-built apps have poor UX. JP's "most are horrible" is an observation, not a measured rate.
- No study found linking UI design quality to app revenue or retention in a crowded category with a credible causal design. The icon-download study (Wang & Li 2017, in `app-store-search.md`) is observational.
- RevenueCat's falling success share needs the absolute count of winners to say whether winning got harder or entrants just multiplied.
- Product Hunt featured-rate figures trace to a Tetriz.io analysis that was not found.
- Golder & Tellis was read only through Blank's summary; later work (e.g. on survivor bias in pioneer studies) was not checked.
- Hinge's like-rate and dates figures are the founder's own and unaudited.
