## 1. Exit base rates

- **M&A is by far the most common exit by count. IPOs dominate by value only in good years.** For full-year 2025 in the US, VC-backed acquisitions totalled **$112.7B across 995 deals**, against **$119.4B from 62 IPOs** [1] [first-party, snippet-only; verified-search: pitchbook.com, 2026-10-04] (re-check 2026-10-05: pitchbook.com and nvca.org return 403). Counting only those two routes, about 94% of exits by count were acquisitions (995/1,057; computed here). PitchBook also reports buyouts as a separate exit type, so this share is approximate.
- PitchBook historically reports that acquisitions make up most VC exits by count. One snippet cites about 80% as a three-year average around 2013 [press-quoted PitchBook, snippet-only]. Treat "~75–95% of exits are M&A, depending on year and definition" as the working range.
- **Q2 2025:** $67.7B of exit value across 394 exits, the highest quarterly value since Q4 2021 [2] [first-party, read 2026-10-05]; the Q1 comparison (330 deals, $52.4B) is not in that PDF [3] [snippet-only]. Pre-seed, seed and early-stage targets made up **63.7% of H1 2025 acquisitions** [3] [first-party, snippet-only], so most acquired startups are young and small. The "35.3%" figure is the share of acquisition count in which the buyer was itself VC-backed, not the acquisitions' share of exits [2] [read 2026-10-05; corrected: it was treated as a garbled exit-share figure].
- **Carta platform M&A:** **642 M&A transactions** targeted startups on Carta in **2024**, the highest annual total in six years (2022: 640). H2 2024 (170 deals in Q3, 163 in Q4; 333 combined) was the busiest second half since at least 2019 [4] [first-party, read 2026-10-05]. Note: Carta's own 2022 report counted **647** M&A deals in 2022 (179 in Q1, 129 in Q4); the 2024 report's "640" for 2022 presumably reflects later revisions.
- **Most acquisitions are small.** In Carta's 2022 data, deals under $5M were **55% of M&A in Q1 2022 and 44% in Q4 2022**. Deals over $100M rose from **3% to 11% in Q3 2022**, then fell back to **7% in Q4** [4] [first-party, read 2026-10-05]. Related Carta figures: in Q1 2022, 55% of acquired startups had raised $10M or less; in Q1 2023, 57% of M&A deals were valued at $10M or less [snippet-only; verified-search: carta.com, 2026-10-04]. A Carta snippet also says about **80% of acquired startups had raised under $50M** in total [4] [snippet-only; a targeted carta.com search on 2026-10-04 did not find it, so treat as unverified. Closest verified: in Q2 2023, ~11% of acquired startups had raised >$100M and 33% had raised ≥$25M]. Inference: the typical startup acquisition is a small, often undisclosed deal, not the headline nine- or ten-figure outcome.
- **Undisclosed prices:** PitchBook fills in undisclosed exit values with a regression model [press-quoted PitchBook methodology, snippet-only]. Published exit-value totals therefore include imputed values, and disclosed-price samples skew toward large deals.
- **Never exiting is the modal outcome.**
  - In the CB Insights 2008–2010 US tech seed cohort, tracked to August 2018, about **30% exited** through M&A or IPO. About 67% ended dead or self-sustaining, meaning no exit [7] [vendor, read 2026-10-05].
  - Crunchbase-based analyses show acquisitions outnumbering IPOs by about **10:1** among exits in older seed cohorts [press, snippet-only].
  - Ghosh (HBS) studied more than 2,000 VC-backed companies funded with at least $1M from 2004 to 2010. About **75% never returned cash to investors**, and **30–40% liquidated all assets** [8] [research reported by WSJ/HBS, snippet-only; unpublished dataset; verified-search: hbs.edu, 2026-10-04] (re-check 2026-10-05: HBS page and WSJ unreachable; HuffPost relay matches).
- **Shutdowns are rising.** Carta counted **966 US startup shutdowns in 2024, up from 769 in 2023** (+25.6%). Enterprise SaaS was 32% of closures, then consumer 11%, health tech 9%, fintech 8%, biotech 7% [5] [first-party via press, read 2026-10-05]. In May 2025 Carta exited the shutdown-services business and backed SimpleClosure instead (TechCrunch 2025-05-07) [press, snippet-only], so future Carta shutdown counts may not be comparable. The count covers only Carta customers that left because of bankruptcy or dissolution, so it understates the true number. In Q1 2024, 136 of 254 shutdowns had raised a priced round [5] [snippet-only] (re-check 2026-10-05: not in the TechCrunch article; Carta's own page not checked).
- **How proceeds flow to common holders:**
  - Proceeds pass through the liquidation stack: creditors and costs first, then preferred up to its preference, then common. If the price is below the preference stack, common gets nothing from the equity sale [vendor/practitioner explainers, snippet-only].
  - Carta Q1 2024: high liquidation preferences appeared in **8%** of new rounds, tied for the highest quarter this decade [6] [first-party, read 2026-10-05; Carta's sentence says "1x or higher" while its chart title says "high liquidation preferences", so read it as above 1x]. The 1x non-participating preference remains the norm. A UK snippet gives 90% non-participating in 2025 [vendor, snippet-only].
- **Inducements to the team are common in sales.** Broughman & Fried hand-collected **50 VC-backed Silicon Valley firms** sold to acquirers. In **45%** of them VCs gave at least one "carrot" (a sale bonus for executives or a carve-out from the liquidation preference for common), worth **9% of deal value** in those deals; across all 50, 4.3% of deal value. Sale bonuses appeared in 16 of 50 (average $1.63M) and carve-outs to common in 11 of 50 (average $3.7M). Overt "sticks" were relatively infrequent [16] [research, read 2026-10-05; corrected: was "carrots in 45% of trade sales" with no sample size, and the playbook read it as carve-outs]. Implication: in sales below the preference stack, what founders get comes mostly from carve-outs, retention packages or new employment, not from their common stock.
- **Acqui-hire structure in practice:** consideration often splits into (a) a small pool that buys the entity and runs through the waterfall, and (b) a larger pool of buyer equity and bonuses paid to the hired people. Investors' outcome depends on (a) and founders' outcome on (b) [vendor/practitioner explainer, snippet-only; no dataset quantifies the split].

## 2. Why acquirers buy (evidence)

- **Eliminating competition ("killer acquisitions"), pharma:** Cunningham, Ederer & Ma (JPE 2021) studied more than 16,000 drug projects [9] [research, read 2026-10-05].
  - Acquired projects that overlap the acquirer's portfolio are **23.4% less likely to see continued development** than projects bought by non-overlapping acquirers.
  - Conservatively, **5.3–7.4% of acquisitions are killer acquisitions**, which the authors translate into about **46–63 pharmaceutical acquisitions per year**. Removing the effect would raise the industry's aggregate drug-project development rate by more than 4%. (Corrected 2026-10-04: an "about 6.4%" point estimate was listed here; it was not found in the source and has been removed.)
  - These deals cluster **just below HSR reporting thresholds**.
  - The effect is stronger where the acquirer has more market power: weak competition, or patents that expire far in the future.
- **The same claim in tech is contested:**
  - *Theory, Kill Zone:* Kamepalli, Rajan & Zingales (NBER WP 27146, 2020) model how the prospect of quick acquisition by a platform lowers the value of a new entrant and VC incentives to fund it [10] [research, theory with some empirical motivation, read 2026-10-05: abstract].
  - *Evidence against a VC chill:* Prado & Bauer (IEP 2022) studied 392 Big Tech start-up acquisitions and 32,367 VC deals from 2010 to 2020. They found a **positive**, statistically significant effect of Big Tech acquisitions on VC activity in the target's segment (173 technology segments; worldwide, with positive effects also for the US and Europe), fading after several quarters [11] [research, read 2026-10-05: abstract; the "173 segments" detail is not in the abstract].
  - *Product fate:* Gautier & Lamesch (IEP 2021) studied **175 GAFAM acquisitions from 2015 to 2017**. In **more than 60%** of them the acquired product was discontinued under its original brand, more often for young targets and for deals in the acquirer's core segments. Their data cannot tell technology or talent acquisition apart from killing a competitor; they say the evidence is compatible with both, that most startups were "killed in their infancy", and that competition authorities should look harder [12] [research, read 2026-10-05; corrected: was "acquirers mainly integrated functionality… very few clear killer cases (roughly 1 in 175)"; neither claim is in the paper].
  - *Synthesis:* in software, being bought and then shut down is the common outcome. Whether that is absorption or burial is unmeasured outside pharma.
- **Acquiring talent (acqui-hire):** see §4 for the retention evidence. Google paid about $2.7B in its Character.AI deal, and press reported that Shazeer's return was the main rationale for the price [30] [press, snippet-only]. That is a direct example of buying people rather than a product. The $2.7B figure and the DOJ review are confirmed in Calcalist [30] [read 2026-10-05].
- **Technology or capability as faster-than-build:** Gautier & Lamesch list it as one of three reasons for discontinuing a bought product but cannot measure it [12] [read 2026-10-05; corrected: was cited as a "substitute for in-house R&D" finding]. No peer-reviewed build-vs-buy time-to-market study was found.
- **Revenue and customers:** no study verified this session splits startup acquisitions by motive (revenue vs. tech vs. team vs. competitor). Carta and PitchBook data imply most targets are early-stage with little revenue [3][4], which points to tech or team as the dominant motive by count. This is inference, not measured.

## 3. "Companies are bought, not sold": practitioner view

- The phrase is ubiquitous in banker and VC advice. **No original source was traced this session** [practitioner, attribution unknown].
- Common practitioner claims, all unsourced here and tagged [practitioner]:
  - Build corp-dev and product-leader relationships 12–24 months before any process.
  - Commercial partnerships (integrations, reseller deals, co-selling) are the most common precursor to strategic acquisition.
  - Strategic buyers pay for value to *their* business (synergy, defence, time to market). Financial buyers pay for standalone cash flow, so strategic prices can exceed financial ones.
  - Competitive tension from two or more interested buyers is the main lever on price.
- **Academic support for "strategic premium" logic, from the buyer side:** Betton, Eckbo & Thorburn report that bidder returns are **positive for small acquirers buying private targets** (about **+6.5%** for small public bidders paying stock for private targets) and **negative for large bidders buying public targets** (about **−2.2%** for large public bidders paying all cash) [21] [research, snippet-only] (re-check 2026-10-05: SSRN blocked; the ECGI PDFs found are different papers). Buying private startups is, on average, the value-creating corner of M&A for acquirers. Founders can use this when negotiating.
- **Valuation multiples:** no first-party, verifiable dataset on revenue multiples for private-startup acquisitions was retrieved. Do not quote "Nx ARR" figures without a dated source.

## 4. Should you build FOR acquisition?

- **Post-acquisition retention is poor. This is the strongest evidence in this file.**
  - **Kim (Strategy Science 2024):** acquired workers are about **twice as likely to leave** as observably similar regular hires at the same buyer. Data: US Census employer-employee data covering about 4,000 high-tech startup acquisitions, 1990–2011 [13] [research, read 2026-10-05: "almost twice as likely to leave" is in the CES working paper, not the journal abstract].
    - The gap shrinks for workers with longer prior tenure, when **the founding team stays intact**, and when the target is **kept structurally separate** rather than integrated.
    - MIT Sloan's summary of the paper: within the first year after acquisition, **33% of acquired workers left vs 12% of comparable regular hires**; over the three-year window acquired workers were 15% more likely to leave [press summary of the paper; verified-search: mitsloan.mit.edu, 2026-10-04; 33% vs 12% read 2026-10-05 in CES WP 18-41]. The two framings (first-year gap vs three-year gap) differ, so quote the abstract's "almost twice as likely" unless you specify the window.
  - **Ng & Stuart (SMJ 2022):** used two decades of VC-backed acquisitions matched to more than 30M résumés. Acquired employees turn over **much faster** than matched direct hires. The gap is **larger for senior ranks and advanced-degree holders**, and highest in executive, technical, business-development and sales roles [14] [research, read 2026-10-05: abstract].
  - **Seitz & Lehmann (Small Business Economics, 2025/26):** studied 454 acqui-hired founders from 241 transactions by Google (322 founders) and Facebook/Meta (132). Published *Small Business Economics* 66(1):361–391, January 2026 [15] [research, read 2026-10-05, §5.1].
    - **77% of founders departed** in the window (Google 78%, Facebook 75%), with **mean tenure 3.7 years** (Google 3.6, Facebook 3.8).
    - About **30% left within two years**, close to **50% by year four**, and only **27% remained by year five**.
    - **Serial founders** were significantly more likely to leave early. Individual founders hired alone left early more often; founders whose **entire co-founder team was also hired** were more likely to stay at least three years, and keeping the co-founder hierarchy intact helped.
    - The authors conclude acqui-hiring often doesn't "pay off" for the acquirer.
- **Product fate:** Gautier & Lamesch found more than 60% of GAFAM-acquired products stopped being supplied under their own brand [12] [research, read 2026-10-05]. Specific Google shutdowns, such as Dodgeball (acquired 2005, closed 2009) and Jaiku (acquired 2007, closed 2012), are **[unverified this session]**. Assume the product will be absorbed or sunset unless contracts say otherwise.
- **Arguments for building toward acquirability (synthesis):**
  - M&A is the most likely good exit by count [1].
  - A product that is clearly a missing feature of a large platform has an obvious buyer set.
  - Small private targets are where acquirers create value [20][21], so buyers are rational and repeat.
- **Arguments against building FOR a sale (synthesis plus evidence):**
  - Exit odds are low: about 30% of a seed cohort exits [7], and about 75% never return capital [8]. A plan whose only payoff is a sale has a fragile base case.
  - Buyer demand is cyclical and regulator-exposed (§5).
  - Retention and integration data show the post-deal life of the team and product is short [13][14][15].
  - Signalling a sale can unsettle customers, who fear sunsetting, and employees, who fear dilution or job loss [practitioner, unquantified].
  - Feature-shaped companies face a single-buyer problem. If the platform builds the feature instead, the value goes to zero [practitioner, unquantified].

## 5. Regulatory climate, 2023–2026

- **2023 Merger Guidelines:** issued jointly by DOJ and FTC in December 2023. On **2025-02-18**, FTC Chairman Ferguson and Acting AAG Assefi said the agencies would **keep using the 2023 Guidelines** [23] [regulator, read 2026-10-05]. Ferguson wrote that guidelines that change "with every new administration… will become largely worthless", and called the 2023 version "by and large… a restatement of prior iterations." Continuity is the 2025 posture on paper.
- **HSR form overhaul:**
  - The expanded premerger notification form took effect on **2025-02-10**, adding time, cost and detail to filings.
  - The US Chamber of Commerce and others sued in E.D. Texas. On **2026-02-12** the court **vacated the rule** as beyond FTC authority and arbitrary and capricious.
  - The district court stayed its order through 2026-02-19 and then denied a longer stay. The Fifth Circuit granted a brief **administrative** stay on 2026-02-19, then **denied a stay pending appeal on 2026-03-19**, so filers reverted to the pre-February 2025 form immediately. The FTC said it would accept old-form filings and voluntary new-form filings. The FTC appeal is pending [24] [law-firm alerts, read 2026-10-05 (Katten, 2026-03-23)].
- **Notable outcomes:**
  - **Adobe–Figma ($20B):** abandoned **2023-12-18** after UK CMA and EC objections. Adobe paid a **$1B** termination fee [25] [press, read 2026-10-05]. Figma then **IPO'd on 2025-07-31** at $33 per share, raising about $1.2B, and closed day one near $68B market cap [25] [press, snippet-only]. Regulatory failure did not destroy the target.
  - **Amazon–iRobot (~$1.65B):** terminated **2024-01-29** after an EC Statement of Objections. Amazon paid a **$94M** reverse break fee, and the same day iRobot announced it would lay off about **350 staff (31%)** by April [26] [press, read 2026-10-05; corrected: was "laid off… the same day"]. iRobot filed **Chapter 11 on 2025-12-14**, with a planned sale to its contract manufacturer and secured lender Shenzhen Picea [26] [press, snippet-only]. Regulatory failure did destroy this target.
  - **Microsoft–Activision ($68.7B):** closed **2023-10-13** after the CMA cleared a restructured deal in which cloud-streaming rights outside the EEA went to Ubisoft for 15 years. The **Ninth Circuit affirmed denial of the FTC's injunction on 2025-05-07**, and the FTC dropped the case that month [27] [court/press, snippet-only].
- **"Reverse acqui-hires" / license-and-hire deals in AI:**
  - **Microsoft–Inflection (Mar 2024):** Microsoft hired Suleyman and most of the roughly 70 staff and paid about **$650M**, mostly as a non-exclusive licence, which reportedly made investors whole. The UK CMA found it a **"relevant merger situation"** but cleared it at Phase 1 (**2024-09-04**) [28] [press, read 2026-10-05; CNBC says Microsoft hired "several members" of the 70-person team, and the $650M is as reported by Reuters and WSJ]. Regulators can treat hiring plus licensing as a merger.
  - **Amazon–Adept (Jun 2024):** Amazon hired CEO David Luan and key staff and licensed the technology. The **FTC opened an informal inquiry (reported 2024-07-16)**. Investors, who had put in about $414M, would **roughly recoup** their money, and Adept itself received about $25M [29] [press, read 2026-10-05]. GeekWire (2026) reports the head of Amazon's AGI lab, from the Adept deal, leaving [29] [press, snippet-only].
  - **Google–Character.AI (Aug 2024):** about **$2.7B** for a non-exclusive licence plus the return of Shazeer, De Freitas and staff. Existing investors were reportedly bought out at about **$2.5B**. Press reported a **DOJ review** of whether the structure avoided merger review [30] [press, read 2026-10-05 (Calcalist, 2025-05-25); the $2.5B buyout figure remains snippet-only].
  - **Google–Windsurf (Jul 2025):** Google paid about **$2.4B** in licence fees and hired CEO Varun Mohan, co-founder Douglas Chen and some R&D staff into Google DeepMind. This followed the collapse of a reported ~$3B OpenAI acquisition. About **72 hours later (2025-07-14)** Cognition signed a definitive agreement to acquire the rest: Windsurf's IP, product, brand and all employees not hired by Google (roughly 250 people had been left behind). At that point Windsurf had **$82M ARR** and 350+ enterprise customers. Cognition said **100% of Windsurf employees would participate financially**, with vesting cliffs waived and vesting fully accelerated for work to date. Cognition's price was not disclosed. Three weeks later Cognition offered Windsurf staff buyouts, and in September 2025 it raised $400M at a $10.2B valuation [press, snippet-only; verified-search: techcrunch.com, cnbc.com, cognition.ai, pitchbook.com, 2026-10-04].
  - **Meta–Scale AI (Jun 2025):** Meta invested about **$14.3B for a 49% stake**, and CEO Alexandr Wang left to lead Meta's new superintelligence group; Jason Droege became interim CEO. Press reported that OpenAI and Google said they would wind down work with Scale soon after (Google reportedly had planned about $200M of 2025 spend). On **2025-07-16** Scale laid off about **200 employees (~14%)**, mostly in data labeling, and cut ties with about 500 contractors. By late August 2025 TechCrunch reported "cracks" in the Meta–Scale partnership [press, snippet-only; verified-search: cnbc.com, techcrunch.com, 2026-10-04. The "non-voting" characterization was not re-confirmed; the stake's voting terms are unverified].
  - **Pattern (synthesis):** license-and-hire deals pay investors back through licence fees and reward hired staff with buyer compensation. They have left **remaining employees with an orphaned company** and are drawing regulator scrutiny: the CMA merger finding, the FTC and DOJ inquiries, and the HSR vacatur debate.
- **EU/UK posture:** the CMA and EC were decisive in killing Figma and iRobot [25][26]. The CMA's Inflection decision shows it will assert jurisdiction over talent-plus-licence deals [28]. Changes to CMA posture in 2025–26, such as government "pro-growth" steering, were not verified this session.

## 6. Making a company acquirable without building FOR acquisition

Mostly practitioner consensus. Evidence for each item is noted, and items with no study are marked.

- **Clean cap table and simple preference stack.** Preferences above 1x appear in about 8% of rounds [6]. Every extra preference turn raises the price at which common gets anything (§1). Keep 1x non-participating where possible [6] [first-party, read 2026-10-05; the founder-outcome link is mechanical].
- **IP assignment:** have signed invention-assignment agreements from every founder, employee and contractor. This is standard diligence [practitioner, no study verified].
- **Financial hygiene:** monthly closes, revenue recognition that survives diligence, and contracts with assignable terms and no change-of-control traps [practitioner].
- **Keep the founding team intact and structurally separable.** Kim finds turnover gaps shrink when founders stay together and the target is kept separate [13] [research, read 2026-10-05]. Acquirers who know this value cohesive teams, and founders can negotiate for separation.
- **Signal retention risk honestly.** Serial founders leave earlier [15] [research, read 2026-10-05]. Buyers price this through retention packages and vesting, so expect them [synthesis].
- **Partnerships as precursor.** Integrations and co-selling create the internal sponsor who argues "buy vs build" [practitioner, unquantified].
- **Be the category leader in a niche the acquirer needs.** Big Tech buys heavily in its own core segments and usually retires the bought brand [12]. Being the obvious best-in-class plug-in is the acquirability signal, but expect the product to be folded in [synthesis; corrected 2026-10-05: was "Gautier & Lamesch show Big Tech buys functionality", which the paper does not show].
- **Watch the regulatory profile.** If your likely buyer is a dominant platform and you overlap its core, expect long reviews or blocks (Figma, iRobot) [25][26]. Negotiate **reverse break fees** ($1B for Figma, $94M for iRobot) and ask whether you could survive a failed deal.

## 7. M&A failure base rates

- **"70–90% of acquisitions fail":** the canonical citation is Christensen et al., HBR March 2011: "the M&A failure rate is between 70% and 90%" [17] [read 2026-10-05: summary line; body paywalled, so the "no source inside" point below is unchecked].
  - **No primary source was found inside the article for this range.** It appears to restate earlier consultancy figures.
  - Its likely ancestor is **KPMG (1999)**: only **17%** of deals added value, **30%** made no difference and **53%** destroyed value, judged by relative share price one year after completion [18] [vendor, read 2026-10-05]. KPMG's sample: 107 companies interviewed, drawn from the top 700 cross-border deals by value, 1996–1998. "Failure" there means underperforming a share-price benchmark, not deal collapse or product shutdown.
  - **Flag:** the range mixes definitions (share price, synergy targets, executive self-report). It is about **large public acquirers**, not small private targets. Do not use it as a base rate for startup acquisitions.
- **Academic evidence on acquirer returns:**
  - **Moeller, Schlingemann & Stulz (JF 2005):** from 1998 to 2001, acquirer shareholders lost **12 cents per dollar spent**, about **$240B** in total, against $7B (1.6 cents per dollar) across the 1980s. The losses came from **a small number of large deals by highly valued acquirers**. Without those deals, acquirer wealth would have **increased** [19] [research, read 2026-10-05: abstract].
  - **Moeller, Schlingemann & Stulz (JFE 2004):** 12,023 acquisitions from 1980 to 2001. The equal-weighted acquirer announcement return was **+1.1%**, but large acquirers lost about $226B while small acquirers gained about $8B. Small acquirers earn more regardless of target type or payment; the size effect matters more than financing or target type [20] [research, read 2026-10-05 in NBER w9523: 12,023 deals, +1.1%, $8B / $226B confirmed; the "about 2 percentage points" figure was not found in that version].
  - **Betton, Eckbo & Thorburn:** bidder size and target status (public vs. private) are the main drivers. Small bidders buying private targets earn positive returns, and large bidders buying public targets earn negative ones [21] [research, snippet-only] (re-check 2026-10-05: SSRN blocked).
- **Consultancy counterpoint:** Bain reports that frequent acquirers (at least one deal a year) earned about **130% higher shareholder returns** than non-acquirers, up from 57% in 2000–2010. Its "about 70% now succeed" is what **executives report** about their own deals, not a measured outcome; Bain's earlier executive surveys found about 60% of deals missed internal expectations [22] [vendor, read 2026-10-05; corrected: was "mountain climbers earned about 11.0% TSR… about 70% succeeding"; the 11.0% figure is not on the cited page]. Treat this as directional only.
- **Bottom line:** "most mergers fail" is a large-public-deal, definition-dependent claim. For the **startup-as-target** case, the acquirer-return literature is **favourable to buyers**. The real failure modes for founders are integration and retention (§4), not acquirer share-price losses.

## Decision rules for a founder (synthesis)

1. **Plan for no exit, then for M&A, then for IPO, in that order of likelihood.** About 30% of a seed cohort exits [7], and about 94% of 2025 M&A-plus-IPO exits by count were acquisitions [1]. Build a business that works standalone.
2. **Run the waterfall before any conversation.** Compute what common receives at 0.5x, 1x and 2x of total preference. If common is underwater, your outcome depends on carve-outs, sale bonuses and retention packages: in a 50-firm sample, 45% of sales included a sale bonus or carve-out, worth 9% of deal value in those deals [16]. Negotiate those explicitly.
3. **Don't build a feature-to-sell company unless at least two credible buyers exist**, and you can survive if the platform builds the feature itself. Single-buyer and regulator exposure (iRobot) can turn a "sure exit" into collapse.
4. **For a dominant-platform buyer, price in regulatory time and risk.** Demand a meaningful reverse break fee and an operating-covenant carve-out. Figma survived its blocked deal; iRobot did not [25][26].
5. **In a license-and-hire offer, get in writing what happens to the people left behind and to common holders.** These structures have repaid investors (Adept, Character.AI) while leaving a residual company [29][30].
6. **Expect to leave within about 4 years, and negotiate on that basis.** The median acquired founder is gone in about 3–4 years [15]. Acquired staff leave about twice as fast as regular hires [13]. Negotiate team integrity and structural separation, which reduce turnover [13], or price the earn-out and retention accordingly.
7. **Earn acquirability through hygiene, not pitching.** Keep a clean cap table, simple preferences, complete IP assignment and diligence-ready financials, and build real partnerships with likely buyers. Most of this pays off even if you never sell.
8. **Don't cite "70–90% of M&A fails" to buyers or your board.** It is unsourced and about large public deals. Small acquirers buying private targets earn positive returns [20][21]. Use that to argue for price.
