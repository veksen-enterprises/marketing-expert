## 1. Exit base rates

- **M&A is by far the most common exit by count. IPOs dominate by value only in good years.** For full-year 2025 in the US, VC-backed acquisitions totalled **$112.7B across 995 deals**, against **$119.4B from 62 IPOs** [1] [first-party, snippet-only]. Counting only those two routes, about 94% of exits by count were acquisitions (995/1,057; computed here). PitchBook also reports buyouts as a separate exit type, so this share is approximate.
- PitchBook historically reports that acquisitions make up most VC exits by count. One snippet cites about 80% as a three-year average around 2013 [press-quoted PitchBook, snippet-only]. Treat "~75–95% of exits are M&A, depending on year and definition" as the working range.
- **Q2 2025:** $67.7B of exit value across 394 deals, up from 330 deals and $52.4B in Q1 [2][3] [first-party, snippet-only]. Pre-seed, seed and early-stage targets made up **63.7% of H1 2025 acquisitions** [3] [first-party, snippet-only], so most acquired startups are young and small. One snippet says "acquisitions made up a 35.3% share of total US exit deal count" in H1 2025. That conflicts with the full-year numbers and is probably a garbled sub-segment, so it is not used.
- **Carta platform M&A:** **642 M&A transactions** targeted startups on Carta in **2024**, the highest annual total in six years (2022: 640). H2 2024 (170 deals in Q3, 163 in Q4) was the busiest second half since at least 2019 [4] [first-party, snippet-only].
- **Most acquisitions are small.** In Carta's 2022 data, deals under $5M were **55% of M&A in Q1 2022 and 44% in Q4 2022**, and deals over $100M ran from 3% to 11% by quarter [4] [first-party, snippet-only]. A Carta snippet also says about **80% of acquired startups had raised under $50M** in total [4] [snippet-only; the exact report year is unclear]. Inference: the typical startup acquisition is a small, often undisclosed deal, not the headline nine- or ten-figure outcome.
- **Undisclosed prices:** PitchBook fills in undisclosed exit values with a regression model [press-quoted PitchBook methodology, snippet-only]. Published exit-value totals therefore include imputed values, and disclosed-price samples skew toward large deals.
- **Never exiting is the modal outcome.**
  - In the CB Insights 2008–2010 US tech seed cohort, tracked to August 2018, about **30% exited** through M&A or IPO. About 67% ended dead or self-sustaining, meaning no exit [7] [vendor, snippet-only].
  - Crunchbase-based analyses show acquisitions outnumbering IPOs by about **10:1** among exits in older seed cohorts [press, snippet-only].
  - Ghosh (HBS) studied about 2,000 VC-backed companies funded with at least $1M from 2004 to 2010. About **75% never returned cash to investors**, and **30–40% liquidated all assets** [8] [research reported by WSJ/HBS, snippet-only; unpublished dataset].
- **Shutdowns are rising.** Carta counted **966 US startup shutdowns in 2024, up from 769 in 2023** (+25.6%). Enterprise SaaS was 32% of closures [5] [first-party via press, snippet-only]. The count covers only Carta customers that left because of bankruptcy or dissolution, so it understates the true number. In Q1 2024, 136 of 254 shutdowns had raised a priced round [5] [snippet-only].
- **How proceeds flow to common holders:**
  - Proceeds pass through the liquidation stack: creditors and costs first, then preferred up to its preference, then common. If the price is below the preference stack, common gets nothing from the equity sale [vendor/practitioner explainers, snippet-only].
  - Carta Q1 2024: liquidation preferences above 1x appeared in about **8%** of new rounds, tied for the highest quarter this decade [6] [first-party, snippet-only]. The 1x non-participating preference remains the norm. A UK snippet gives 90% non-participating in 2025 [vendor, snippet-only].
- **Management carve-outs are common in sales.** Broughman & Fried found VCs gave "carrots" to the entrepreneurial team in **45% of trade sales**, averaging **9% of deal value**. Coercive "sticks" were rare [16] [research, snippet-only]. Implication: in sales below the preference stack, what founders get comes mostly from carve-outs, retention packages or new employment, not from their common stock.
- **Acqui-hire structure in practice:** consideration often splits into (a) a small pool that buys the entity and runs through the waterfall, and (b) a larger pool of buyer equity and bonuses paid to the hired people. Investors' outcome depends on (a) and founders' outcome on (b) [vendor/practitioner explainer, snippet-only; no dataset quantifies the split].

## 2. Why acquirers buy (evidence)

- **Eliminating competition ("killer acquisitions"), pharma:** Cunningham, Ederer & Ma (JPE 2021) studied more than 16,000 drug projects [9] [research, snippet-only].
  - Acquired projects that overlap the acquirer's portfolio are **23.4% less likely to see continued development** than projects bought by non-overlapping acquirers.
  - Conservatively, **5.3–7.4% of acquisitions (about 6.4%) are killer acquisitions**.
  - These deals cluster **just below HSR reporting thresholds**.
  - The effect is stronger where the acquirer has more market power: weak competition, or patents that expire far in the future.
- **The same claim in tech is contested:**
  - *Theory, Kill Zone:* Kamepalli, Rajan & Zingales (NBER WP 27146, 2020) model how the prospect of quick acquisition by a platform lowers the value of a new entrant and VC incentives to fund it [10] [research, theory with some empirical motivation, snippet-only].
  - *Evidence against a VC chill:* Prado & Bauer (IEP 2022) studied 392 Big Tech start-up acquisitions and 32,367 VC deals from 2010 to 2020. They found a **positive**, statistically significant effect of Big Tech acquisitions on VC activity in the target's sub-industry, fading after several quarters [11] [research, snippet-only].
  - *Product fate:* Gautier & Lamesch (IEP 2021) studied **175 GAFAM acquisitions from 2015 to 2017**. Many acquired products were no longer supplied under their original brand, but acquirers mainly integrated functionality, technology, talent and IP. They read M&A as a substitute for in-house R&D. They found very few clear "killer" cases (summarised as roughly 1 in 175) [12] [research, snippet-only].
  - *Synthesis:* in software, being bought to be absorbed is common, while being bought to be buried is rare in the evidence but not zero.
- **Acquiring talent (acqui-hire):** see §4 for the retention evidence. Google paid about $2.7B in its Character.AI deal, and press reported that Shazeer's return was the main rationale for the price [30] [press, snippet-only]. That is a direct example of buying people rather than a product.
- **Technology or capability as faster-than-build:** Gautier & Lamesch's "substitute for in-house R&D" finding [12] is the best evidence found here. No peer-reviewed build-vs-buy time-to-market study was verified this session.
- **Revenue and customers:** no study verified this session splits startup acquisitions by motive (revenue vs. tech vs. team vs. competitor). Carta and PitchBook data imply most targets are early-stage with little revenue [3][4], which points to tech or team as the dominant motive by count. This is inference, not measured.

## 3. "Companies are bought, not sold": practitioner view

- The phrase is ubiquitous in banker and VC advice. **No original source was traced this session** [practitioner, attribution unknown].
- Common practitioner claims, all unsourced here and tagged [practitioner]:
  - Build corp-dev and product-leader relationships 12–24 months before any process.
  - Commercial partnerships (integrations, reseller deals, co-selling) are the most common precursor to strategic acquisition.
  - Strategic buyers pay for value to *their* business (synergy, defence, time to market). Financial buyers pay for standalone cash flow, so strategic prices can exceed financial ones.
  - Competitive tension from two or more interested buyers is the main lever on price.
- **Academic support for "strategic premium" logic, from the buyer side:** Betton, Eckbo & Thorburn report that bidder returns are **positive for small acquirers buying private targets** (about **+6.5%** for small public bidders paying stock for private targets) and **negative for large bidders buying public targets** (about **−2.2%** for large public bidders paying all cash) [21] [research, snippet-only]. Buying private startups is, on average, the value-creating corner of M&A for acquirers. Founders can use this when negotiating.
- **Valuation multiples:** no first-party, verifiable dataset on revenue multiples for private-startup acquisitions was retrieved. Do not quote "Nx ARR" figures without a dated source.

## 4. Should you build FOR acquisition?

- **Post-acquisition retention is poor. This is the strongest evidence in this file.**
  - **Kim (Strategy Science 2024):** acquired workers are about **twice as likely to leave** as observably similar regular hires at the same buyer. Data: US Census LEHD, 1990–2011 [13] [research, snippet-only].
    - The gap shrinks for workers with longer prior tenure, when **the founding team stays intact**, and when the target is **kept structurally separate** rather than integrated.
    - One snippet says about one-third of acquired hires leave within the first year. That figure was not confirmed against the paper.
  - **Ng & Stuart (SMJ 2022):** used two decades of VC-backed acquisitions matched to more than 30M résumés. Acquired employees turn over **much faster** than matched direct hires. The gap is **larger for senior ranks and advanced-degree holders**, and highest in executive, technical and business-development roles [14] [research, snippet-only].
  - **Seitz & Lehmann (Small Business Economics, 2025/26):** studied 454 founders from 241 startups acquired by Google and Facebook [15] [research, snippet-only].
    - **77% of founders departed** in the window, with **mean tenure 3.7 years** (Google 3.6, Facebook 3.8).
    - About **30% left within two years**, close to **50% by year four**, and only **27% remained by year five**.
    - **Serial founders** were significantly more likely to leave early.
    - The authors conclude acqui-hiring often doesn't "pay off" for the acquirer.
- **Product fate:** Gautier & Lamesch found many GAFAM-acquired products stop being supplied under their own brand [12] [research, snippet-only]. Specific Google shutdowns, such as Dodgeball (acquired 2005, closed 2009) and Jaiku (acquired 2007, closed 2012), are **[unverified this session]**. Assume the product will be absorbed or sunset unless contracts say otherwise.
- **Arguments for building toward acquirability (synthesis):**
  - M&A is the most likely good exit by count [1].
  - A product that is clearly a missing feature of a large platform has an obvious buyer set.
  - Small private targets are where acquirers create value [20][21], so buyers are rational and repeat.
- **Arguments against building FOR a sale (synthesis plus evidence):**
  - Exit odds are low: about 30% of a seed cohort exits [7], and about 75% never return capital [8]. A plan whose only payoff is a sale has a fragile base case.
  - Buyer demand is cyclical and regulator-exposed (§5).
  - Retention and integration data show the post-deal life of the team and product is short [13][14][15].
  - Signalling a sale can unsettle customers, who fear sunsetting, and employees, who fear dilution or job loss [practitioner, unquantified].
  - Feature-shaped companies face a single-buyer problem. If the platform builds the feature instead, the value goes to zero [practitioner, unquantified].

## 5. Regulatory climate, 2023–2026

- **2023 Merger Guidelines:** issued jointly by DOJ and FTC in December 2023. On **2025-02-18**, FTC Chairman Ferguson and Acting AAG Assefi said the agencies would **keep using the 2023 Guidelines** [23] [regulator, snippet-only]. Ferguson wrote that guidelines that change "with every new administration… will become largely worthless", and called the 2023 version "by and large… a restatement of prior iterations." Continuity is the 2025 posture on paper.
- **HSR form overhaul:**
  - The expanded premerger notification form took effect on **2025-02-10**, adding time, cost and detail to filings.
  - The US Chamber of Commerce and others sued in E.D. Texas. On **2026-02-12** the court **vacated the rule** as beyond FTC authority and arbitrary and capricious.
  - The **Fifth Circuit denied a stay on 2026-03-19**, so filers reverted to the pre-February 2025 form. The FTC appeal is pending [24] [law-firm alerts, snippet-only].
- **Notable outcomes:**
  - **Adobe–Figma ($20B):** abandoned **2023-12-18** after UK CMA and EC objections. Adobe paid a **$1B** termination fee [25] [press, snippet-only]. Figma then **IPO'd on 2025-07-31** at $33 per share, raising about $1.2B, and closed day one near $68B market cap [25] [press, snippet-only]. Regulatory failure did not destroy the target.
  - **Amazon–iRobot (~$1.65B):** terminated **2024-01-29** after an EC Statement of Objections. Amazon paid a **$94M** reverse break fee, and iRobot laid off **31% (about 350)** of staff the same day [26] [press, snippet-only]. iRobot filed **Chapter 11 on 2025-12-14**, with a planned sale to its contract manufacturer and secured lender Shenzhen Picea [26] [press, snippet-only]. Regulatory failure did destroy this target.
  - **Microsoft–Activision ($68.7B):** closed **2023-10-13** after the CMA cleared a restructured deal in which cloud-streaming rights outside the EEA went to Ubisoft for 15 years. The **Ninth Circuit affirmed denial of the FTC's injunction on 2025-05-07**, and the FTC dropped the case that month [27] [court/press, snippet-only].
- **"Reverse acqui-hires" / license-and-hire deals in AI:**
  - **Microsoft–Inflection (Mar 2024):** Microsoft hired Suleyman and most of the roughly 70 staff and paid about **$650M**, mostly as a non-exclusive licence, which reportedly made investors whole. The UK CMA found it a **"relevant merger situation"** but cleared it at Phase 1 (**2024-09-04**) [28] [press, snippet-only]. Regulators can treat hiring plus licensing as a merger.
  - **Amazon–Adept (Jun 2024):** Amazon hired CEO David Luan and key staff and licensed the technology. The **FTC opened an informal inquiry (reported 2024-07-16)**. Investors, who had put in about $414M, would **roughly recoup** their money, and Adept itself received about $25M [29] [press, snippet-only]. GeekWire (2026) reports the head of Amazon's AGI lab, from the Adept deal, leaving [29] [press, snippet-only].
  - **Google–Character.AI (Aug 2024):** about **$2.7B** for a non-exclusive licence plus the return of Shazeer, De Freitas and staff. Existing investors were reportedly bought out at about **$2.5B**. Press reported a **DOJ review** of whether the structure avoided merger review [30] [press, snippet-only].
  - **Google–Windsurf (Jul 2025):** about $2.4B for a licence plus hiring CEO Varun Mohan and some staff. Cognition then acquired the remaining Windsurf company and team **[unverified this session]**.
  - **Meta–Scale AI (Jun 2025):** about $14.3B for a 49% non-voting stake, with CEO Alexandr Wang joining Meta. Scale later cut staff, and some lab customers reportedly reduced work with Scale **[unverified this session]**.
  - **Pattern (synthesis):** license-and-hire deals pay investors back through licence fees and reward hired staff with buyer compensation. They have left **remaining employees with an orphaned company** and are drawing regulator scrutiny: the CMA merger finding, the FTC and DOJ inquiries, and the HSR vacatur debate.
- **EU/UK posture:** the CMA and EC were decisive in killing Figma and iRobot [25][26]. The CMA's Inflection decision shows it will assert jurisdiction over talent-plus-licence deals [28]. Changes to CMA posture in 2025–26, such as government "pro-growth" steering, were not verified this session.

## 6. Making a company acquirable without building FOR acquisition

Mostly practitioner consensus. Evidence for each item is noted, and items with no study are marked.

- **Clean cap table and simple preference stack.** Preferences above 1x appear in about 8% of rounds [6]. Every extra preference turn raises the price at which common gets anything (§1). Keep 1x non-participating where possible [6] [first-party, snippet-only; the founder-outcome link is mechanical].
- **IP assignment:** have signed invention-assignment agreements from every founder, employee and contractor. This is standard diligence [practitioner, no study verified].
- **Financial hygiene:** monthly closes, revenue recognition that survives diligence, and contracts with assignable terms and no change-of-control traps [practitioner].
- **Keep the founding team intact and structurally separable.** Kim finds turnover gaps shrink when founders stay together and the target is kept separate [13] [research, snippet-only]. Acquirers who know this value cohesive teams, and founders can negotiate for separation.
- **Signal retention risk honestly.** Serial founders leave earlier [15]. Buyers price this through retention packages and vesting, so expect them [research, snippet-only].
- **Partnerships as precursor.** Integrations and co-selling create the internal sponsor who argues "buy vs build" [practitioner, unquantified].
- **Be the category leader in a niche the acquirer needs.** Gautier & Lamesch show Big Tech buys functionality to plug into its ecosystem [12]. Being the obvious best-in-class plug-in is the acquirability signal [research plus synthesis].
- **Watch the regulatory profile.** If your likely buyer is a dominant platform and you overlap its core, expect long reviews or blocks (Figma, iRobot) [25][26]. Negotiate **reverse break fees** ($1B for Figma, $94M for iRobot) and ask whether you could survive a failed deal.

## 7. M&A failure base rates

- **"70–90% of acquisitions fail":** the canonical citation is Christensen et al., HBR March 2011: "the M&A failure rate is between 70% and 90%" [17] [snippet-only].
  - **No primary source was found inside the article for this range.** It appears to restate earlier consultancy figures.
  - Its likely ancestor is **KPMG (1999)**: only **17%** of deals added value, **30%** made no difference and **53%** destroyed value, judged by relative share price one year after completion [18] [vendor, snippet-only]. KPMG's sample is about 100+ large cross-border deals. "Failure" there means underperforming a share-price benchmark, not deal collapse or product shutdown.
  - **Flag:** the range mixes definitions (share price, synergy targets, executive self-report). It is about **large public acquirers**, not small private targets. Do not use it as a base rate for startup acquisitions.
- **Academic evidence on acquirer returns:**
  - **Moeller, Schlingemann & Stulz (JF 2005):** from 1998 to 2001, acquirer shareholders lost **12 cents per dollar spent**, about **$240B** in total, against $7B (1.6 cents per dollar) across the 1980s. The losses came from **a small number of large deals by highly valued acquirers**. Without those deals, acquirer wealth would have **increased** [19] [research, snippet-only].
  - **Moeller, Schlingemann & Stulz (JFE 2004):** 12,023 acquisitions from 1980 to 2001. The equal-weighted acquirer announcement return was **+1.1%**, but large acquirers lost about $226B while small acquirers gained about $8B. Small acquirers earn about **2 percentage points** more regardless of target type or payment [20] [research, snippet-only].
  - **Betton, Eckbo & Thorburn:** bidder size and target status (public vs. private) are the main drivers. Small bidders buying private targets earn positive returns, and large bidders buying public targets earn negative ones [21] [research, snippet-only].
- **Consultancy counterpoint:** Bain reports that frequent, material acquirers outperformed inactive peers on TSR. Its "mountain climbers" earned about **11.0%** annual TSR. Bain also claims the old "70% fail" pattern has roughly reversed over 20 years, with about 70% succeeding [22] [vendor, snippet-only; methodology not read]. Treat this as directional only.
- **Bottom line:** "most mergers fail" is a large-public-deal, definition-dependent claim. For the **startup-as-target** case, the acquirer-return literature is **favourable to buyers**. The real failure modes for founders are integration and retention (§4), not acquirer share-price losses.

## Decision rules for a founder (synthesis)

1. **Plan for no exit, then for M&A, then for IPO, in that order of likelihood.** About 30% of a seed cohort exits [7], and about 94% of 2025 M&A-plus-IPO exits by count were acquisitions [1]. Build a business that works standalone.
2. **Run the waterfall before any conversation.** Compute what common receives at 0.5x, 1x and 2x of total preference. If common is underwater, your outcome depends on carve-outs and retention packages: 45% of trade sales include them, averaging 9% of value [16]. Negotiate those explicitly.
3. **Don't build a feature-to-sell company unless at least two credible buyers exist**, and you can survive if the platform builds the feature itself. Single-buyer and regulator exposure (iRobot) can turn a "sure exit" into collapse.
4. **For a dominant-platform buyer, price in regulatory time and risk.** Demand a meaningful reverse break fee and an operating-covenant carve-out. Figma survived its blocked deal; iRobot did not [25][26].
5. **In a license-and-hire offer, get in writing what happens to the people left behind and to common holders.** These structures have repaid investors (Adept, Character.AI) while leaving a residual company [29][30].
6. **Expect to leave within about 4 years, and negotiate on that basis.** The median acquired founder is gone in about 3–4 years [15]. Acquired staff leave about twice as fast as regular hires [13]. Negotiate team integrity and structural separation, which reduce turnover [13], or price the earn-out and retention accordingly.
7. **Earn acquirability through hygiene, not pitching.** Keep a clean cap table, simple preferences, complete IP assignment and diligence-ready financials, and build real partnerships with likely buyers. Most of this pays off even if you never sell.
8. **Don't cite "70–90% of M&A fails" to buyers or your board.** It is unsourced and about large public deals. Small acquirers buying private targets earn positive returns [20][21]. Use that to argue for price.
