# Intake question design: survey methodology applied to the get_started profile questions (checked 2026-10-27)

Scope: the eight first-run profile questions in `src/prompts.ts` (get_started, step 3) and how their answers are saved by `save_business_profile` and read by `match_small_bets`. The question is which wording and storage changes the survey-methods literature supports, and how strongly.

Method caveat: "read" = full primary text or a full secondary text that reports the primary study was read this session. [abstract-only] = only the abstract or a catalogue page was read. [secondary] = the finding is taken from a review or later paper that describes it, not from the original. Nothing here was read in a paywalled journal version. Tags: [research] peer-reviewed or government methods study; [vendor] company with a stake; [derived] my inference, not in a source.

Transfer warning, read first: almost all of this literature studies people reporting **their own** behaviour (purchases, doctor visits, program participation) to an interviewer. A founder answering the intake is doing something different: reporting facts about a business, and often about **other people's** behaviour (where users came from). Results on comprehension and records transfer well, because they are about the question and the task. Results on recall accuracy transfer only partly. None of the sources below studied founders answering an onboarding form. Treat every rewrite here as a hypothesis to test in the evaluations, not a measured improvement.

## 1. The model: four places an answer can go wrong
- Tourangeau, Rips & Rasinski split answering into four processes: comprehension (what the question means), retrieval (searching memory), judgment (combining what came to mind into an estimate) and response (fitting it to the answer format and deciding what to reveal). [research, 1, secondary]
- Use for this project: each profile question can fail at a different step, so each needs a different fix. Wording fixes comprehension. Nothing in wording fixes retrieval of a fact the founder never saw. [derived]

## 2. Comprehension: undefined terms cause large errors, and definitions fix them
- Conrad & Schober, lab experiment: 41 respondents, 43 interviews, 12 questions from three government surveys about fictional scenarios with known right answers. When the scenario fit the question's terms plainly, accuracy was about 97–98% either way. When it didn't (the "complicated mapping" case, for example a room with one fixture and the official definition of a half bathroom), standardized interviews were about 28% accurate and interviews that could give the definition about 87%. [research, 5, read; n=41; lab; fictional scenarios]
- Cost: median 11.5 minutes per flexible interview against 3.4 for standardized. [research, 5, read]
- Transfer to question 1, "how many people use it now": "use" and "now" are exactly the kind of terms that map badly. A founder with 400 sign-ups, 60 people who opened it this month and 12 who use it weekly has three defensible answers. The definition has to be in the question. [derived from 5]
- The agent can act as the flexible interviewer: it can give the definition in the question at no extra time cost to the founder, which removes the main cost in source 5. [derived]

## 3. Retrieval: records beat memory, and small counts should be listed, not estimated
- Records: in the BLS Consumer Expenditure work, records existed for only 36% of purchases, and reported amounts matched records within 5–10% for 53% of items. Records available and accuracy of reports were related (Strohm et al. 2012, as cited). Memory fades as time since the event grows. [research, 7, read; household spending, not business metrics]
- Telescoping: first interviews, which have no earlier interview to bound them, report more spending than later ones (first-wave home furnishings 48.6% higher, clothing 14.5%, Silberstein 1990, as cited); Neter & Waksberg (1964) found more telescoping for larger home repairs. [research, 7, read; secondary for both studies]. Telescoping means pulling events from before the reference period into it. For "users now", the likely analogue is counting a launch-week spike as current use. [derived; not tested]
- Enumeration vs estimation: people list episodes one by one when they are few, and switch to rates or general impressions as counts grow or the period lengthens. More time to answer led to more listing. "How often" drew more rate-based answers than "How many times", but the wording effect was weak. [research, 6, read; summarising Blair & Burton 1987 and Burton & Blair 1991]
- Mean reported frequencies by strategy in Conrad et al.'s own data: listing 2.3, rate retrieval 7.8, rate estimation 11.1, general impression 12.3 a month. This shows strategy follows count, not that one strategy is more accurate. The paper says it does not address accuracy, and that listing tends to undercount. [research, 6, read]
- Transfer: the intake is aimed at products with 0–10 customers. At that size the founder can name each customer, which is a record, not an estimate. Asking for the list instead of a number moves the answer from judgment to retrieval. [derived from 6]

## 4. "Where users came from" is mostly not a memory question
- The founder usually never saw how a user found the product. There is nothing to retrieve, so the answer is built at the judgment step from what the founder does remember: the launch post they wrote, the spike they watched, the one user who mentioned Reddit. [derived from 1]
- Proxy reports (one person reporting another's behaviour) are only slightly worse than self-reports when checked against administrative records, and the advantage of self-reports is "generally small". [research, 9, abstract-only; government program participation]. This does not rescue question 2: in Moore's study the proxy was a household member who had seen the behaviour. A founder has not. [derived]
- Even users asked directly misremember the source, favour recent and memorable touchpoints, and pick the first option in a list. [vendor, 12, read; no data shown]
- Consequence: no rewrite makes question 2 accurate. A rewrite can only (a) ask for specific recent cases, which the founder may actually know, and (b) ask how they know. The useful output is the basis, not the answer. [derived]
- Code fact, checked 2026-10-27: the answer is saved to `channels`, and no tool reads `channels`. `grep -n channels src/lib/*.ts` finds it only in the profile type and `missingFields`. The commit that added question 2 (902f52f) says the questions cover "the facts the tools key on"; for channels that is not true yet. Only the agent reads it.

## 5. Founder numbers lean optimistic
- 2,994 new business owners (NFIB members, 1985): 81% rated their own chance of success 7 out of 10 or better, and 33% gave 10 out of 10. For businesses like theirs, 39% said 7 or better. Mean 8.1 for their own odds, 5.9 for others'. [research, 10, read; n=2,994; one US survey, 1985; forecasts, not reports of current facts]
- Owners' survey answers about business income, value and returns differed from tax records, sales and filings by amounts the authors call "economically relevant". [research, 11, abstract-only; direction of the error not seen]
- Transfer: these show founders' judgments can be biased upward; they do not show founders inflate a user count they can look up. The case for tagging rests on section 3 (records beat memory), not on optimism. [derived]

## 6. Satisficing: eight questions at once, and a standing offer to skip
- Satisficing is giving a good-enough answer with minimum effort: weakly (every step done less carefully) or strongly (a step skipped). It is more likely when the task is hard, ability is low and motivation is low. Krosnick expected these to multiply. [research, 2 via 3, secondary]
- The systematic review finds the evidence mixed, few tests of the predicted interactions, and "little appetite" to try to falsify the theory; a null result is usually read as "no satisficing here" rather than as evidence against it. [research, 3, read]. So satisficing is a useful description, not a precise predictor.
- Offering a "no opinion" option: across nine experiments in three household surveys, those who took it were lower in cognitive skill, answering in writing rather than aloud, later in the survey, and putting in less effort. Leaving the option out did not lower data quality. [research, 4, abstract-only; attitude questions, not facts]
- Transfer: get_started says once that any question can be skipped. For the two effortful ones (1 and 2) that is a "no opinion" option, and source 4 predicts it draws the answers that need most effort. Asking for a rough number marked as a guess keeps the answer and records that it is weak. [derived from 4; attitudes vs facts not tested]
- The questions already go in order of how much they change the advice, which puts the effortful ones first, where effort is highest. That matches source 4's later-in-survey finding. [derived]

## 7. Examples in the question shape the answer
- Response options and examples in a question tell the respondent what the asker counts and what range is normal; answers move with them. [research, 8, abstract-only; details not seen]
- Question 2 lists "a post, search, word of mouth, a community, a store listing". That list works as answer options, and source 12 reports users pick the first visible option. [vendor, 12, read; derived for this case]. Question 6 lists "their own following, communities they're active in, data nobody else has published", which may make the founder look only for those three. [derived]

## Open questions
- Does tagging change advice quality? Untested. The evaluation would compare runs where the same fictional founder gives estimated vs looked-up traction near the stage-2 threshold (100 active users) and check whether the answer says the stage depends on a guess.
- Schober & Conrad (1997) and Krosnick (1991) originals not read; the four-process model is from a review of the book, not the book.
- No study found of founders answering onboarding questions, of founders' recall of acquisition sources, or of whether a list of named customers is more accurate than a count. The enumeration argument rests on household-survey research.
- Availability (Tversky & Kahneman 1973) is the usual name for "memorable channels get over-credited". Not used here: the 1973 letter-frequency result has been contested and its replication status was not checked.
