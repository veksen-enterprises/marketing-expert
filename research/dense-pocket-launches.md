## 1. Facebook: one campus, then one campus at a time

- Five days after launch at Harvard, "over 650 students had registered". Zuckerberg expected about 900 by the next morning. [press, 1; contemporary; founder's own count]
- Two months in: 12 universities, 38,327 registered users, "an average of 1,000 new registration requests daily". [press, 2; contemporary; no per-campus figures]
- The one study found of the staggered campus entry (source 3) is about long-run social capital, not launch success. Its draft asks not to be cited, so its figures are left out. Nothing in what was read gives a threshold for a pocket working. [research, 3, read; fetch summary; working paper, not quoted at the authors' request]

## 2. Who an invitation reaches matters more than how many

- In 54 million Facebook invitation emails, the chance an invitee joined rose with the number of separate groups among their friends already on the site. Once that was held fixed, more friends on the site was "generally a negative indicator". The same pattern predicted who was still active about three months later. [research, 4, read; fetch summary; observational; one platform; join rates only in figures, not seen]
- For a pocket, this favours a campus of many overlapping circles over one closed clique. It is friends joining friends, not strangers matching. [derived from 4]

## 3. Tinder: sororities, then parties at one school

- TechCrunch, as quoted: Tinder was seeded at USC "with just 300 people" and "by the end of the week it had 1,000 users". [press retelling, 5; original not read]
- A co-founder: after the sorority and fraternity visits, Tinder "had grown to almost 15,000 users". [founder's own account, 5; undated]
- The party: to get in you had to show the app installed; "We put a bouncer in the house to check that you had done it." Then: "just throw another party" at other schools. [practitioner, 6; Chen retelling a founder; highlights only]
- Chen's figure for that first cohort: "95 percent of this initial cohort started to use this app every day for three hours a day." No cohort size was in the highlights seen. [founder's story via Chen, 6; unaudited]
- Chen gives no head count for an atomic network. His nearest figure is Uber's: "maybe on the order of hundreds of people, at a specific moment in time". [practitioner, 7]

## 4. Hinge and The League: one city, gated

- Hinge in Washington, DC, 2013: the founder said "over 20,000 are active on Hinge" of "110,000 single college grads in the city". The article also reports 85% of downloaders active a week later and 75% a month later, without saying who measured it. [press, 9; founder's numbers, unaudited]
- That is about 18% of the target group in one city: the only stated density in any source found, and a founder's claim. [derived from 9]
- The League launches a city with a "500-person founding class"; everyone else waits. It waits for "a large enough mass" on the waitlist, then studies the local singles. In Richmond "more than 3,000 people" had downloaded the app at launch. [press, 10; company spokesperson; no number for "large enough"] The DC launch press gives no numbers. [press, 11]
- Bumble's campus ambassadors: the one analysis found counts posts and followers, not installs, mixed with paid media. [vendor, 12]

## 5. What the evidence amounts to

- Every outcome is a winner's own number. No source compares pockets that worked with ones that didn't, or states a users-per-campus or per-city threshold. [derived from 1–12]
- The numbers that exist: a few hundred to start (Facebook 650 in five days; Tinder 300 seeded; The League 500 per city) and one share of the target group (Hinge, about 18%). [derived; founders' accounts]
