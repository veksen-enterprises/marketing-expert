## 1. What it costs

- Money, one US region, 2026: a $199 application fee, then $599 a year. Membership is "a one or two-year commitment". Fees are "non-refundable without exception", and the region may "collect the full balance due if the member leaves". [first-party, 5; one region; forming chapter]
- A secondary 2026 article gives US ranges of $150–299 to apply, $539–699 a year, and $15–25 for the meal at each meeting. [secondary, 6; no source linked]
- Time: chapters meet weekly; the article says for 90 minutes. [vendor, 1; secondary, 6] BNI's general policies allow "three absences within a continuous six-month period". A substitute you send does not count as an absence, and you must "stay for the entire published meeting time". [first-party, 3; undated] One region caps substitutes at three per rolling six months. [first-party, 4]
- So six months in BNI is about 26 meetings, roughly 50 hours with travel, and about $800 already paid that won't come back. [derived from 3, 5, 6; travel time assumed]
- A chamber of commerce leads group can be much cheaper: $25 per half-year on top of chamber dues, weekly one-hour meetings, 20–30 members, "one member per industry". [first-party, 8; one chamber; chamber dues not seen]
- The seat rule is BNI policy everywhere: "Only one person from each BNI classification can join a chapter." [first-party, 3]

## 2. What members get: BNI's own numbers

- BNI reports 355,000+ members, 11,800+ chapters, 17.8 million referrals and $26.9 billion of "member generated business" in the 12 months to 31 Dec 2025. Its footnote: "historical BNI Member self-reported data… Errors in self-reporting of data or in subsequent analysis are possible." [vendor, 1; self-reported; not audited]
- In July 2023 it reported 14.2 million referrals and $21.9 billion from 300,000+ members. [vendor, 2; self-reported]
- Divided out, that is about 50 referrals and $76,000 of reported business per member per year. That is an average over members who stayed and reported. It says nothing about people who left, or about the middle member. [derived from 1]
- BNI's founder: people who credit networking for their success spent "an average of 6 1/2 hours a week networking". No sample or method is given. [vendor, 7; no method]

## 3. Independent evidence

- None found that measures referral-group members' sales against non-members, or how many members leave. [derived; about 14 searches]
- One student thesis surveyed BNI members in Sweden and Denmark. "Business opportunities" had the strongest link to satisfaction; social benefits the weakest. It measures satisfaction, not sales. [student thesis, 9, abstract-only; sample size not seen]
- Two firm-level studies of formal networks found gains, but neither network is a referral group. Flemish SMEs in a government-backed programme of structured networking (1992–2008): membership "significantly positively correlated" with net asset and value-added growth. [research, 10, abstract-only; observational; one region] Italian firms in formal network contracts: higher value added and exports, no gain in profit; larger gains for smaller firms. [research, 11, abstract-only; working paper; Italy]

## 4. Rules for regulated professions

- Quebec dietitians may not "receive, pay or agree to pay any benefit, rebate or commission" tied to their practice, beyond their fee and modest thanks (art. 60). They must avoid real or apparent conflicts of interest (art. 52) and may refer a patient only when the patient's interest requires it (art. 38). [law, 12, read; fetch summary; Quebec; in force 2026]
- Whether a referral-group seat counts as a "benefit" is not stated in the code. A group that expects you to pass referrals in exchange for receiving them sits close to art. 60. [derived from 12; not an ethics opinion]
- North Carolina: a lawyer may join a for-profit referral group only if passing referrals is not "a condition of membership", may not refer on a quid pro quo basis, and may not let members hand out the lawyer's cards. [ethics opinion, 13; one state; 2006]
- Washington: a group with mandatory referrals and loss of membership for making none "likely" breaches the rule against giving value for recommendations. [ethics opinion, 14; one state; 2002; advisory]
- BNI policy and these opinions pull opposite ways: the group expects referrals; two bars say a required referral is the problem. [derived from 3, 13, 14]

## Agreement and disagreement with existing notes

- Agrees with `professional-services.md` line 39 that paying for referrals is restricted in regulated professions. This note adds a Quebec article and two US opinions, and shows that a reciprocal duty, not only cash, can be the issue.
- Disagrees in kind with the `build-a-network` bet's text, which says "don't start pitching". A referral group is organised pitching with an obligation attached. It is a different bet, not a room for that one.

## What this changes

Vendor claims only on outcomes, so **no new bet**. A bet needs an evidence grade and a judge window, and nothing independent measures what members get. A one-year non-refundable term also makes a 6-month stop line hard to follow.

**One note on `build-a-network`, for regulated professions only**, where the evidence is law, not vendor data. In `src/lib/smallBetsCatalog.ts`, add to `build-a-network`:

```ts
notes: [{
  match: REGULATED,
  note: "if you're asked to join a referral group (BNI, a chamber leads group), check your code first: Quebec dietitians may not give or receive any benefit or commission for referrals (art. 60), and two US bars say a lawyer may not join a group that requires passing referrals. What members get is known only from the groups' own self-reported figures",
}],
```

Same with `category: "freelance designer"` → reason does not match it. Run today on `dist/`, both profiles get `build-a-network` in fitsNow with reason "works with no users".

No general note for other profiles: it would carry only vendor numbers.
