## 1. Coworking: some members subcontract each other, in some spaces

- In nine Austin coworking spaces (16 owners, 17 members interviewed, 2008–2011), three members of one space said they had subcontracted others there. One said he trusted them because "I've seen them make agreements and deliver or not deliver." [research, 1, read; fetch summary; qualitative; one city; self-reported]
- In a second space, members saw possible partnerships but had formed none. In a third, client-facing space, none had business ties with each other; they "sought customers rather than partners." Only 5 of 17 described coworking as working on projects together. [research, 1, read; fetch summary; qualitative; convenience sample of 17]
- Spinuzzi names two patterns. "Good partners": freelancers use the space to form short-lived teams and subcontract. "Good neighbours": people work side by side and see their own clients there. Every space had both; one usually dominated. [research, 1, read; fetch summary; one author]
- In the 2011 Deskmag survey, 38% said their income had risen since joining; 2.5% said it fell. [corrected 2026-10-28: source 7 says "38% of respondents suggested that working in a coworking space directly improved their income" and "only 2.5% believed their income had decreased since joining"; respondent count not on that page] Members reported 3.6 "useful" new acquaintances on average in two months, and 84% attended events. The survey did not ask, in what was seen, whether any of this came from other members. [vendor, 6–8, fetch summary; self-selected online sample of 1,500+; self-reported; denominators per item not stated; no comparison group]
- The University of Michigan studies of coworking measure thriving and sense of community, not work between members. [research, 9–10; abstract and press piece only]

So coworking leads to paid work between members sometimes, through subcontracting among people whose work fits together. No study seen counts how often, or compares coworking with other rooms. [derived from 1, 6–11]

## 2. At an open mixer, people mostly talk to people they know

- At an 80-minute business mixer for 92 executive MBA students, electronic name tags logged who faced whom. Guests had known about a third of the room beforehand, yet their 14 encounters on average were split "roughly evenly between pre-mixer friends and strangers." A strong prior friendship made a meeting about 223% more likely. [research, 2–3, read; fetch summary; one event, n=92; observational]
- 95% of guests said they came to meet new people. The authors' answer to their title: "no (or not very much) in terms of meeting new people". They suggest that guests who come without their friends meet more strangers. [research, 2, read; fetch summary; the "without friends" point is the authors' suggestion, not a tested result]
- Guests did mix across sex, race and job type. So the failure is about new people, not about different people. [research, 2; one event]

## 3. A small, structured session with people on your topic raises working together

- At a Harvard medical imaging symposium, 402 researchers were randomly placed in 90-minute breakout rooms. Pairs put in the same room applied for a grant together more often: 0.28% of pairs against 0.16% (26,789 pairs), about 70–75% more. [research, 4–5, read and abstract; fetch summary; randomized; one event; scientists, grant applications, not paid work; significant at 10% in the working paper]
- The effect was larger for pairs in the same clinical area: from 0.35% to 0.94%. It was not larger for pairs whose skills complemented each other. [research, 5, read; fetch summary; one experiment]
- The base rates are tiny. Being in the room helps, but almost no pair works together either way. [derived from 5]

## 4. Agreement with other notes

- Agrees with `solo-first-clients.md` §6: work moves through acquaintances, and evidence is about ties, not clients. Nothing here measures clients either.
- Partly disagrees with the bet's flat list. The list treats the rooms as equal. The evidence says behaviour in the room, and sharing a topic, matter more than the type of room. No source ranks coworking against meetups, classes or clubs.
- `community-and-hobby-products.md` does not cover in-person rooms; no overlap.

## What this does not settle

- Whether any kind of room leads to paid work more often than another. No study compares them.
- How often coworking members get paid work from each other. Spinuzzi gives stories, Deskmag gives self-reported income with no comparison group.
- Whether talking to strangers at mixers leads to work. Ingram & Morris measured meetings, not outcomes.
- Whether the grant-application result carries over to clients for a solo practitioner. Different people, different outcome.
- Bouncken & Reuschl (2018) on coworking and opportunism was not seen.
