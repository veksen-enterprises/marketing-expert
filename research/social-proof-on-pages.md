# Does showing reviews, ratings, counts, testimonials or logos on a page change conversion, and when does it backfire? (checked 2026-10-27)

Question: what controlled or field evidence shows that reviews, star ratings, review counts, testimonials, customer logos or "N users" lines on a web page change what visitors do, how large the change is, and when it backfires (in particular, whether showing a small number hurts)? It would change two things: whether `audit_page` should detect proof on the page (AggregateRating/Review JSON-LD, number-plus-noun claims like "10,000 teams", testimonial blocks with a name and role, logo strips found by alt text) and raise an info flag when none is found, and how strong that flag may be; and the tags in the "Proof and specificity" section of `knowledge/landing-pages-and-cro.md`.

## 1. Popularity information raises choices, but the field effects are small

- On LinkedIn, showing job seekers how many people had already applied raised finished applications by 3.5% relative (2.536% to about 2.63%; +0.089 points). Starts rose 2.0–3.6% relative. About 2.3 million users, randomized. [research, 3; RCT; one platform, 2012]
- On a wedding-vendor directory, showing past click counts and ranking by them raised clicks for vendors that were already popular. [research, 1; field experiment; clicks, not sales]
- In comment voting, one random early up-vote made the next vote 32% more likely to be positive and raised final scores by 25% on average. A random early down-vote was corrected and had no lasting effect. [research, 5; abstract only]
- One random early unit of success (a donation, a "very helpful" rating, 12 petition signatures) raised later success on three of four sites. Kickstarter: 70% of treated projects got further funding vs 39% of controls. The Change.org difference was not significant. [research, 4; field experiments, 200–521 units each]
- Showing download counts made song success more unequal and less predictable than when no counts were shown. A stronger signal (songs sorted by downloads) made both worse. [research, 2; online experiment, n=14,341]
- Put together: shown popularity moves behaviour, but where the outcome is a real conversion on a real site with many users (source 3) the lift is a few percent, not the doublings vendor posts report. [derived from 1–5]

## 2. Does a small number hurt? No controlled evidence of a threshold

- The LinkedIn study tested whether the effect depended on the count shown. It found no consistent pattern: the interaction was "neither consistently signed nor significant", with only weak (10% level) evidence that very high counts deterred some applicants. [research, 3]
- Most of the gain comes from the first unit of proof. Kickstarter: going from zero to one donor added about 4.3 later donations; each of the next three added about 1.7, and one vs four donors did not differ significantly. Epinions: one rating vs four did not differ significantly. [research, 4]
- Low popularity can hurt in a ranked list of alternatives. Unpopular vendors with broad appeal "suffer a greater loss in clicks" than unpopular niche vendors once counts are shown. That is a ranked comparison on a directory, not a count shown alone on one firm's page. [research, 1]
- In two-option choices, people pick the option with more reviews, even when both are rated low and the bigger count makes the worse product more likely. [research, 9; hypothetical choices] When choosing among the best options on one review attribute, preference shifts toward the lower-rated option with more reviews as the review count becomes more diagnostic. [research, 10; abstract]
- Restaurants' revenue response to a Yelp rating depends on how many reviews back it, and is stronger when the rating "contains more information". [research, 11; observational, regression discontinuity]
- The reverse can also happen. Near a fundraising goal, people gave more when told there were few donors (23) than many (1,923): $14.06 vs $11.31 (d=.38) in one lab study, $13.66 vs $12.11 (d=.21) in another. Far from the goal there was no significant difference. [research, 12; lab studies plus observational platform data]
- A low descriptive norm ("some people do X") still helped compared with nothing in a conference carbon-offset experiment, reportedly "up to a 200% increase in offsetting". [research, 17; working paper, abstract]. Saying the number is growing beat a static minority statement: 34% vs 17% ordered a meatless lunch. [research, 16; press release]
- Practitioner tests point both ways. A blog's share button with 0 tweets got more than twice the clicks of one showing 776 (one test, 36 hours); three other count tests showed no significant difference. [practitioner, 21] A vendor reports that adding a large subscriber count to a sign-up modal cut sign-ups 11.2%, while removing testimonials from order pages cut orders. No sample sizes. [vendor, 22]
- So "only 12 users hurts" is not supported by any controlled study found here. It is a plausible guess with anecdotes on both sides. The evidence supports a weaker claim: when a visitor can compare options side by side, the option with the larger count tends to win. [derived from 1, 3, 4, 9, 12, 21, 22]

## 3. Reviews and ratings: the well-known figures are observational

- Spiegel's "five reviews = 270% higher purchase likelihood than none" comes from one high-end gift retailer, tracking sales as each product accumulated reviews. No randomization or control group is described. PowerReviews, which sells review software, partnered and supplied data. Retailers are unnamed and full papers are "available upon request". [vendor-partnered, 7; observational]
- The same report says displaying reviews raised conversion 190% for lower-priced and 380% for higher-priced products; the web page's chart captions say about 200% and "almost 400%". [vendor-partnered, 6, 7] Gains diminish "rapidly after the first five reviews". [vendor-partnered, 6]
- The rating peak: purchase probability rises with the star rating to about 4.2–4.5, then falls. [research, 8; observational, three sites, four categories] Spiegel's web page and report text say 4.0–4.7; its own summary slide says 4.2–4.5. [vendor-partnered, 6, 7]
- A one-star rise on Yelp went with 5–9% more revenue for independent restaurants. Chain restaurants showed no effect. [research, 11; observational, regression discontinuity] Like the narrow-appeal result in source 1, proof matters most where the visitor knows least about the seller. [derived from 1, 11]
- The "verified buyer" badge figure (+15% odds of purchase) is from the vendor-partnered page with no method stated. [vendor-partnered, 6]

## 4. Testimonials: two randomized trials, one null

- On a live registration page of a free online therapy program, a past-user testimonial, a health-professional testimonial and no testimonial produced the same registration rate: 45.6%, 45.5%, 45.5% (14,267 visits; p>0.05). The authors say testimonials may have "limited use" for well-established programs. [research, 13; RCT, abstract]
- In an online survey, fictional testimonials raised students' stated intention to use a stress-management app over information alone (d=0.50). Testimonials from fellow students worked best, through perceived similarity. [research, 14; RCT, n=231; intentions, not behaviour]
- So the one behavioural test found nothing, and the positive result is about intentions. The similarity finding fits practitioner advice to match testimonials to the audience; no controlled test of "name, role, company and a specific result" versus generic praise was found. [derived from 13, 14]
- Proof from a group the visitor does not identify with can backfire in polarized settings: Biden supporters told that most Trump supporters didn't wear masks became more willing to wear them (d=0.16–0.22, replicated twice). In a non-polarized task the norm worked as usual. [research, 15; online experiments, intentions] This is far from customer logos, so treat it as a caution, not a rule.

## 5. Customer logos: no controlled evidence

- No peer-reviewed or large field study of customer logo strips was found. The one test located ran logos, numbers and reviews as trust bars on nine local-trades sites: reviews "won by 18%" on form fills, with the winner varying by trade, no visitor counts and no significance stated. [vendor, 23; A/B anecdote]
- A loosely related retail RCT found that showing one cue (price or review) next to recommendations sold more than showing none or both. [research, 24; university summary only] It does not test logos or proof lines and is cited only as a reminder that more badges are not always better.

## 6. Rules that apply to proof on a page

- Google (2019): "We call reviews 'self-serving' when a review about entity A is placed on the website of entity A." Such reviews marked up as LocalBusiness or Organization may no longer show review stars in results; Product markup on product pages is not expected to be affected. [first-party via secondary quote, 19; 2019; the current review-snippet documentation was not read]
- US FTC rule, effective 21 October 2024: it is unlawful "for a business to write, create, or sell" fake or false consumer reviews or testimonials, including ones from people who don't exist, didn't use the product, or whose experience is misrepresented. Insider reviews, review suppression and fake social-media indicators (bought followers or views) are covered in other sections. [first-party, 20; section titles and gist from the fetch summary]

## What this changes

1. **`audit_page`: report proof found, as data, not as a grade.** Input: the page HTML (and the rendered DOM in render mode). Output: a `proof` object listing what was found:
   - `ratings`: AggregateRating or Review in JSON-LD, with ratingValue and ratingCount/reviewCount; plus text matches like "4.8/5", "4.8 out of 5", "4.8 stars".
   - `counts`: a number followed within three words by a customer noun (customers, users, teams, companies, businesses, members, subscribers, developers, downloads, installs, reviews, clients, stores), e.g. "10,000+ teams", "2.3k users". Report the number and phrase as found.
   - `testimonials`: a `blockquote`, a `figure` with `figcaption`, or an element whose class contains testimonial/review/quote, holding quoted text followed by a short name-like line. Report whether a role or company appears.
   - `logos`: three or more `img` elements under one parent whose alt or src contains "logo", or that follow a heading matching "trusted by", "used by", "customers include", "as seen in".
2. **Info flag when nothing is found, worded no stronger than the evidence.** Rule: `proof` is empty → severity `info`, never `warning`. Suggested text: "No reviews, ratings, customer counts, testimonials or customer logos found in the HTML (heuristic). Not checked: proof inside images, review widgets loaded in iframes or by script (use render=true), and other pages. Evidence on adding proof is mixed: on one site with 2.3 million users, showing how many others had applied raised completions about 3.5%; on a registration page with 14,267 visits, testimonials changed nothing. If the business has real customers, test adding proof near the main claim." Evidence grade: [research; mixed; small effects in field RCTs] (sources 3, 13).
3. **No conditional "small number" flag.** No controlled study found that showing a small count lowers conversion, and the LinkedIn RCT found no consistent count effect (§2). The tool should report the count it found and must not call it too low. If the advisor discusses it, the honest line is: "the first few units of proof seem to carry most of the gain [research; 4 field experiments]; whether a small number hurts is untested; anecdotes point both ways."
4. **No rating-ceiling flag.** The 4.2–4.5 peak is observational (three sites, four categories) and not causal, so a "5.0 looks fake" flag would be stronger than the evidence. Report the rating only.
5. **New structured-data check (first-party, needs one re-read).** If AggregateRating or Review is nested in Organization or LocalBusiness (including subtypes) on that business's own site → `info`: "Google said in 2019 it does not show review stars for 'self-serving' reviews (reviews of a business placed on its own site, marked up as LocalBusiness or Organization). The markup may stay valid but is unlikely to earn stars." Before building, read Google's current review-snippet page (the `structuredData.ts` source `review-snippet`) and quote the rule from there; the 2019 wording here came through a secondary site.
6. **No check for fake reviews.** A tool can't tell fake from real; the FTC rule is a reason for the advisor never to suggest invented counts or testimonials, which `knowledge/behavioral-science.md` already says.
7. **Playbook `knowledge/landing-pages-and-cro.md`, "Proof and specificity":**
   - The section tag `[research]` overstates it; make it `[research, mixed]`.
   - Replace "No peer-reviewed effect size exists for testimonials on landing pages" with: "Two randomized trials of testimonials: on a live registration page they changed nothing (45.6% vs 45.5%, 14,267 visits) [research; RCT; one health site]; in a survey, testimonials from similar people raised stated intent (d=0.50) [research; RCT, n=231; intentions]. Specific, similar-to-the-reader testimonials are practitioner advice."
   - Add: "Showing how many others already acted raised completions about 3.5% on LinkedIn [research; RCT, n≈2.3M; one platform], and the first few units of proof carry most of the gain [research; 4 field experiments]. Proof matters most where visitors know least about the seller: independent restaurants, not chains [research; observational, Yelp]."
   - Any use of the Spiegel 270% figure must carry `[vendor-partnered; observational; one retailer]`.

## Open questions
