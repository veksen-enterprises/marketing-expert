# Event-moment products: selling something about an event to the people who lived it

Research date: 2026-10-13. **Not legal advice.**

**Scope.** Products and assets tied to one public event, aimed at the people who took part in it or followed it, and put in front of them within days: personalised race prints (course map, name, time), concert and setlist prints, single-game prints, and the general pattern behind them. Covers why people buy keepsakes of experiences, what personalisation adds, field sizes and runner spending, how fast interest fades, real businesses (official partners and unofficial sellers), Meta location and interest targeting and its ad rules, print-on-demand order APIs, event data sources, OpenStreetMap for printed maps, race-result privacy, and the trademark, copyright and publicity law that limits using an event's name, marks, maps, photos and people.

## Part D: Demand and real cases

### A. Why people buy keepsakes of experiences

**D1. Kahneman, D., Fredrickson, B. L., Schreiber, C. A., & Redelmeier, D. A. "When More Pain Is Preferred to Less: Adding a Better End." *Psychological Science* 4(6), 401–405, 1993.** JSTOR stable URL https://www.jstor.org/stable/40062570 (PDF read via a University of Zurich course copy: https://ius.uzh.ch/dam/jcr:5ae9adc9-61ec-4174-b37c-4b752f36c23b/...).
Subjects held one hand in 14 °C water for 60 s (short trial). In the long trial they held the other hand in 14 °C water for 60 s and then for 30 s more while the water warmed to 15 °C. Asked which trial to repeat, "most subjects (22 of 32, or 69%) preferred to repeat the long trial". Among the 21 subjects whose discomfort ratings dropped at the end of the long trial, 17 (81%) preferred it. The paper treats this as evidence that remembered evaluations depend heavily on the worst moment and the final moment, with duration largely neglected (the peak-end pattern). Relevance: a race or concert is remembered by its peak and its ending, and a finish time or final score is the end moment itself. This is an inference; the study does not test purchases.
[research; lab experiment, n=32] [read 2026-10-13]

**D2. Van Boven, L., & Gilovich, T. "To Do or to Have? That Is the Question." *Journal of Personality and Social Psychology* 85(6), 1193–1202, 2003.** DOI 10.1037/0022-3514.85.6.1193. PDF: https://cdn.vanderbilt.edu/vu-wp0/wp-content/uploads/sites/59/2011/05/06143905/to-do-or-to-have.pdf
The abstract says "experiential purchases—those made with the primary intention of acquiring a life experience—made them happier than material purchases". The paper reports two surveys and two experiments. In Study 2, Harris Interactive surveyed 1,279 Americans aged 21–69 by telephone (random-digit dialing; analysed N = 1,263). 57% said their experiential purchase made them happier, against 34% who said their material purchase did. The authors attribute the difference to experiences being "more open to positive reinterpretations", "a more meaningful part of one's identity", and contributing "more to successful social relationships". Relevance: a keepsake is a material object that stands for an experience. The paper does not test such hybrid objects.
[research; 2 surveys + 2 experiments, national survey n=1,263] [read 2026-10-13]

**D3. Swanson, K. K., & Timothy, D. J. "Souvenirs: Icons of meaning, commercialization and commoditization." *Tourism Management* 33(3), 489–499, 2012.** DOI 10.1016/j.tourman.2011.10.007. Abstract via RePEc/IDEAS: https://ideas.repec.org/a/eee/touman/v33y2012i3p489-499.html
This is a literature review, not an empirical study. The abstract opens "This article examines the development of souvenirs research in tourism studies" and organises the field into historical perspectives, "souvenirs as messengers of meaning", souvenirs as tradable commodities, and commodification of souvenirs and handicrafts. It also covers supply and demand (producers and distribution) and suggests future research. The abstract reports no effect sizes or samples. The ScienceDirect page returned HTTP 403.
[research; review] [read 2026-10-13; abstract] (full text [blocked: ScienceDirect 403])

**D4. Sthapit, E., & Björk, P. "Relative contribution of souvenirs on memorability of a trip experience and revisit intentions: a study of visitors to Rovaniemi, Finland." *Scandinavian Journal of Hospitality and Tourism* 19(1), 1–26, 2017 (online July 2017).** DOI 10.1080/15022250.2017.1354717. https://research.hanken.fi/en/publications/relative-contribution-of-souvenirs-on-memorability-of-a-trip-expe/
The study used grounded theory with semi-structured interviews of visitors from 14 nationalities. The abstract says "uniqueness, usability and functionality emerged as central elements that prolonged memorability of the travel experience" and that these elements encouraged revisit intention. ### B. Personalization, self-design and willingness to pay

**D5. Franke, N., Schreier, M., & Kaiser, U. "The 'I Designed It Myself' Effect in Mass Customization." *Management Science* 56(1), 125–140, 2010.** DOI 10.1287/mnsc.1090.1077. Full text read via WU Vienna copy: https://www.wu.ac.at/fileadmin/wu/d/i/entrep/4_forschung/full-text-papers/franke_schreier_kaiser_2010_designed_it_myself.pdf
The abstract says "self-designed products generate a significantly higher willingness to pay. This effect is mediated by feelings of accomplishment and moderated by the outcome of the process as well as the individual's perceived contribution". The paper reports five studies, mostly student samples, with WTP measured by incentive-compatible BDM auctions. Results:
- **Study 2** (T-shirt with an identical design). Self-designers bid €6.85 against €4.75 for the same shirt off the shelf, "over 40%" more (n=38/33/38). A control group that did the same design work but bid on an off-the-shelf shirt bid €5.26, not significantly different from off-the-shelf.
- **Study 3** (skis, n=116). Self-design €74.42 against off-the-shelf €45.89, with higher "feeling of accomplishment".
- **Study 4** (T-shirt, n=129). The premium was larger when the design was attractive (+€4.90) than when it was unattractive (+€1.94).
- **Study 5** (watch). High design freedom produced WTP €30.34 against €19.21 for configuring from predefined options.

Relevance: the premium depends on the buyer feeling they authored the product and on liking the result. A print with a name and finish time involves little authorship by the buyer. Whether the effect transfers to "personalized with my data" products is not tested here.
[research; 5 experiments, n≈20–129 per study] [read 2026-10-13]

### D. Timing and attention decay

**D15. Lorenz-Spreen, P., Mønsted, B. M., Hövel, P., & Lehmann, S. "Accelerating dynamics of collective attention." *Nature Communications* 10, 1759, 2019.** DOI 10.1038/s41467-019-09311-w. https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6465266
The authors analysed Twitter hashtags (2013–2016), Google Books n-grams, US box office, Google Trends (2010–2018), Reddit, Wikipedia and citations. They find "increasing gradients and shortened periods in the trajectories of how cultural items receive collective attention". On Twitter, a hashtag stayed in the top 50 "for 17.5 hours on average" in 2013, falling to "11.9 hours in 2016". The paper studies public topics, not participants' personal events, and gives no post-peak half-life in days.

**D17. Dai, H., Milkman, K. L., & Riis, J. "The Fresh Start Effect: Temporal Landmarks Motivate Aspirational Behavior." *Management Science* 60(10), 2563–2582, 2014.** DOI 10.1287/mnsc.2014.1901. PDF: https://faculty.wharton.upenn.edu/wp-content/uploads/2014/06/Dai_Fresh_Start_2014_Mgmt_Sci.pdf

### Open questions / not verified

- **Purchase rates.** No source read gives the share of finishers who buy a race photo, poster or print. MarathonFoto publishes volumes but not conversion. Sportpxl gives €1–1.80 revenue per participant with no rate. A "~10% buy" figure appeared only in a 2010 forum post, seen as a snippet, and is unusable.
- **Post-event purchase window.** No sales-by-day curve was found for event photos or keepsakes. The only numeric decay claim (Flashframe: lose about 10–15% of email opens per day of delay) is vendor-sourced and snippet-only. Lorenz-Spreen et al. measure public-topic attention, not personal-event demand.
- **Personalization premium for data-personalized products.** Franke et al. test self-design (authorship). Whether adding a buyer's name and time to a print creates a similar WTP premium is untested in the sources read.
- **Runner income and education.** Current figures are not verified. The 2022 Running USA income chart did not parse. The 2011/2013 figures are snippet-only, and all Running USA samples are convenience samples of "core runners".
- **Berlin 2025/2026 official finisher counts.** Not found on the organizer's site; 49,831 (2025) is snippet-only from a third party.
- **Montréal 2026 (Oct 9–11).** Only the pre-event 35,000 expected participants (all distances) is verified; no marathon finisher count was found.
- **NYC 2025 figures** are explicitly preliminary ("subject to change after final race scoring verification").
- **Licensing and IP.** Unofficial sellers (Juno Store, The Sense of Doubt) use event and artist names without stating licences. Whether race names, course maps and logos can be used without a licence was not researched.
- **Revenue of any keepsake business.** None of the businesses above publishes revenue, unit sales or conversion. No figures are inferred.
- Swanson & Timothy (2012) and Sthapit & Björk (2017) were read at abstract level only. Neither gives effect sizes.

### 1. Boston Marathon / BAA enforcement

**L1. Boston Athletic Ass'n v. Sullivan, 867 F.2d 22 (1st Cir., decided 1989-01-27)**
https://static.case.law/f2d/867/cases/0022-01.json (Caselaw Access Project full text; same opinion at https://law.resource.org/pub/us/case/reporter/F2/867/867.F2d.22.88-1352.html)
The First Circuit reversed the district court and entered judgment for the BAA and its licensee against an unlicensed T-shirt seller. The court treated the case as a "promotional goods" case: the defendants were "using the Boston Marathon sponsored and operated by the BAA to promote the sale of goods which are adorned so as to capitalize on the race." Because the defendants "intentionally referred to the Boston Marathon on its shirts" and buyers bought the shirts "precisely because of that reference," the court held "we think it fair to presume that purchasers are likely to be confused about the shirt's source or sponsorship." The defendants offered no rebuttal evidence. The court said "there is generally an affirmative duty to avoid the likelihood of confusion" and faulted them for taking no steps to clearly distinguish "their lack of BAA sponsorship." It also said that asking whether the public would think shirts were "authorized" or "official" is "a confusing and, in many contexts, virtually meaningless question." The court accepted Holmes's point that a mark can be used "to tell the truth," but held that intentionally using another's mark "as a means of establishing a link in consumers' minds with the other's enterprise" carries "an unmistakable aura of deception." The permanent injunction covers "Boston Marathon" and confusingly similar designs, "including goods which are imprinted with '19xx Marathon; [runners]; Hopkinton — Boston,' or with 'Boston; [runner/s]; 1988.'" That last design does not contain the word "Marathon."

### 2. Statutes protecting Olympic and major-event marks

**L4. 36 U.S.C. § 220506, Exclusive right to name, seals, emblems, and badges (Ted Stevens Olympic and Amateur Sports Act), via Cornell LII**
https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/36/220506
Subsection (a) gives the USOPC exclusive rights to the rings, its emblem and the words "Olympic", "Olympiad", "Citius Altius Fortius", "Paralympic", "Paralympiad", "Pan-American" and others. Subsection (c) lets the USOPC seek Lanham Act remedies against anyone who, without consent, "uses for the purpose of trade, to induce the sale of any goods or services" the symbol, the emblem, or the words "or any combination or simulation of those words tending to cause confusion or mistake, to deceive, or to falsely suggest a connection with the corporation or any Olympic ... Games activity," or "any trademark, trade name, sign, symbol, or insignia falsely representing association with, or authorization by" the IOC, IPC, PASO or the corporation. Subsection (d) exempts users from before September 21, 1950 and a narrow geographic use of "Olympic" (the Olympic mountains/region in Washington State).
[legal] [read 2026-10-13]

**L5. San Francisco Arts & Athletics, Inc. v. USOC, 483 U.S. 522 (1987), via Cornell LII**
https://www.law.cornell.edu/supremecourt/text/483/522
SFAA promoted the "Gay Olympic Games" on letterheads, mailings, newspapers and "various merchandise." The USOC obtained an injunction. The Supreme Court affirmed that the statute (then § 110) gives the USOC exclusive use of "Olympic" "without regard to whether use of the word tends to cause confusion," and "does not incorporate defenses available under the Lanham Act." The Court explained that the statute differs from normal trademark protection: "the USOC need not prove that a contested use is likely to cause confusion, and an unauthorized user of the word does not have available the normal statutory defenses." It rejected the First Amendment and genericness challenges: an entity whose word acquired value through "organization and the expenditure of labor, skill, and money" may constitutionally obtain "a limited property right in the word."
[legal] [read 2026-10-13]

**L6. Olympic and Paralympic Marks Act, S.C. 2007, c. 25 (Canada), Justice Laws (current to 2026-09-21, last amended 2019-06-17)**
https://laws-lois.justice.gc.ca/eng/acts/O-9.2/FullText.html
The Act is still in force, but parts of it were time-limited.
- **Permanent:** Section 3(1) says "No person shall adopt or use in connection with a business, as a trademark or otherwise, an Olympic or Paralympic mark" or a mark so nearly resembling one "as to be likely to be mistaken for it." Section 3(2) covers translations. Schedule 1 still lists the marks, including "Olympic", "Olympics", "Olympic Games", "Olympian", "Olympiad", "Jeux olympiques", "Paralympic", "Faster, Higher, Stronger", "Citius, Altius, Fortius" and others.
- **Time-limited:** Section 4(1) prohibits misleading promotion suggesting approval by, or a business association with, the Games or the committees only "during any period prescribed by regulation." Section 6 relieves applicants of proving irreparable harm for interim injunctions only during such a period. Schedule 2 (Vancouver 2010 marks with expiry dates) and Schedule 3 (suspicious expression combinations) are shown as "[Repealed, 2007, c. 25, s. 13]," and the coming-into-force note says "Section 13 comes into force on December 31, 2010."
- **Exceptions in s. 3(4):** These include written consent, pre-March 2, 2007 users, and "(g) the use by a person of their address, the geographical name of their place of business, an accurate indication of the origin of their goods or services, or an accurate description of their goods or services to the extent that the description is necessary." Section 3(5) excludes news reports, criticism and parody. Section 3(6) says inclusion of a mark in an artistic work by its author "is not in itself a use in connection with a business if the work is not reproduced on a commercial scale." ### 3. Ambush marketing, association, passing off, fair use

**L14. Trademarks Act, R.S.C. 1985, c. T-13 (Canada), ss. 7, 20, 22, Justice Laws (current to 2026-09-21)**
https://laws-lois.justice.gc.ca/eng/acts/T-13/FullText.html
- Section 7(b) (statutory passing off): no person shall "direct public attention to his goods, services or business in such a way as to cause or be likely to cause confusion in Canada ... between his goods, services or business and the goods, services or business of another."
- Section 20(1)(a): infringement where a person "sells, distributes or advertises any goods or services in association with a confusing trademark or trade name."
- Section 20(1.1)(b): registration does not prevent "any bona fide use, other than as a trademark, of the geographical name of his or her place of business or of any accurate description of the character or quality of his or her goods or services," if not likely to depreciate goodwill.
- Section 22(1): "No person shall use a trademark registered by another person in a manner that is likely to have the effect of depreciating the value of the goodwill attaching thereto."
[legal] [read 2026-10-13]

**L15. Bennett Jones, "Famous Trademarks in Canada" (2006-06-10), on Veuve Clicquot v. Boutiques Cliquot, 2006 SCC 23, and Mattel v. 3894207 Canada, 2006 SCC 22**
https://www.bennettjones.com/Insights/Updates/2006/06/Famous-Trademarks-in-Canada
The s. 22 test has four elements:
1. The registered mark "was used by the defendant in connection with wares or services."
2. The mark has "significant goodwill."
3. It was used "in a manner likely to have an effect on that goodwill (i.e. linkage)."
4. "the likely effect would be to depreciate the value of its goodwill (i.e. damage)."

The Veuve Clicquot claim failed for lack of evidence of likely depreciation. Mattel held: "If there is no likelihood of a link, there can be no likelihood of a mistaken inference, and no confusion under the Act."
[practitioner] [read 2026-10-13] (the SCC judgment itself was not read: [blocked: scc-csc.lexum.com HTTP 403])

**L16. New Kids on the Block v. News America Publishing, 971 F.2d 302 (9th Cir. 1992)**
https://static.case.law/f2d/971/cases/0302-01.json
The court set out the three requirements for nominative fair use: "First, the product or service in question must be one not readily identifiable without use of the trademark; second, only so much of the mark or marks may be used as is reasonably necessary to identify the product or service; and third, the user must do nothing that would, in conjunction with the mark, suggest sponsorship or endorsement by the trademark holder." The court used the Boston Marathon as an example: it is no more possible to refer to the New Kids "than it is to refer to the Chicago Bulls, Volkswagens or the Boston Marathon without using the trademark." Protection "does not extend to rendering newspaper articles, conversations, polls and comparative advertising impossible." The case involved newspaper polls, not merchandise.
[legal] [read 2026-10-13] (law.resource.org returned HTTP 451; read through the Caselaw Access Project)

**L17. 15 U.S.C. § 1115(b)(4) (descriptive or "classic" fair use defense), via Cornell LII**
https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/15/1115
This is a defense where the use is "otherwise than as a mark, ... of a term or device which is descriptive of and used fairly and in good faith only to describe the goods or services of such party, or their geographic origin."
[legal] [read 2026-10-13]

**L18. Board of Supervisors for LSU v. Smack Apparel Co., 550 F.3d 465 (5th Cir. 2008-11-25)**
https://static.case.law/f3d/550/cases/0465-01.json
Unlicensed T-shirts referring to the 2004 Sugar Bowl and the teams' records, using school colors but not school names or logos, infringed. Sample shirt slogans include "Bourbon Street or Bust" and "Sweet as Sugar!" The court found likely confusion given "the defendant's admitted intent to create an association with the plaintiffs and to influence consumers in calling the plaintiffs to mind." It found that Smack "intended to capitalize on the potential for confusion," knowing the shirts sold "alongside officially licensed merchandise." Nominative fair use was rejected, because the defense "cannot be one that creates a likelihood of confusion as to source, sponsorship, affiliation, or approval." The jury awarded $10,506.80 in actual damages and $35,686 in lost profits, and the district court issued an injunction, which the Fifth Circuit affirmed. The case shows that references to an event plus trade dress can infringe without any word mark.
[legal] [read 2026-10-13]

**L29. EU Trade Mark Regulation (EU) 2017/1001, Art. 14; UK Trade Marks Act 1994, s. 11(2)**
https://eur-lex.europa.eu/eli/reg/2017/1001/2025-12-01/eng/pdf ; https://www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/1994/26/section/11
- EU Art. 14(1)(b): a mark owner cannot stop use of "signs or indications which are not distinctive or which concern the kind, quality, quantity, intended purpose, value," "geographical origin, the time of production of goods" "or other characteristics of the goods or services".

### 4. Facts, results, maps, setlists

**L19. Feist Publications, Inc. v. Rural Telephone Service Co., 499 U.S. 340 (1991), via Cornell LII**
https://www.law.cornell.edu/supremecourt/text/499/340
"That there can be no valid copyright in facts is universally understood." "No one may claim originality as to facts ... facts do not owe their origin to an act of authorship." A compilation is protected only for an original "selection, coordination, and arrangement." Rural's white pages were "raw data" and "uncopyrightable facts." Feist concerns phone listings. It does not mention race results, scores or setlists, so applying it to them is inference.
[legal] [read 2026-10-13]

**L20. National Basketball Ass'n v. Motorola, Inc., 105 F.3d 841 (2d Cir. 1997-01-30)**
https://static.case.law/f3d/105/cases/0841-01.json
"[T]he underlying basketball games do not fall within the subject matter of federal copyright protection because they do not constitute 'original works of authorship'." "Sports events are not 'authored' in any common sense of the word." The court distinguished "collecting and retransmitting strictly factual information about the games" (e.g., box scores) from the copyrighted broadcasts. It held that transmitting real-time scores was not "hot news" misappropriation, though "a narrow 'hot-news' exception does survive preemption." This supports treating scores and results as unprotected facts in the Second Circuit.
