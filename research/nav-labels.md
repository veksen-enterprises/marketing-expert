# Can a tool predict navigation problems from link and menu labels alone? (checked 2026-10-27)

Question: `research/home-page-vs-landing-page.md` ("What this changes", item 2) proposes an `audit_page` field `navLabels` (the text of menu and header links) and an info flag for generic labels ("Learn more", "Explore") on home pages. Is there a label-level rule a tool can check deterministically, with no semantic model or a simple one, that was validated against whether users finish their tasks? Or do validated predictions need a goal statement and a semantic model, so the tool can only list the labels and leave the judgment to the agent and a first-click test? It would change the `navLabels` field, the generic-label flag, and the home-page branch of `landing_page_teardown`.

## 1. The validated tool (CWW / AutoCWW) needs a goal and a semantic model

- CWW uses Latent Semantic Analysis (LSA, a statistical model of word meaning built from a large text corpus) to score how similar each **user goal statement** is to each heading and link on a page ("information scent"). It finds problem types and predicts user success. [research, 2, abstract]
- The 2005 paper ran two experiments to build a dataset, wrote rules to find problems automatically, fitted a regression formula for problem severity, and tested it on a third, independent experiment. The abstract claims full cross-validation and reports hit rates, false alarms, correct rejections and misses. No figure was seen. [research, 1, abstract]
- The 2003 paper tested repairs (rewriting headings and links) with LSA scores against the goals, and replicated that users struggle on goals with unrepaired problems. [research, 3, abstract]
- The final version (2012): 428 tasks by 82 participants; outcome was time to find the right page for 32 search goals, failures scored at the 130-second cap. Four variables explained 57% of the variance (adjusted R² = 0.57). "Competing headings" entered first and explained 44% alone. With one competing heading, people "persistently clicked links nested under the competing heading". [research, 4; author's own summary, paper not read]
- The problem types named across sources are: unfamiliar correct link, weak-scent correct link, competing links, and competing headings. [research, 4, 5] Every one is defined relative to a goal: "competing" means another heading or link is as close to the goal as the right one; "weak scent" means the right link is not close to the goal. "Unfamiliar" depends on how often the words appear in the LSA corpus, which is the closest thing to a goal-free rule, but its threshold was not seen. [derived from 1–5]
- The test sites were simulated encyclopedia pages with search goals for single articles, not marketing sites. [research, 4, 5, 22]
- AutoCWW needed the practitioner to type in each link's text and region by hand, and layout position was not modelled, so predictions "did not match data at a more detailed level of analysis". [research, 22; excerpt only]

## 2. Replications: the direction holds, the click predictions don't

- A Japanese replication on a simulated site: task success was 97.7% on problem-free goals, against 72.3% (weak scent), 87.7% (unfamiliar) and 84.6% (competing links). CWW's predicted clicks were 2.29–4.26; observed clicks were 3.92–9.45. The fetch summary says the predicted order did not match the observed one (weak scent was worst for users, competing links worst in the model). [research, 5; 26 people analysed, 5 items per type]
- So flagged goals were harder, but the model under-predicted the size and mis-ordered the problem types in this sample. [derived from 5]
- A different model (MESA) concluded "the quality of the link labels is a greater factor for navigation times than the structure of the pages". Its fit to human data was calibrated on one dataset and reached a rank correlation of 0.85 with 24 condition means from the authors' own study (n=45). Label ambiguity was simulated as noise, not measured from real labels. [research, 6]
- Bloodhound (Xerox PARC) predicted where users go from a goal's keywords and the site's link text, and was tested with 244 users over 1,385 sessions on 4 sites; the abstract says results correlate with real users, with no figure seen. [research, 8] Its 2001 predecessor was checked against judges' ratings, not user behaviour. [research, 7]
- Browsing (as opposed to site search) was affected by both menu breadth and information scent on e-commerce sites; site search did not fix poor menus. [research, 21, abstract; no numbers seen]
- Text around the links matters too: removing the page's non-link text lowered accuracy in one of two small studies (F(1,32)=24.3, p<.001; n=36). Models that score link text alone ignore this. [research, 11; students, mock site]
- A newer model using sentence embeddings reproduces aggregate trends (more steps in deeper menus) on Blackmon-style tasks, with no new human data. [research, 13; preprint]

All of these models take a goal as input (Pirolli & Card's theory defines scent as the match between proximal cues and a goal [research, 9]; SNIF-ACT's inputs are "the user's information goal" and the link text [research, 10]). None of the sources scores labels with no goal and tests that score against task success. [derived from 1–13]

## 3. Can an LLM stand in for the semantic model? Not yet, for success rates

- ChatGPT-4 given the IRS.gov menu and 10 tasks found the right path on nearly every task; 33 people averaged about 51% success (15% to 88% by task). The authors: "ChatGPT is not suitable for estimating how well humans will find items in a tree test." Its ease ratings correlated with people's at r = .62 (p = .06, 10 tasks). [practitioner research, 14]
- LLM agents walking through two mobile apps completed more tasks than people (97–100% vs 88%) and found 3 of the 10 failure points people hit. Given the full path history, their confusion ratings did predict where people failed (odds ratios 2.2–7.5); on single screens, they mostly did not. Each run needed the task text. [research, 15; preprint; small samples]
- So a model reading labels is a better navigator than people, which makes it a poor judge of where people get lost. With a task and context it can point to some likely failure points. [derived from 14, 15]

## 4. Generic labels: deterministic lists exist, outcome evidence doesn't

- Lighthouse's SEO audit fails a link whose whole text, trimmed and lowercased, exactly matches a list. English: "click here", "click this", "go", "here", "information", "learn more", "more", "more info", "more information", "right here", "read more", "see more", "start", "this". It also has lists for Japanese, Spanish, Portuguese, Korean, Swedish, German, Tamil and Persian. It skips links with no href, `rel=nofollow`, `javascript:` and `mailto:` links, and links to the same page. [first-party, 17; list as returned by the fetch tool]
- The docs give the reason as "both users and search engines can more easily understand your content". [first-party, 16] No outcome study is cited.
- "Solutions", "Resources", "Explore" and "Products" are not on the list. No source found tests whether such menu labels lower task success. [first-party, 17; and search found none]
- axe's "identical-links-same-purpose" rule checks that links with the same accessible name serve a similar purpose (WCAG 2.4.9, level AAA). This is the only deterministic "competing labels" check found, and it is about identical text, not similar meaning. [first-party, 18]
- NN/g's case against "Learn more" is expert argument with no study; it says the label is fine when qualified or when the text before it makes the target obvious. [practitioner, 19] Spool's "trigger words" claims (users scan for their own words, then search) come from a keynote with no method or sample. [practitioner, 20]
- One measured counter-example is already in the repo: "Learn More" beat "Sign Up" on the 2008 Obama splash page (`cta-and-competing-actions.md` §6).

## Answer to the question

No. No validated rule predicts navigation problems from labels alone. Every validated predictor (CWW, MESA, Bloodhound, SNIF-ACT) takes the user's goal and a semantic model, and the strongest result (57% of variance in time-to-find, competing headings the biggest factor) came from simulated encyclopedia sites. A replication kept the direction but not the sizes or order. An LLM can play the semantic model but overestimates success. What a tool can check deterministically is narrower: exact-match generic link text (Lighthouse's list, first-party, framed as SEO and accessibility) and identical text pointing to different pages (WCAG AAA). Neither was validated against task success. Judging whether "Solutions" or two similar headings will mislead visitors needs the top tasks, so it belongs to the agent and a first-click test.

## What this changes

1. **`audit_page` `navLabels`: build it as a list, no score.** Input: the parsed HTML (or rendered DOM in render mode). Rule: for `<a>` and `<button>` elements inside `<nav>`, `[role=navigation]` and `<header>`, in document order, record `{ text, href, region }` (region = "nav" or "header"), first 30, with `truncated: true` past that. Text is the accessible name: visible text, else `aria-label`, else an image's `alt`. Output only the list. The field's description must say: "Menu labels only. Whether a label leads visitors to the right page depends on what they came to do; this field doesn't judge that. Items inside dropdowns are included only if they are in the HTML; menus built by script appear only in render mode." Grade: a measurement, no evidence claim. A test can use any fixture page with a menu.

2. **Generic-label flag: narrow it to Lighthouse's exact list and frame it as Lighthouse does.** Rule: a link (anywhere, not only the menu) whose trimmed, lowercased text exactly equals an entry in Lighthouse's list for the page's `lang` (all lists if no `lang`), using Lighthouse's skip rules (no href, nofollow, `javascript:`, `mailto:`, same-page). Output (info): `N links have text that says nothing about where they go ("learn more" ×3, "read more" ×1). Google's Lighthouse flags these for search engines and screen-reader users. No study shows they lower sales or sign-ups; a vague label beat a specific one in one large test.` Copy the list into the code with its source and Lighthouse version in a comment, and re-check it against the source before shipping (it was seen through a fetch summary). Grade: [first-party] for the list, no outcome evidence. This replaces the home-page note's broader list: do **not** add "explore", "solutions", "resources" or "products"; no source supports them. It agrees with `cta-and-competing-actions.md` item 3, which keeps this out of conversion advice.

3. **Optional same-text flag.** Rule: two or more links with the same text (after trim and lowercase) and different `href`s (ignoring fragments). Output (info): `"Learn more" leads to 4 different pages. Visitors and screen readers can't tell them apart from the text alone (WCAG 2.4.9, level AAA).` Grade: [first-party; accessibility standard], not a navigation-success measure.

4. **No "competing labels" or "unfamiliar words" flag in code.** CWW's version needs the goals and an LSA space, the thresholds were not seen, and the one replication did not reproduce the order of problems. The tool should say so if asked; nothing should be presented as a navigation-problem prediction.

5. **`landing_page_teardown` home-page branch: make the label check task-based.** Replace "Flag generic ones ('Learn more', 'Explore', 'Solutions'…)" in the home-vs-landing proposal with: "For each top task, write the task in the visitor's words. From `navLabels`, name the label you would click first, and any second label that seems just as likely (two menu headings that both fit the task are the strongest predictor of lost visitors in lab studies). Don't trust your own pick as the visitor's: language models find the right link far more often than people do. Confirm with a first-click test on 5 people." Evidence: [research; lab, simulated encyclopedia sites; 4] for competing headings, [practitioner research; one tree, n=33; 14] for the model caveat. This is guidance to the agent, not a tool output.

6. **Playbook tags.** If `knowledge/landing-pages-and-cro.md` gains a line on menu labels, tag it: "Two menu headings that both fit a visitor's task slow people down more than menu depth does [research; lab, simulated sites; one research group, one partial replication]. Generic link text ('learn more') is flagged by Lighthouse for search engines and accessibility [first-party]; no study shows it lowers conversion." Existing tags stay.

## Open questions

- CWW's exact rules: the LSA cosine cut-offs for "competing" and "weak scent", the frequency rule for "unfamiliar", and the 2005 severity formula. Only the abstracts and the author's summary were reached. If "unfamiliar" is a pure word-frequency rule, it could be checked with a word-frequency list and no goal; it would need its own validation on marketing sites.
- Has anyone validated CWW-type predictions on commercial or marketing sites (not encyclopedia mock-ups) against task success or conversion? None found.
- Does an LLM given the top tasks and the menu predict which tasks people fail on a first-click test (per-task correlation, not only ease ratings)? A small eval could test this: 3–5 home pages, 5 tasks each, a first-click test with 20 people, compare the agent's predicted problem tasks.
- Bloodhound's correlation with its 244 users, and the 2025 tree-test comparison in *Information and Software Technology*: neither was reachable.
