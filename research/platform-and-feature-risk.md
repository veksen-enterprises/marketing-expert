## 1. Empirical research: what happens when the platform enters your space

**Amazon (Zhu & Liu 2018) [research][snippet-only]**
- Sample: 163,853 products in 22 subcategories that were originally sold only by third-party sellers. Two collection rounds, June 2013 to April 2014. Amazon entered 4,852 of them (about 3%) in roughly 10 months.
- Amazon is **more likely to enter product spaces with higher sales and better customer ratings**, and spaces whose sellers **do not use Fulfilled by Amazon (FBA)**.
- Amazon is **less likely to enter spaces that require greater seller effort to grow**. Complementors' platform-specific investments deter entry.
- After entry, affected third-party sellers **appear discouraged from growing their business on the platform** later on.
- Operational read: success that is visible on the platform's own dashboards (sales rank, ratings) is the entry signal. Low-effort, commodity listings are the most exposed. Using the platform's own services (FBA) correlates with lower entry, which may mean the platform prefers to monetize you rather than replace you.

**Google Photos on Android (Foerderer et al. 2018) [research][snippet-only]**
- Natural experiment: Google entered photography apps on Android in 2015. Panel of 6,620 apps.
- Entry was associated with a **9.6% increase in the likelihood of major updates** for affected apps versus similar unaffected apps. Entry **did not crowd out** complementary innovation.
- Mechanism: **more consumer attention to the category**, not competitive "racing"/Red Queen effects. The spillover was **stronger for larger and more diversified complementors**.
- Read: platform entry can validate and grow a category. The upside goes mostly to complementors with scale and a broad portfolio.

**Google entry threat on Android (Wen & Zhu 2019) [research][snippet-only]**
- When the *threat* of Google entry rises, affected developers **reduce innovation in the affected apps and raise their prices** (they harvest).
- They do not stop innovating. They **shift effort to unaffected and new apps**.
- The authors call the welfare effect ambiguous. Complementors are pushed into possibly less lucrative areas, but some redundant development is avoided.
- Read: the rational response to credible entry threat is to harvest the exposed product and redeploy R&D. This matches "move up-stack or sideways."

**Apple "Sherlocking" (Leyden 2026 working paper) [research][snippet-only]**
- Covers 22 App Store submarkets Apple entered between 2016 and 2021. Controls are never-treated Google Play apps in the same markets. Markets are defined with text embeddings, and the design is staggered difference-in-differences.
- Apple's entry **deters third-party entry by about 22%**, with **no detectable change in exit**. Incumbents shift toward **paid pricing** even though Apple's product is free.
- **Strong heterogeneity:** many markets show no effect. The monetization response is larger when the entry is **integrated into the OS**, and effects vary with how close the app is to Apple's product.
- Read: Sherlocking mainly chills *new* entrants. Incumbents mostly survive by moving to paid tiers and serving users who want more than the default. OS-level integration is the most dangerous kind of entry.

**Apple Sherlocking, revenue exposure (Appfigures via TechCrunch, June 2024) [vendor][snippet-only]**
- iOS 18 / WWDC 2024 features overlapped apps with an estimated **$393M** in annual revenue and about **58M downloads** in the prior year. Trail apps (e.g., AllTrails) accounted for **78% of potentially sherlocked revenue**. Other exposed categories included grammar, math solvers, password managers and custom emoji.
- Caveat: this measures exposure, not realized losses.

**Related literature (titles verified, findings not read):** Zhu (2019, JEMS) reviews platform-owner entry. Kapacinskaite (2024, SMJ) studies how complementor positioning shapes **cross-platform response** to entry. Wang et al. (2024, SMJ) study how platform acquisitions of complementors deter entry on iOS. Choi & Kim model Sherlocking as a platform **information-policy** problem (the platform sees complementor data). [research][title-only]

**Instagram Stories vs Snapchat [press, citing first-party S-1 data][snippet-only]**
- Instagram launched Stories on **2016-08-02**. Kevin Systrom told TechCrunch Snapchat "deserve[s] all the credit" for the format, but "This isn't about who invented something. This is about a format, and how you take it to a network…" [first-party quote via press]
- Snap's DAU growth (from the S-1, as reported by TechCrunch 2017-02-02): Q2'16 143M (+17.2% q/q), Q3'16 153M (+7%), Q4'16 158M (+3.2%). TechCrunch framed this as growth slowing about 82% after Stories launched. Correlation only: the effect is not identified.
- Instagram Stories reported more than 200M DAU within about eight months [press]. Lesson: a platform with an existing network can copy a format and win on distribution.

**Microsoft Teams vs Slack (bundling) [first-party + press][snippet-only]**
- **2020-07-22:** Slack filed an EU complaint. Its statement read: "Microsoft has illegally tied its Teams product into its market-dominant Office productivity suite, force installing it for millions, blocking its removal, and hiding the true cost to enterprise customers." Slack asked that Teams be sold separately.
- **2023-07-27:** the Commission opened formal proceedings.
- **Oct 2023 (EEA) / 2024-04-01 (global):** Microsoft began selling M365/O365 suites without Teams. Reported Teams add-on price: $5.25/user/month [press].
- **2024-06-25:** Statement of Objections, IP/24/3446. The Commission's preliminary view was that tying Teams to O365/M365 breached Art. 102 and that Microsoft's changes were "insufficient" [first-party].
- **2025-09-12:** commitments made legally binding (IP/25/2048). They cover unbundling at lower prices, interoperability and data portability. Duration is 7 years, or 10 for interoperability/portability. An independent trustee monitors compliance. **No fine** [first-party].
- Read: remedies came about **5 years after the complaint**. Slack was acquired by Salesforce in the meantime. Do not count on regulation as a survival plan.

## 2. Access-cutting: API and algorithm changes (dated cases)

- **Twitter, Aug 2012** [first-party via practitioner][snippet-only]. Michael Sippey announced API v1.1 on 2012-08-16. Client apps that mimic core Twitter were capped at **100,000 user tokens** (or 2× current, per contemporary reading). Apps needing more than 1M tokens had to work directly with Twitter. OAuth became mandatory for every request. Marco Arment's same-day reading: Twitter was signaling that third-party clients should not exist.
- **Twitter, Jan–Mar 2023** [press][snippet-only]. Tweetbot, Twitterrific and Echofon broke on about 2023-01-12 without notice. On 01-17 @TwitterDev said it was "enforcing its long-standing API rules." On **2023-01-19** the developer terms were updated to bar using the API "to create or attempt to create a substitute or similar service or product to the Twitter Applications." Twitterrific was discontinued and Tapbots ended Tweetbot. Free API access ended in Feb 2023. On **2023-03-29** new tiers were announced: Free (about 1,500 posts/month), Basic **$100/month**, and Enterprise reportedly **about $42,000/month**.
- **Reddit, 2023** [press quoting first-party][snippet-only]. New API pricing was **$12,000 per 50M requests**. Apollo's developer Christian Selig said that would cost about **$20M/year**. Apollo shut down **2023-06-30**, and subreddits went dark in protest.
- **Facebook Platform, 2012–2015** [first-party filings][snippet-only].
  - Zynga relied on Facebook for **86% of revenue / 81% of bookings in 2012** and **75% / 69% in 2013** (10-Ks).
  - Facebook's S-1-era filings said social-game apps, "particularly Zynga," produced "substantially all" Payments revenue. Zynga was about 19% of FB revenue in 2011 and 14% in 1H 2012 [press].
  - At F8 2014 Facebook launched Graph API v2.0 and removed friends-data access. **v1.0 was shut off 2015-04-30**, getting one year of support where later versions got two [first-party changelog].
- **Google Search as a platform** [press citing vendor data][snippet-only].
  - Panda launched **2011-02-23** and affected about 12% of English queries.
  - Hitwise: Google traffic to Demand Media sites fell **about 40%** between January and April 2011. Google was **about 28% of Demand Media revenue** in the first nine months of 2010.
  - The 2023 Helpful Content Update hit independent review sites, e.g., HouseFresh. The reported traffic-loss figures come from secondary blogs and are not verified.
- **Google AI Overviews (Pew, 2025)** [research via press][snippet-only]. About 70,000 searches from about 900 users. The organic click rate was **about 15% without an AI Overview and about 8% with one**. Links inside the Overview were clicked **about 1%** of the time. Sessions ended on the results page in 26% of AI-Overview searches versus 16% otherwise. The platform answers the query itself: this is feature absorption of the whole content layer.
- **AI model providers as platforms (2025–26)** [press quoting first-party][snippet-only].
  - In early **June 2025** Anthropic cut Windsurf's first-party capacity for Claude 3.x models with **less than five days' notice**. This followed reports of OpenAI acquiring Windsurf (about $3B). Jared Kaplan: "it would be odd for us to be selling Claude to OpenAI."
  - In **Aug 2025** Anthropic revoked OpenAI's API access for violating terms that bar using the service to "build a competing product or service."
  - On **2026-01-09** Anthropic blocked consumer-subscription OAuth tokens in third-party harnesses (OpenCode, Cline, RooCode and others). The tools received an error saying the credential is "only authorized for use with Claude Code." Anthropic cited existing ToS.
  - Lesson: model access sits under acceptable-use and competitive clauses that the provider can enforce quickly when ownership or strategy changes.

## 3. "You're a feature, not a product": origin, Dropbox, and tests

- **Origin** [press quoting first-party][snippet-only].
  - Forbes (Victoria Barret, 2011-10-18; Nov 7, 2011 cover) reports that in **December 2009** Steve Jobs invited Drew Houston and Arash Ferdowsi to Cupertino and made an acquisition pitch, reported as "nine figures." Houston declined.
  - Houston, quoted in that piece: "He said we were a feature, not a product." Jobs reportedly said he would go after the market.
  - Houston confirmed it publicly at DLD, Jan 2012 ("Yes, Steve Jobs called Dropbox a feature"; TechCrunch 2012-01-23).
  - Jobs introduced iCloud at WWDC in June 2011 [press].
  - An "$800M" offer figure circulates in secondary sources and is **unverified**. Forbes's own wording is "nine-figure."
- **How Dropbox survived** [first-party S-1][snippet-only].
  - By the 2018 S-1: **500M+ registered users** in 180 countries, **11M paying users**, revenue of $603.8M / $844.8M / $1,106.8M for 2015–17.
  - The S-1 risk factors name Amazon, Apple, Google and Microsoft as competitors in storage. They warn that platform owners "have inherent advantages developing products… that more tightly integrate," and could degrade interoperability or give "preferential treatment."
  - Houston has described cross-platform neutrality ("free to use whatever platform they want") as core [first-party via interview; snippet-only].
  - Mechanism (synthesis): be the **neutral layer across rival platforms** (Windows/Mac/iOS/Android/web), which no single OS owner will build well. Then move from a consumer feature (sync) to team and business workflow (paying seats).
  - Counterpoint: storage did commoditize. Dropbox's later growth slowed, and Houston has publicly discussed product misfires [press; snippet-only].
- **Feature vs product vs company tests** (synthesis of the cases above; no single authoritative source found):
  - *Feature:* solves one step of a workflow the platform already owns. Value is unchanged if the platform ships a "good-enough" default. No independent reason to open it daily. Examples: PDF chat, OS flashlight apps, single-format stories.
  - *Product:* owns a full job-to-be-done, with its own UI, data and repeat usage. It can survive a default because a segment needs more depth (Leyden: incumbents moved to paid tiers and did not exit).
  - *Company:* owns multiple products, distribution, or a cross-platform position that the platform is structurally unwilling to replicate (e.g., neutrality across rivals). It also has a path to expand before the platform copies the wedge.

## 4. AI era (2023–2026): wrappers, absorption, and counter-evidence

**Absorption events (dated) [press][snippet-only]**
- **Late Oct 2023:** ChatGPT Plus beta added document/PDF upload and an "all tools" mode (browse, Python, DALL·E 3 without mode switching). Commentators named HiPDF, SmallPDF and ChatPDF as exposed. Stripe's Sahar Mor: the move "will wipe out dozens of AI companies" [anecdote].
- **2023-11-06, OpenAI DevDay:** GPT-4 Turbo (128K context), **GPTs** (no-code custom chatbots) and the **Assistants API** (built-in retrieval/threads). TechCrunch the same day: "Startups learn the hard way that relying on OpenAI's tech can burn them."
- **Not verified this session:** later ChatGPT features that overlap startup categories, namely memory (2024), Canvas (Oct 2024), Operator/agents (2025) and Deep Research (2025). Exact dates were not checked.
- **Jasper:** raised $125M at a $1.5B valuation in Oct 2022, about a month before ChatGPT launched. In 2023 it cut its internal valuation about 20% (The Information), cut its 2023 ARR forecast by at least 30%, laid off staff in July 2023, and replaced its CEO (co-founder Dave Rogenmoser handed over to Timothy Young) [press]. Claims that revenue fell from about $120M to about $55M come only from low-quality secondary sites and are **unverified**.

**Value-accrual framing [practitioner][snippet-only]**
- Cahn (Sequoia), **"AI's $600B Question," 2024-06-20**. Method: take Nvidia's data-center run-rate revenue, ×2 for total data-center cost (GPUs ≈ half of TCO), ×2 again for a 50% end-user gross margin. That gives about $600B of annual end-user AI revenue needed. The essay contrasts this with OpenAI at about $3.4B run-rate. It updates his Sept 2023 "$200B Question," and a later "$1.5T" update exists.
- Implication for founders: infrastructure is over-built relative to application revenue. Model providers therefore have a strong incentive to move down-stack into apps and capture revenue. This raises absorption risk for thin apps.

**Counter-evidence: application-layer companies scaling [press citing first-party][snippet-only]**
- **Cursor (Anysphere):** raised $2.3B at a **$29.3B post-money valuation** (CNBC, 2025-11-13) and reported crossing **$1B annualized revenue** in 2025. Later run-rate figures ($2B to $3B in 2026) come from secondary aggregators and are **unverified**. Note Cursor's dependence on third-party models, while model labs ship competing coding agents (e.g., Claude Code).
- **Harvey:** announced **$100M ARR in Aug 2025** (about 3 years after launch; 500+ customers; 42% of AmLaw 100 per Artificial Lawyer). Later reports: $190M (Jan 2026), a $200M raise at **$11B** (CNBC 2026-03-25), and the co-founder citing more than $400M (Sept 2026) [press; later figures unverified].
- **Perplexity:** reported rounds in 2025 at $14B (June), $18B (July) and **$20B (Sept)**. ARR around $150–200M in late 2025 [press/aggregators; unverified].
- Pattern (synthesis): the winners own a **vertical workflow** (legal), a **work surface** (the IDE), or a **destination/brand** (answer engine). They are model-agnostic or multi-model. They sell to buyers who need domain features, compliance and integrations that horizontal chat will not prioritize.

## 5. Defensibility that lowers absorption risk

- **Network effects** [research; background — not verified this session].
  - Rysman (2009, JEP) reviews two-sided market economics: pricing across sides, multi-homing, and the platform's choice of which side to subsidize.
  - McIntyre & Srinivasan (2017, SMJ) review the network/platform strategy literature. They note that network effects do not guarantee winner-take-all outcomes; quality, timing, and multi-homing moderate them.
  - NFX's practitioner taxonomy (the "Network Effects Map/Bible") claims network effects drove a large majority of tech value created since the 1990s. That figure is NFX's own and was **not verified**.
  - Instagram vs Snapchat shows a **larger adjacent network can neutralize a format innovation**. Network effects protect only if your network is *the* network for that use.
- **Complementor-specific investment and effort-intensive spaces** [research]. Zhu & Liu find Amazon avoids spaces needing high seller effort. Leyden finds incumbents survive Sherlocking (no exit effect) by differentiating into paid tiers. Depth beats breadth.
- **Scale and diversification across products** [research]. Foerderer et al.: the benefits of entry (attention spillover) went disproportionately to larger, more diversified complementors. Wen & Zhu: developers redeploy innovation to unaffected apps. A portfolio is a hedge.
- **Proprietary data as protection: critique** [practitioner][snippet-only]. Casado & Lauten (a16z, May 2019) argue most claimed "data network effects" are really **data scale effects**. For those, "the cost of adding unique data to your corpus may actually go up, while the value of incremental data goes down." Data is often less proprietary than assumed, and lasting protection usually comes from elsewhere (distribution, switching costs, workflow). Treat proprietary data as real protection only if it is (a) exclusive, (b) used in a loop that measurably improves the product per user, and (c) hard for the platform to collect itself.
- **Switching costs / system of record / workflow depth** (synthesis). Teams beat Slack in seats via bundling. Slack still retained its value enough to be acquired, and EU remedies now force interoperability and portability (IP/25/2048). This cuts both ways: regulation increasingly *lowers* switching costs on gatekeepers, which helps challengers.
- **Neutrality / multi-homing** [first-party S-1 + synthesis]. Dropbox's cross-platform position. Model-agnostic AI apps that can swap providers (the Windsurf cut-off shows why).
- **Distribution and brand.** Instagram and Teams both won mainly through **existing distribution**, not product superiority (synthesis from the cases). A startup's counter is to own a channel the platform does not control: direct customer list, community, or enterprise sales relationships.

## 6. Platform-dependence mitigation (operational)

- **Concentration limits.** Zynga (75–86% of revenue via Facebook) and Demand Media (about 28% via Google) show that dependence above roughly a quarter of revenue turned into existential exposure when rules changed [first-party/press]. Track the share of revenue, acquisition and data access coming through any single platform. Disclose it to your board the way public companies do in their risk factors.
- **Own the customer relationship.** Third-party Twitter and Reddit clients had users but no portable identity or billing. When access went, users went too [press]. Capture email, billing and account identity on your own systems. Avoid being only a "skin" over the platform's account system.
- **Read the contract like a competitor would.** Twitter 2023 ("substitute or similar service") and Anthropic (no "competing product or service"; consumer OAuth only for first-party apps) both enforced clauses already in, or quickly added to, their terms [press quoting first-party]. Before building, map which clause could cut you off, and how much notice the terms give. Windsurf got fewer than 5 days.
- **Model / provider redundancy (AI).** Keep at least two production-qualified model providers, plus abstraction layers and evals that let you switch within days. Consider committed-capacity contracts with notice periods (synthesis).
- **Early-warning signals** (synthesis, grounded in the cases):
  1. The platform's own **feature releases in adjacent categories** (WWDC, Google I/O, OpenAI DevDay, F8). These keynotes are where entries were announced in every case above.
  2. **API versioning/deprecation notices** and shortened support windows. Graph API v1.0 got one year instead of two.
  3. **Developer-terms edits** adding "substitute/competing product" language, or restricting token types.
  4. **Pricing changes on access** (Twitter tiers, Reddit per-request pricing).
  5. **Ownership/strategy changes** at the platform, or at you (Musk's acquisition of Twitter; Windsurf/OpenAI deal talks).
  6. Your category showing **high sales and ratings** on the platform's own visible metrics (Zhu & Liu entry predictors).
  7. Platform job postings, earnings-call language and product roadmaps that name your category. [practitioner logic; no empirical source verified]
- **Responses once entry looks likely** [research]:
  - Harvest and raise price on the exposed product, and move R&D to unaffected adjacencies (Wen & Zhu).
  - Push into paid, power-user segments the free default will not serve (Leyden).
  - If you have scale, use the attention spike and ship major updates (Foerderer et al.).
- **Exit option value.** Dropbox (2009), Windsurf (2025) and the Slack-to-Salesforce sale all show the platform or a rival may prefer to buy rather than copy. A credible acquisition path is a legitimate mitigation, not a failure (synthesis).

## 7. Regulation of self-preferencing: what it means for a startup

*All items in this section are [background — not verified this session]. Re-verify dates and figures before relying on them.*
- **EU Digital Markets Act** (Reg. 2022/1925).
  - Gatekeepers designated Sept 2023: Alphabet, Amazon, Apple, ByteDance, Meta, Microsoft. Obligations applied from **2024-03-07**.
  - Relevant obligations: no self-preferencing in ranking (Art. 6(5)); no use of non-public business-user data to compete with those users (Art. 6(2)); anti-steering limits; interoperability; data portability.
  - Fines can reach 10% of global turnover (20% for repeat infringement). The first non-compliance decisions were in April 2025: Apple €500M (anti-steering) and Meta €200M (pay-or-consent).
  - Startup relevance: gives EU-market complementors grounds to complain about ranking demotion or misuse of their data, and some steering freedom. It does **not** stop a gatekeeper from building a competing feature.
- **US v. Google (search).**
  - On 2024-08-05 Judge Mehta found Google unlawfully maintained a general-search monopolist position, largely through default-placement contracts.
  - Remedies decision in early Sept 2025: no forced Chrome divestiture; limits on exclusive default deals; some sharing of index and interaction data with qualified competitors. Appeals were expected.
  - Startup relevance: modestly better odds for search/answer-engine entrants getting distribution and data. Little direct protection for publishers or SEO-dependent businesses.
- **FTC v. Amazon** (filed 2023-09-26 with states). Alleges Amazon maintains its monopoly by penalizing sellers for lower prices elsewhere and tying Prime eligibility to Fulfillment by Amazon. Trial has been reported for 2027. Startup relevance: none yet. Seller-side exposure stays as described in Zhu & Liu.
- **Japan Mobile Software Competition Act** (enacted June 2024; full application reported for Dec 2025). Applies to designated smartphone OS/app-store providers (Apple, Google). Restricts self-preferencing and requires allowing alternative app stores and payment.
- **EU Teams case** (verified, see §1). Art. 102 commitments rather than DMA, and slow (2020 complaint to 2025 remedy).
- **Bottom line for founders:** regulation constrains *how* gatekeepers compete (ranking, data use, tying, steering). It rarely prevents them shipping your feature, and it works on multi-year timelines. Price it in as upside, not as protection.

## Risk assessment questions for a founder (synthesis)

1. If the platform shipped a free "good-enough" version of our core feature next quarter, what share of users would still pay? Why, specifically? (Leyden: survivors move to paid power-user tiers.)
2. Does our success show up in the platform's own metrics (sales rank, ratings, API call volume, category downloads)? Then the platform can see us (Zhu & Liu).
3. What share of revenue, acquisition and data access flows through any single platform? Is any share above about 25%? (Zynga, Demand Media.)
4. Which clause in the platform's terms could end our access ("substitute," "competing product," token-type restrictions), and how much notice would we get?
5. Do we own customer identity, billing and contact, or does the platform?
6. Are we neutral across rival platforms or models in a way no single platform owner would replicate (the Dropbox logic)? Can we switch model providers in under a week?
7. Is our value in one step of the workflow, or are we the system of record / full job-to-be-done? What would a customer lose, beyond the feature, by switching?
8. Is our proprietary data actually exclusive and compounding, or is it a scale effect with diminishing returns (Casado & Lauten)?
9. Which of the platform's upcoming keynotes, API deprecations, pricing changes or job postings would signal entry? Who on the team watches them?
10. If entry happens, what is the playbook: harvest and redeploy, go up-market, ride the attention spike, or sell? Which acquirers would prefer buying us to building us?
11. Is the platform's revenue gap (Cahn) pushing it down-stack into our category?
12. Does any regulation (DMA, Japan MSCA, US remedies) give us a lever? On what timeline, and are we prepared to wait that long?
