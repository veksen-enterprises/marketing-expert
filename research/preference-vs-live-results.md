# Does what people pick in a stated test predict which version wins live? (checked 2026-10-27)

Question: `design-lever-checks.md` proposes a render-mode "side-by-side kit" (unlabelled first screens of the founder's page and 3 competitors; ask 10 or more strangers "which would you try first?") and cheap human checks in the `landing_page_teardown` prompt (`src/prompts.ts`) and the teardown checklist in `knowledge/landing-pages-and-cro.md`. Those checks are only a decision aid if a stated pick (preference test, 5-second test, panel message test, "which page would you try") agrees with the version that wins on behaviour. How often does it? If agreement is high, the kit can help choose; if near chance, it is only a clarity aid and must say so. Also decides how strongly a playbook may say a blind pick predicts results.

Hop 1 from `design-lever-checks.md` (open question 2: "No study compares a side-by-side choice against conversion"). Access: every direct curl failed ("CONNECT tunnel failed, response 403"), so pages were read through WebFetch, which returns a model's summary and chosen quotes; every read mark says "fetch summary". Figures are the tool's, not checked against the text.

Already in the repo, cited not repeated: picking is more reliable than rating, and comparative-judgement reliability (`design-lever-checks.md` §4); choice resists flattery when both answers are equally kind (`choice-vs-rating.md` §1–2); the comparative-intent r = .53 / .09 gap is a product-mix effect (`dual-response-and-new-products.md` §2); wording features and GPT-4 pick news-headline winners near chance (`hero-message-clarity.md` §2); looks move ratings more than behaviour (`design-quality-outcomes.md` §1–5).

## 1. Judging which version is better: near chance against real outcomes

- **Perceived persuasiveness picks the winner 58% of the time.** In 151 message pairs, the message people rated as more persuasive was the one that actually persuaded more in 58% of cases (87/151; 95% CI .50–.65; not significantly above 50%) [research, 1, abstract; via fetch summary]. Actual persuasiveness was measured by attitude, intention or behaviour in a randomised exposure.
- **The usual fixes did not help.** Accuracy did not differ by the size or significance of the rated difference, the number of raters, expert versus target-audience raters, or whether the rating was comparative (side by side) [research, 1, abstract; via fetch summary]. A commentary reports the rate rose from 53% to 67% when the rated difference was statistically significant [research, 2, read; fetch summary]; the abstract says that moderator was not significant. Both can be true with few pairs per group; the full text was not read.
- **The defence.** Supporters say most rated differences were tiny, and that the measure works for sorting many messages that differ a lot, not for close pairs: separating small differences is "a fool's errand" [research, 3, read; fetch summary; author's lab developed the measure]. The founder's case (two or four pages, often close) is the close-pair case. [derived from 1, 3]
- **Headlines with a clear live winner: picks at chance.** Undergraduates saw 20 pairs each of Upworthy headlines whose click rates differed significantly in live tests, and chose "the headline that you find more attractive." They matched the live winner 45.2% (202 students) and 51.6% (339 students) of the time; neither differs from 50% [research, 5, read; fetch summary; students, not Upworthy's audience; 2013–15 headlines]. This is the closest found to the kit's format: a blind pairwise pick for oneself, scored against live clicks.
- **Experts do no better.** Forecasts of 53 gym-visit interventions by professors, practitioners and laypeople were uncorrelated with the real effects (pooled r = 0.02; laypeople r = 0.25, p = .07) and 9.1 times too optimistic [research, 13, read; fetch summary; field megastudy]. 1,524 political practitioners were no better than 21,247 laypeople, both "barely better than chance", at predicting which of 172 messages persuaded [research, 14, read; fetch summary; press release; outcome is survey opinion].
- **CRO tests specifically: vendor data only.** 70,149 guesses on GuessTheTest picked the A/B winner 59% of the time (chance 50%), from 13% to 94% per test; about 700 guesses in Kohavi's class, with a "no difference" option, scored 48% (chance 33%) [vendor, 16, read; fetch summary; guessers not described; tests chosen to be surprising]. 15 practitioners scored 48.5% on 50 CRO tests (κ = −0.03) [vendor, 17, read; fetch summary; company preprint]. These are forecasts by marketers, not picks by target users.
- Read together: when people judge which version is better, more attractive or more persuasive, they match the real winner somewhere between chance and about 60%. Side-by-side, expert and target-audience raters do not fix it. [derived from 1, 5, 13, 14, 16, 17]

## 2. Where stated tests do agree with behaviour

- **Measuring a response, not asking for a judgment.** When each person is randomly shown one message and their intention is measured, the direction of the difference matched the behavioural difference in 94% of comparisons (intention vs behaviour) and 82% (attitude vs behaviour); 346 of 401 comparisons overall, 86% (CI .83–.89) [research, 4, read; fetch summary; 317 studies; message variables such as framing and evidence types, not page design]. The same author's earlier figure for perceived effectiveness was a rank correlation of .05 across 35 studies [research, 4, read; fetch summary]. So the gap is between judging and responding, not between surveys and behaviour. [derived from 1, 4]
- **Paired choices about one's own decision, averaged over many people.** Swiss voters choosing between two immigrant profiles side by side gave attribute effects within 2 percentage points of real naturalisation votes [research, 15, abstract]. This is each person's own choice, averaged over attributes and thousands of choices, not a forecast of what others will do. [derived from 15]
- **TV ads: liking picked the sales winner in a small set.** Five pairs of packaged-goods ads with significant split-cable sales differences (+8% to +41%) were copy-tested with 400–500 people per cell. Ad liking was the most consistent predictor of the sales winner (reported as 87% correct at a directional 50% level; how that percentage is counted over 5 pairs was not clear from the summary); main-point communication scored 60% [research, 6, read; fetch summary]. The authors: "smaller than we would have liked", no new products, losing pairs without significant sales differences excluded, no measure can be rejected [research, 6]. A copy-testing firm described liking as "three times higher than chance" on the study's own index [practitioner, 7].
- **TV ads, larger sets: weaker.** In 26 ads with market sales elasticities, liking was not a significant predictor once brain measures were added; traditional measures together beat company dummies [research, 8, read; fetch summary; small n]. Lodish et al.'s 389 split-cable tests "failed to find a relationship between recall and sales" [secondary, 9; original not read].
- **Usability: preference after use tracks performance, loosely.** Across 113 pairs of interfaces, users' stated preference correlated r = .44 with their measured performance [practitioner, 10, reporting Nielsen & Levy 1994; original not read]; in about three-quarters of the cases performance predicted preference [research, 11, read; fetch summary; Hornbæk's account of Nielsen & Levy]. With raw data from 73 later studies, preference correlated .24 with errors and .31 with time [research, 11]. These preferences were stated after using each design; Nielsen warns that people who have not tried the designs judge "surface features" [practitioner, 10]. Bailey reports two studies where designers' preferences did not match user performance [research, 12, abstract]. Performance here is task speed and errors, not sign-ups. [derived]

## 3. Read together

- Agreement depends on what the test asks. "Which is better / more attractive / would work?" agrees with the live winner little better than chance [1, 5, 13, 14]. "Show one version, measure what this person intends or does" agrees with behaviour in most comparisons [4], and own-choice picks averaged over many people track real choices [15]. [derived]
- The kit's question, "which would you try first?", sits between the two: it is a pick for oneself, but made side by side after a glance. The one direct test of that format (headline pairs, "more attractive") was at chance [5]. [derived from 4, 5, 15]
- More testers do not fix it. `design-lever-checks.md` §4 shows picks are reliable with about 10 comparisons; reliable means the same people would pick the same page again, not that the page wins with real visitors. Source 1 found accuracy did not rise with more raters. [derived from 1 and design-lever-checks.md §4]
- What a few testers can find is unchanged: whether people can say what the page is, for whom, and what to do next (`design-lever-checks.md` §2; `stop-line-statistics.md` §2). That is a discovery question, and it does not need to predict a winner. [derived]
- No study puts a web-page preference test, 5-second test or panel test against a live A/B test of the same variants. The nearest evidence is news headlines, health messages and TV ads. [derived]

Output text, replacing the proposed text: "First-screen screenshots of 4 pages, labelled A–D in random order (key in key.txt). Use the answers to find a page people can't understand or clearly pass over. ## Open questions

- O'Keefe 2018 full text not read: which message types, whether any subset (large rated differences, behavioural outcomes only, target-audience raters) reached a useful rate, and the conflict between the abstract (significance of the rated difference did not matter) and the commentary (53% to 67%).
- A monadic stated test (each tester sees one page at random; measure stated intent or a click on a fake door) agreed with behaviour far better in persuasion studies [4]. Whether it does for landing pages, and whether founder-scale samples can detect anything with it, is untested.
- Nielsen & Levy 1994 and Lodish et al. 1995 not read; their figures here are secondary.
- No public dataset pairs a preference test, 5-second test or panel test with a live A/B result on the same web pages. A founder running the kit and an A/B test could supply one.
- Whether a lopsided pick (say 9 of 10) carries more signal than a close one for pages: Cappella's defence [3] suggests large differences sort better; untested on pages.
