## Quebec

1. **Charter of the French language, CQLR c. C-11**, consolidated English text. https://www.legisquebec.gouv.qc.ca/en/document/cs/C-11 — s.50.2: a business must respect consumers' "right to be informed and served in French". s.51: every inscription on a product, its packaging, instructions and warranty "must be drafted in French"; translations allowed but none "given greater prominence". s.51.1: a registered trademark may stay in another language on a product only if no French version is registered, and "a generic term or a description of the product" in it must appear in French. s.52: "Regardless of the medium used, catalogues, brochures, folders, commercial directories, order forms and any other documents of the same nature that are available to the public must be drawn up in French"; another language only if the French version is "available on terms that are at least as favourable". s.52.1: "All computer software, including game software and operating systems … must be available in French unless no French version exists"; other-language versions allowed if the French one is no less favourable (price may differ only for higher costs). s.55: contracts of adhesion (standard-form contracts the customer cannot negotiate) "must be drawn up in French"; the parties may be bound by another-language version only "after its French version has been remitted to the adhering party"; no charge for the French version; exception for "a contract used in relations with persons outside Québec". s.57: invoices and receipts in French. s.58: public signs and commercial advertising in French; bilingual allowed if French is "markedly predominant". s.58.1: a registered trademark with no registered French version may appear in another language in ads, but on signs visible from outside, French must be markedly predominant. s.177: the OQLF (Office québécois de la langue française, the language regulator) may order a business to comply. s.205: breaching an OQLF order is an offence, fine "$3,000 to $30,000" for a company. s.207: doubled for a second offence, tripled after. s.208.4: due-diligence defence. ### What this means for a small product (Quebec)

- **Language first.** A product marketed to Quebec customers needs a French website, French app UI (s.52.1 says software "must be available in French unless no French version exists"), French terms of service shown before an English version can be accepted, French receipts and French ads. English can sit next to French if French is no less available. The free game companion site and the craft tool are "software" and "commercial publications"; the open-source library's docs are probably not commercial publications, but its paid tier's site would be (judgment; not tested). Enforcement is complaint-driven and slow: an OQLF warning, a 15-day notice, an order, then a fine only if the order is ignored; reported fines are $1,500–$3,000.
- **Contracts.** Since 1 June 2023 the terms of service must be offered in French first. Online, having a French site with the French terms accessible meets this. Customers outside Quebec are exempt.
- **Privacy.** Every business needs a named privacy officer (by default the CEO) on the website, a plain-language privacy policy, an incident register, and a PIA before building a system or sending data to a cloud outside Quebec (any US host counts). Profiling, location and ad-targeting cookies must be off until the user turns them on; Quebec does not require a GDPR-style banner for strictly necessary or plain analytics cookies, but the CAI treats tracking for profiling as opt-in. Fitness and health data is sensitive and needs express consent. Under-14s need a parent's consent.
- **Kids.** No advertising directed at under-13s, judged by product appeal and placement. A game companion site or fitness game that appeals to children should not run ads aimed at them in Quebec.
- **Prices and subscriptions.** Advertised prices must include every fee except sales taxes (the order-ahead app cannot add a "service fee" at checkout). Since 12 September 2026, online subscriptions need an easy cancel button, and a reminder 2–10 days before a discount ends. Free trials cannot require the customer to opt out to avoid the full price (s.230(c)). Tip prompts calculate on the pre-tax amount.
- **Contests** no longer need RACJ filing or duties (since 27 October 2023), but the rules must still be fully disclosed (CPA s.233; federal Competition Act s.74.06).
- **Delivery caps.** Quebec has none today; the 2021 caps lasted only while dining rooms were closed.

### What this means for a small product (Canada and Ontario)

- **Email and SMS are opt-in.** CASL covers any message with a commercial purpose sent to or from a computer in Canada, including B2B. Use express consent (an unticked box with a clear purpose and your name), or track implied-consent clocks: two years after a purchase, six months after an inquiry. A published work address helps only for messages relevant to that person's job. Every commercial message needs your identity, contact details and a working unsubscribe that takes effect within 10 business days. There is no private lawsuit route; the CRTC acts on complaints, and settlements have run $100,000–$200,000 for mid-size firms.
- **Privacy outside Quebec is lighter.** PIPEDA has no administrative fines, no appointed-officer publication rule and no transfer ban, but consent must be meaningful (key points up front), health data needs express consent, parents consent for under-13s, and behavioural ads can be opt-out only for non-sensitive data and never on child-directed sites. Breaches with real risk of significant harm must be reported. Bill C-36 is at second reading; nothing changes until it passes.
- **All-in prices.** The order-ahead app must show a price that includes every mandatory fee (only government taxes may be added). Cineplex paid $38.9 million over a $1.50 fee; the Bureau sued DoorDash over the same pattern. Since June 2025 competitors can also bring these cases.
- **Reviews and influencers.** Staff and paid creators disclose their connection in every post; testimonials need written permission.
- **Ontario subscriptions.** The 2002 Act still applies; the 2023 Act's renewal-consent and easy-cancel rules are coming but not in force. Build to Quebec's cancel-button rule and California's rules and you will likely already meet Ontario's.
- **Delivery fees.** Ontario's cap was a lockdown measure. BC's permanent 20% cap applies only to platforms with 500+ restaurants that both list and deliver, so a pickup-only order-ahead app is outside it.

### What this means for a small product (United States and California)

- The repo already covers CAN-SPAM (opt-out email), the FTC reviews rule, COPPA 2025 and the click-to-cancel ruling. New here: California's all-in price law, the statute text of the Automatic Renewal Law, CCPA thresholds and the 2026 rules, the CMIA for health apps, and San Francisco's ordinance text.
- **All-in prices (California).** Since July 2024 every displayed price must include mandatory fees except taxes and shipping. A café can show a separate service charge on its own menu with an explanation; a food delivery platform cannot. Whether a pickup-only order-ahead app counts as "any other food delivery platform" is not settled in the text; showing all-in prices avoids the question.
- **Subscriptions (California).** Online sign-up means online cancel, a button or link, no maze; one save offer next to a "click to cancel" button; reminders before long trials end and before annual renewals; keep consent records three years. The FTC is enforcing ROSCA hard (Amazon $2.5 billion, Uber suit) even without a federal click-to-cancel rule.
- **CCPA reach.** A small product is a "business" only above $26.6 million revenue, or if it buys, sells or shares data of 100,000+ Californians a year. Ad pixels count as "sharing", so a popular free companion site with retargeting pixels can cross the line on traffic alone. Below the thresholds, honouring GPC is still good practice and cheap.
- **Health data (California).** A fitness app that stores health information so users can manage it may be treated as a health-care provider under the CMIA; get explicit consent before any sharing and do not feed health data to ad pixels.
- **Delivery fees (San Francisco).** Platforms must offer restaurants a 15% "core delivery" tier and need a written agreement before listing them; a pickup-only app is probably outside the delivery cap but should still sign restaurants before listing them (California BPC §22599 already requires this for delivery platforms).

## Folklore

- **"Bill 96 bans English websites."** False. English may sit beside French if French is available on terms at least as favourable (Charter s.52) [1]. The real trap is the reverse: an English-only checkout or terms page.
- **"Our brand is English, so the label can be."** Only a registered trademark with no registered French version, and since 1 June 2025 its generic or descriptive words must also appear in French [1, 2, 5].
- **"A public email address means I can email it."** Under CASL only if it was conspicuously published without a "no spam" note and the message fits the person's job [22].
- **"Canadians can sue me for spam."** CASL's private right of action was repealed before it came into force [22]; the risk is CRTC penalties and Competition Bureau action.
- **"Showing the fee before payment fixes drip pricing."** No. In Canada the advertised price must itself be attainable [32]; Cineplex disclosed its fee before payment and still lost [37]. California requires the fee inside the displayed price [46].
- **"Quebec and Ontario cap delivery commissions."** Both caps were lockdown measures that applied only while dining rooms were closed [18, 42]. BC's cap is permanent but only for big platforms that deliver [43–44].
- **"Law 25 needs a GDPR cookie banner."** Partly. Cookies are excluded from the privacy-by-default rule (s.9.1), but tracking for profiling, location or identification must be off until the user turns it on (s.8.1; CAI guidance) [9, 11].
- **"CCPA applies to every site with California visitors."** Only above the thresholds [48–49], but ad-pixel "sharing" counts toward the 100,000-person threshold.
