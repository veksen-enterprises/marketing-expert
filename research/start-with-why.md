# "Start with why": the Golden Circle, the brain claim, and the evidence on brand purpose

Researched 2026-10-06 for a section of knowledge/positioning.md. Scope: what Simon Sinek's 2009 TEDx talk and book actually claim; whether the brain explanation holds; what evidence exists that purpose-led marketing grows sales; how the idea relates to April Dunford's positioning method (already covered in research/positioning-messaging.md, not repeated here); and where a stated "why" plausibly helps or hurts a small product.

Access: the TED transcript, the publisher's book sample, the IPA news page, Dunford's quickstart post, Marketing Week and The Drum articles, and paper abstracts (OpenAlex, Europe PMC, Crossref) were read in full on 2026-10-06. Unilever's own press release (unilever.com) returned "Access Denied" to both curl and WebFetch; its figures come from Marketing Week's relay. Kantar's Purpose 2020 report was not reachable; its figures come from The Drum's relay. ## Sources

### Primary: Sinek

1. **Simon Sinek, "How great leaders inspire action", TEDxPugetSound, September 2009.** Transcript: https://www.ted.com/talks/simon_sinek_how_great_leaders_inspire_action/transcript [read 2026-10-06; full transcript extracted from the page data].
   - Golden Circle: "Why? How? What?" "Every single person, every single organization on the planet knows what they do, 100 percent. Some know how they do it ... But very, very few people or organizations know why they do what they do." The "why" is "What's your purpose? What's your cause? What's your belief?", explicitly not profit ("That's a result").
   - Claim: inspired organisations "think, act and communicate from the inside out". Apple example: the same features, re-ordered to start with "we believe in challenging the status quo", and "What it proves to us is that people don't buy what you do; people buy why you do it." Also: "The goal is not to do business with everybody who needs what you have. The goal is to do business with people who believe what you believe."
   - Brain claim: "None of what I'm telling you is my opinion. It's all grounded in the tenets of biology." The neocortex "corresponds with the 'what' level" and is "responsible for all of our rational and analytical thought and language". The limbic brain is "responsible for all of our feelings, like trust and loyalty. It's also responsible for all human behavior, all decision-making, and it has no capacity for language."
   - Hiring: "if you hire people just because they can do a job, they'll work for your money, but if they believe what you believe, they'll work for you with blood and sweat and tears."
   - Evidence offered: case stories chosen after the outcome was known (Apple vs Dell and Gateway; the Wright brothers vs Samuel Langley; TiVo as a failure that led with features; Martin Luther King). No data, comparison group or failed "why-first" cases.
   - Diffusion: cites "the law of diffusion of innovation" (2.5% innovators, 13.5% early adopters) and a "tipping point between 15 and 18 percent market penetration". He says "The next 34% are your early majority, your late majority and your laggards", which compresses Rogers' categories (34% early majority, 34% late majority, 16% laggards) [category split: snippet-only, secondary summaries of Rogers].
2. **Simon Sinek, *Start with Why: How Great Leaders Inspire Everyone to Take Action*. Portfolio/Penguin, 2009; 15th-anniversary edition 2025.** Publisher sample PDF: https://cdn.penguin.co.uk/dam-assets/books/9781405977593/9781405977593-sample.pdf [read 2026-10-06; foreword, contents, introduction only].
   - Contents include chapter 4 "This Is Not Opinion, This Is Biology", chapter 8 "Start with WHY, but Know HOW" and chapter 9 "Know WHY. Know HOW. Then WHAT?". The book itself does not say the "what" can be skipped; it says it comes last.
   - Introduction: inspiring organisations "have the most loyal customers and the most loyal employees. They tend to be more profitable and more innovative than others in their industry." No figures are given in the sample for this.
   - Introduction: "This book is not designed to tell you what to do or how to do it ... Its goal is to offer you the cause of action."
   - 2025 foreword: the idea spread "by word of mouth"; the TEDx talk "would go on to become the second-most-watched talk on TED.com" (author's own claim).

### Research: the brain claim

3. **Cesario, J., Johnson, D. J., & Eisthen, H. L. (2020). "Your Brain Is Not an Onion With a Tiny Reptile Inside." *Current Directions in Psychological Science*.** https://doi.org/10.1177/0963721420917687 [read 2026-10-06; abstract]. The idea that newer brain layers were added over older ones and brought "newer and more complex psychological functions, behavioral flexibility, and language" "has long been discredited among neurobiologists"; the authors "urge psychologists to abandon this mistaken view". This is the layered ("triune") model Sinek's neocortex-versus-limbic split relies on.
4. **Rolls, E. T. (2015). "Limbic systems for emotion and for memory, but no single limbic system." *Cortex*.** https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cortex.2013.12.005 [read 2026-10-06; abstract]. "The concept of a (single) limbic system is shown to be outmoded." Emotion, reward valuation and reward-related decisions involve the orbitofrontal cortex and amygdala; memory involves the hippocampal system, which does not do emotion. The two operate by different principles.
5. **Kotter, R. & Meyer, N. (1992). "The limbic system: a review of its empirical foundation." *Behavioural Brain Research*.** https://doi.org/10.1016/s0166-4328(05)80221-9 [snippet-only: title and metadata via OpenAlex; abstract not available]. Long-standing critique of the limbic-system concept.
6. **Kragel, P. A. & LaBar, K. S. (2015). "A Bayesian Model of Category-Specific Emotional Brain Responses." *PLoS Computational Biology*.** https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pcbi.1004066 [read 2026-10-06; abstract]. Brain activity from 148 studies (2,159 participants). Each emotion category shows patterns "across multiple brain systems including the cortex, thalamus, amygdala"; the results "are incompatible with ... those that propose emotion is localized primarily in subcortical activity".
7. **Bechara, A., Damasio, A. R., Damasio, H., & Anderson, S. W. (1994). "Insensitivity to future consequences following damage to human prefrontal cortex." *Cognition*.** https://doi.org/10.1016/0010-0277(94)90018-3 [read 2026-10-06; abstract]. Patients with damage to the ventromedial prefrontal cortex (part of the cortex, not the limbic system) make poor real-life decisions while their other intellectual functions are normal. This is the work usually cited for "emotion is needed for decisions". It supports the idea that feeling matters in choice, but it places a key part of that process in the cortex, against Sinek's mapping.
8. **LeDoux, J. (2012). "Rethinking the emotional brain." *Neuron*.** https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3625946/ [read 2026-10-06; abstract and text searched]. Proposes "survival circuits" in place of an emotional brain system; argues these circuits "are not causally related to emotional feelings". Used here only as context: current emotion research does not use a single "feeling brain".

### Evidence on purpose-led marketing

9. **IPA, "Peter Field makes strong case for potential power of brand purpose campaigns", 12 October 2021** (EffWorks Global 2021). https://ipa.co.uk/news/power-of-brand-purpose [read 2026-10-06]. IPA Effectiveness Awards Databank: 47 brand purpose cases vs 333 non-purpose cases. Average "very large business effects": 1.1 for purpose cases vs 1.6 for non-purpose. "Strong" purpose cases (57% of purpose cases): 2.1 vs 1.6; 50% achieved very large customer acquisition vs 30%; 41% very large market share growth vs 26%. Unsuccessful purpose cases: 0.1 business effects, 5% acquisition, 0% share growth. Definition note on the page: "Strong purpose cases were defined as those that achieved at least one very large improvement across this basket of six business metrics (i.e they are defined by a minimum level of effectiveness)." Strong cases "clearly align purpose to product virtues/benefits and ensure this is credible". Field also reports effects on employees, investors and distributors. Awards entries are a self-selected sample of campaigns their agencies thought were successful; not peer-reviewed.
10. **The Drum, "Brand purpose skeptics take a 'very different story' from Peter Field's IPA study", 14 October 2021.** https://www.thedrum.com/news/2021/10/14/brand-purpose-sceptics-take-very-different-story-peter-field-s-ipa-study [read 2026-10-06]. Richard Shotton: comparing "a subset of purpose ads – the well-executed ones [v] all non-purposeful ads" is "a statement of circular logic"; any subset of well-executed ads beats the average. Mark Ritson's analogy: proving left-footed players score more by keeping only the top-scoring left-footers.
11. **Marketing Week, "Peter Field responds to 'emotional' critics of purpose research", 25 October 2021.** https://www.marketingweek.com/peter-field-purpose-critics/ [read 2026-10-06]. Field: "The objective was not to elevate brand purpose, which, as I made clear in my opening charts performed poorly in general." He concedes "an awful lot" of purpose activity has little or no consumer effectiveness.
12. **Mark Ritson, "Good purpose, bad purpose: Marketers shouldn't oversimplify the arguments", Marketing Week, 17 January 2022.** https://www.marketingweek.com/mark-ritson-good-purpose-bad-purpose/ [read 2026-10-06] [practitioner]. The average purpose campaign "was significantly less likely to generate very strong, long-term business effects"; Field later acknowledged that comparing the best non-purpose cases with the best purpose cases would again favour non-purpose. Purpose is "a strategic choice": review market, category and competitors, then decide. "Purpose can exist, happily and effectively, without an explicit position that reflects it to the market." Examples: Pret a Manger gives unsold food to shelters but positions on fresh food; Ritson's own course has a purpose he does not use to recruit students "because most don't care".
13. **Mark Ritson, "Brand purpose doesn't require a commercial excuse", Marketing Week, 17 July 2024.** https://www.marketingweek.com/ritson-brand-purpose-commercial-excuse/ [read 2026-10-06] [practitioner]. "For the most part, taking a stand for others, animals, the planet or any other socio-cultural cause is much more likely to cost you money than make it."
14. **Kantar Consulting, *Purpose 2020: Igniting Purpose-Led Growth*, 2019**, via The Drum, "Kantar singles out 'purpose' as the key to brand growth". https://www.thedrum.com/news/kantar-singles-out-purpose-the-key-brand-growth [read 2026-10-06; press relay] [vendor]. 20,000 consumers and 100 brands; brands with high purpose saw valuation rise 175% over 12 years vs 70% for others; 76% of marketing heads said their organisation had a defined purpose, but about one in 10 could produce a purpose statement and plan. Correlational: brand value growth and "purpose" scores are both measured by a firm that sells purpose consulting.
15. **Unilever, June 2019: Sustainable Living Brands "grew 69% faster than the rest of the business, up from 46% in 2017, and delivered 75% of Unilever's overall growth"** (2018 figures; 28 brands), via Marketing Week, "Major Unilever investor says business has 'lost the plot' with brand purpose focus", 12 January 2022. https://www.marketingweek.com/unilever-investor-brand-purpose-error/ [read 2026-10-06; press relay; unilever.com press release returned Access Denied] [vendor]. The company chose which brands counted as "sustainable living"; the list included its largest brands (Dove, Knorr, Hellmann's, Lipton). Same article: Terry Smith (Fundsmith, a major shareholder) in his January 2022 letter: "A company which feels it has to define the purpose of Hellmann's mayonnaise has in our view clearly lost the plot."
16. **Marketing Week, "Unilever CEO: We will stop 'force fitting' purpose to our brands", 26 October 2023.** https://www.marketingweek.com/unilever-ceo-stop-force-fitting-purpose-brands/ [read 2026-10-06]. Hein Schumacher: "When done well, and with credibility, [brand purpose] can be highly effective" (Dove, Lifebuoy), "But we will not force fit this across the entire portfolio, for some brands it simply won't be relevant and that's okay." Also: purpose "inspires many people to join and stay with Unilever".
17. **John Dawes (with Tait, Beal, Sharp), "Does having a brand purpose actually lead to growth?", Marketing Week, 1 April 2026.** https://www.marketingweek.com/purpose-brands-actually-grown/ [read 2026-10-06] [research; Ehrenberg-Bass analysis of Euromonitor share data, 4 countries; column, not peer-reviewed]. Market share change for five purpose-positioned brands (US, UK, Australia, Canada): Ben & Jerry's up a little less than one point over seven years; Dove up 0.8 points over eight years; Hellmann's flat; Lipton fell 2016–2022; Knorr flat in these countries and down from 4.8 to 4.2 in Western Europe.
18. **Ehrenberg-Bass Institute (Tait, Beal, Dawes, Sharp), brand purpose awareness report, 2025**, via Marketing Week, "Just one in five consumers aware of brand purpose, Ehrenberg-Bass suggests", 12 September 2025. https://www.marketingweek.com/consumers-aware-brand-purpose-ehrenberg-bass/ [read 2026-10-06; press relay] [research; ~3,000 respondents, UK/US/Australia, 14 purpose-exemplar brands; report not opened]. Respondents matched purposes to brands. Average recognition 18% UK, 14% Australia, 22% US, including a guessing rate of about 9 points; genuine awareness "rarely" exceeds 10%. Nike is the exception (32% UK, 52% US), partly due to high penetration. More than half of the brands had communicated their purpose for over 20 years. Authors: adding a purpose "is not imperative" for brand success.

### Academic studies on purpose, sales and hiring

19. **Gartenberg, C., Prat, A., & Serafeim, G. (2019). "Corporate Purpose and Financial Performance." *Organization Science*.** https://doi.org/10.1287/orsc.2018.1230 [read 2026-10-06; abstract] [research; ~500,000 employee survey responses, US firms; correlational]. "We find that this measure of purpose is not related to financial performance." Firms with both high purpose and "high clarity from management" show higher future accounting and stock performance, driven by middle managers and professional staff, not senior executives.
20. **Burbano, V. (2016). "Social Responsibility Messages and Worker Wage Requirements: Field Experimental Evidence from Online Labor Marketplaces." *Organization Science*.** https://doi.org/10.1287/orsc.2016.1066 [read 2026-10-06; abstract] [research; two randomized field experiments; short online jobs]. Experiment 1: information about the employer's social responsibility "marginally reduced" wage requirements on average, with a significant effect on the highest performers. Experiment 2: "44% lower wage bids for the same job".
21. **Hainmueller, J., Hiscox, M., & Sequeira, S. (2015). "Consumer Demand for Fair Trade: Evidence from a Multistore Field Experiment." *Review of Economics and Statistics*.** https://doi.org/10.1162/rest_a_00467 [read 2026-10-06; abstract] [research; field experiment, one US grocery chain, coffee]. Sales of the two most popular coffees rose almost 10% with a Fair Trade label vs a placebo label. Demand for the higher-priced coffee held after an 8% price rise; for the cheaper coffee, a 9% rise cut sales 30%. "There is significant heterogeneity in willingness to pay."
22. **Gneezy, A., Gneezy, U., Nelson, L. D., & Brown, A. (2010). "Shared Social Responsibility: A Field Experiment in Pay-What-You-Want Pricing and Charitable Giving." *Science*.** https://doi.org/10.1126/science.1186744 [read 2026-10-06; abstract] [research; field experiment, n=113,047; souvenir photos at one attraction]. At a fixed price, giving half the revenue to charity "only slightly increased demand"; combined with pay-what-you-want it was "substantially more profitable".
23. **Newman, G. E., Gorlin, M., & Dhar, R. (2014). "When Going Green Backfires." *Journal of Consumer Research*.** https://doi.org/10.1086/677841 [read 2026-10-06; abstract] [research; lab experiments]. Buyers are "less likely to purchase a green product when they perceive that the company intentionally made the product better for the environment"; they assume resources were diverted from product quality.
24. **Luchs, M. G., Naylor, R. W., Irwin, J. R., & Raghunathan, R. (2010). "The Sustainability Liability." *Journal of Marketing*.** https://doi.org/10.1509/jmkg.74.5.018 [read 2026-10-06; abstract] [research; experiments]. Ethical products are associated with gentleness and not with strength; when strength matters, sustainability can reduce preference. Explicit strength cues reduce the effect.
25. **Algesheimer, R., Dholakia, U. M., & Herrmann, A. (2005). "The Social Influence of Brand Community: Evidence from European Car Clubs." *Journal of Marketing*.** https://doi.org/10.1509/jmkg.69.3.19.66363 [read 2026-10-06; abstract] [research; survey, structural model, car club members]. Identification with a brand community leads to more engagement, but also to "normative community pressure and (ultimately) reactance".
26. **Lakhani, K. R. & Wolf, R. G. (2005; SSRN 2003). "Why Hackers Do What They Do: Understanding Motivation and Effort in Free/Open Source Software Projects."** https://doi.org/10.2139/ssrn.443040 [read 2026-10-06; abstract] [research; survey, 684 developers in 287 projects]. "Enjoyment-based intrinsic motivation ... is the strongest and most pervasive driver"; user need, intellectual stimulation and skill-building are top motivators; about 40% are paid to take part.
27. **Liu, C., White, R. W., & Dumais, S. (2010). "Understanding web browsing behaviors through Weibull analysis of dwell time." SIGIR 2010.** https://doi.org/10.1145/1835449.1835513 [read 2026-10-06; abstract] [research; large browser log data]. Web browsing shows "negative aging": "some initial screening has to be passed before a page is examined in detail" ("screen-and-glean").

## What this means

- **Sinek's claim is a rhetorical order, not a tested finding.** The talk offers case stories picked after the fact and a brain explanation. No data in the talk or the book sample compares why-first with what-first communication.
- **The brain explanation is wrong as stated.** The "limbic system" as one emotion centre is outdated [4], the layered-brain model is rejected by neurobiologists [3], emotion is spread across cortical and subcortical systems [6], and the classic evidence that feelings guide decisions points to a prefrontal (cortical) area [7]. What survives: feelings take part in decisions. That gives no support to any particular order of words on a page.
- **Purpose campaigns underperform on average in the IPA data.** 1.1 vs 1.6 very large business effects [9]. The headline "strong purpose" result is defined by its own outcome, so it cannot show purpose causes growth [9][10][12].
- **The vendor growth figures are correlations chosen by interested parties.** Kantar's 175% vs 70% [14] and Unilever's 69% [15] compare brands the company itself classed as purpose-led. Unilever later stopped applying purpose to every brand [16], and independent share data for its purpose brands show little growth [17]. Most buyers cannot name even famous brands' purposes [18].
- **Academic studies say "sometimes, and it depends on fit".** Ethical labels can lift sales modestly with buyers who care, but willingness to pay varies a lot [21]; a charity link at a fixed price barely moves demand [22]; announcing good intentions can make buyers infer lower quality [23]; sustainability hurts when buyers want strength [24]. Purpose alone does not predict company performance; purpose plus clear direction from management does [19].
- **The strongest evidence is for hiring, not selling.** Telling job candidates about the employer's social responsibility lowered their wage bids, most among top performers [20]; firm performance links to purpose felt by middle managers [19]. Both are about people who work for you.
- **Community: identity helps, but the activity is the main pull.** Members who identify with a community engage more but also feel pressure [25]; open-source contributors mostly come for enjoyment and their own need [26].
- **Dunford and Sinek answer different questions.** Dunford says positioning is not your "why" [28]; it answers "why pick this over what you do today?" Sinek's "why" answers "why does this organisation exist, and do I share that belief?" A product page for a new product must answer the first question before the second can matter, because visitors screen a page quickly [27] and B2B buyers spend little time with any one supplier [29].

## Folklore and weak claims

- **"People don't buy what you do; they buy why you do it."** No controlled study supports it as a general rule. The "biology" behind it rests on the discredited single-limbic-system and layered-brain models [3][4][6].
- **"Purpose-led brands grow twice as fast."** Vendor correlations [14][15]; the IPA average points the other way [9]; Ehrenberg-Bass share data on purpose brands show small or no gains [17].
- **"Strong purpose campaigns outperform."** True by construction: "strong" was defined as having achieved a very large business effect [9].
- **TiVo failed because it led with features.** A single after-the-fact story in the talk [1]; no evidence was given that a belief-led message would have changed its results. Not checked further.
- **"Apple communicates why first."** Offered as an illustration with an invented script [1]; Sinek's own wording is "a marketing message from them might sound like this".

## Open questions

- Kantar's Purpose 2020 report and Unilever's 2019 press release were not opened; method details (how "purpose" was scored, how growth was compared) are unknown.
- The Ehrenberg-Bass 2025 awareness report and the 2026 share analysis were read through Marketing Week, not in the original reports.
- No study found that tests a why-first against a what-first headline on a real product page, B2B or consumer. The landing-page advice in the section is an inference from screening behaviour [27], buyer time [29] and Dunford's practice [28], not a direct test.
- No study found on founder posts about "why I built this" and sales or signups for small products.
- Burbano's experiments used short online gig jobs; whether the effect holds for full-time hires at a tiny company is untested.
- Sinek's book chapters 4, 8 and 9 were seen only as titles; their full argument was not read.
