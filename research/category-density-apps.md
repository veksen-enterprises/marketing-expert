## 1. More apps in a category means fewer downloads per app

- Google split 6 game categories into 18 on 17 March 2014; non-game categories did not change. Difference-in-differences on all US Google Play apps, 2012–2014 (about 2.6 million apps) (1) [research, 1, read; natural experiment; one store; downloads estimated from rank and install ranges, not observed].
- The split drew entry: "developers entered 34% more game apps than non-game apps" (1) [research, 1, read].
- The paper's estimate: "A one percent increase in the number of apps in a category reduces app downloads by 0.65 percent." (1) [research, 1, read; elasticity from the paper's model, games only].
- Existing apps that landed in the smaller new sub-categories gained most: downloads rose about 100–120% there, against 20–30% in large ones (1) [research, 1, read]. The less crowded shelf, not a better app, made the difference.
- New and existing apps did not differ significantly in the demand model's key parameters (1) [research, 1, read]. So no evidence here that newcomers are hit harder than incumbents.
- Not measured: revenue, survival or exit (1) [research, 1, read]. The study measures being found, not lasting.
- Mechanism [derived from 1]: a store category is a browse list. More apps on the same list means each one is seen less. The cost falls on discovery, so it matters most for products that rely on store browsing.

## 2. Crowding hurts most among close substitutes

- In an early app platform, more producers meant more varieties of software, but "the average effect of adding producers on innovation incentives was negative." Effects went up or down "depending on whether apps were differentiated or close substitutes"; for close substitutes, "the crowding of similar apps dominated" (3) [research, 1, abstract-only; early handheld app platform; outcome is innovation incentives, not entrant sales].
- Later cohorts "generated less compelling software than earlier cohorts" (3) [research, 1, abstract-only]. Read with care: this is selection of who enters late, not proof that late entry itself costs.

## 3. Being distinct helps, but "distinct from what" matters

- **Barlow et al. outcome measure (resolves the open question):** the abstract's managerial summary says first-app developers "can increase installs" by writing descriptions that resemble the exemplar's while differing from the prototype's (2) [research, 1, abstract-only]. So the outcome is installs. Whether survival or ratings were also modelled was not checked; the full text could not be fetched.
- Finding: over 139,000 apps from 83,000+ first-time Google Play developers, "the optimally distinct entry point is at a high level of exemplar similarity and a low level of prototype similarity" (2) [research, 1, abstract-only; observational; similarity measured from app descriptions].
- A later working paper points the other way on leaders. On mature apps below the 90th download percentile, Sensor Tower data 2014–2021, higher similarity to a cluster's top apps went with fewer downloads; moderate similarity to the cluster as a whole went with more ("close, but not too close") (4) [research, 1, read; working paper; IV estimates, authors report failed overidentification tests in several checks; not entrants].
- Outside apps, distinctiveness only pays at the extremes in uniform categories. Across 69,188 Dutch creative-industry firms, revenue showed "a strong U-shaped effect of distinctiveness on performance in homogeneous categories": middling firms did worst. In varied categories, "distinctiveness has no effect" (5) [research, 1, read; survey revenue brackets, n=2,279 respondents; not software].
- Read together [derived from 2–5]: no study here shows distinctiveness cancelling the crowding cost in §1. The evidence says: be clearly different from the average product, and avoid the muddled middle. Whether to look like the leader is contested (2 vs 4).

## 4. Other app studies named in the lead

- Kapoor & Agarwal study whether iOS and Android developers keep superior performance. They find platform generation changes make it harder; more complex ecosystems help (6) [research, 1, abstract-only]. Their abstract does not test category density. Not evidence on crowding.
- Yin, Davis & Muzyrya: prior app experience and no updating raise the odds of a top-grossing game; more updates raise them for non-games. The authors list "level of competition" only as a "possible" driver (7) [research, 1, abstract-only]. Not a density test.
- Carroll & Hannan density delay (crowded-markets.md §1): no test in software or apps was found. Searches turned up only reviews and non-software populations. The "entering at peak crowding has a lasting cost" bullet stays unsupported for software [derived].
