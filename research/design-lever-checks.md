## 1. Computed page measures: good at predicting "looks busy", weak at predicting "looks good"

- **Perceived complexity and colourfulness can be computed.** Image measures of a screenshot predicted mean perceived colourfulness (R² = .78) and mean perceived complexity (R² = .65) [research, 1, read; fetch summary; in-sample, no hold-out sites]. Colourfulness used Hasler and Süsstrunk's metric (r = .71 alone) plus pixel shares near web colours; complexity used text and non-text area, layout leaves, text groups and image areas. [research, 1, read; fetch summary]
- **Appeal is a different matter.** The 2013 appeal model "explains 48% of the variance" [research, 1, read; fetch summary]. But it included demographics and random effects for each site and each participant, and the paper does not say how much the image measures explain alone. [research, 1, read; fetch summary]
- **The larger follow-up splits it.** With 32,222 participants, fixed effects (computed colourfulness and complexity, their squares, age, gender, country, education) explained 7% of appeal ratings (marginal R² = .07). Adding who rated and which site took it to 47%. The authors: "appeal is largely subjective even after a short exposure time of 500ms." [research, 2, read; fetch summary]. So the computed measures plus demographics explain little of one person's rating. How well they rank sites by average appeal was not reported. [derived from 1–2]
- **The one robust direction: very busy pages lose.** "A high visual complexity results in the largest decrease in appeal"; low and medium complexity were liked about equally [research, 1, read; fetch summary]. In source 2, peak versus high complexity was d = 2.0 [research, 2, read; fetch summary; the paper's figure labels show other values]. Tuch et al. found the same with human-rated complexity, which affected appeal from 17 ms [research, 6, read; fetch summary; lab]. The ideal colourfulness varies widely by group (peaks from 3.6 to 7.6 on a 9-point scale), so "too colourful" has no general threshold. [research, 2, read; fetch summary]
- **Better models exist, but they grade design.** Eight hand-built metrics explained up to 49% of webpage aesthetics in 62 pages [research, 3, abstract; exploratory; in-sample]. A neural network reached a cross-validated correlation of 0.752 with mean ratings [research, 4, abstract]. Both output an aesthetic score, which is what the owner's stance rules out. [derived]
- **AI judges of "which design is better" are near chance on real screens.** In 892 designer-rated pairs, GPT-4V picked the designers' choice 51.6% of the time and refused about 10%; LLaVA 54.6%. The best trained model scored 75% overall but 58% on real app screens. [research, 5, read; fetch summary]. This backs the stance: an LLM advisor's opinion of a page's looks is not evidence.
- **Even appeal does not predict buying.** No study found links computed appeal, or rated appeal, to conversion for a small product (`design-quality-outcomes.md` §5). A trigger built on appeal would chain two weak links. [derived]

## 2. 5-second tests: valid for "what is it", noisy for "do I like it"

- **Looks are judged fast, and the average is stable.** Mean attractiveness after 500 ms and after 10 s were highly correlated, and pages re-rated later kept their ratings; "averaged over users, immediate aesthetic impressions of web pages are remarkably consistent." The same abstract reports "considerable individual differences" in ratings and in how consistent each person is. [research, 7, abstract; n=40; coefficients not seen]. Appeal and novelty judged at 500 ms were stable within raters; usability and credibility judgments were less consistent. [research, 8, abstract]
- **Gronier's numbers.** Attractiveness after 5 seconds equalled attractiveness after unlimited viewing (4.75 vs 4.82, p = .72); only pragmatic quality (usefulness, clarity) differed (5.23 vs 5.64, p = .01) [research, 9, read; fetch summary; one weather site, n=68 vs 78]. The main purpose was named by 97% after 5 s; secondary purposes by 3% or less, and by 9% or less with unlimited time. The author says 5 s is "not sufficient" to identify all of a site's goals and the test probably cannot find usability problems. [research, 9, read; fetch summary]
- **Busy pages need longer.** Visually complex pages shown briefly made it harder to name the page's purpose; the authors advise adapting exposure to the page's complexity [research, 10, abstract].
- **No test-retest study of the 5-second test itself was found.** Gronier reports none [research, 9]; the search found only vendor guides. [derived]
- **People disagree on looks.** One person's appeal rating moved by about 0.6–0.7 points (SD) on a 9-point scale when they saw the same page again [research, 1–2, read; fetch summary; pairs off by more than 2 points were removed first]. Award-nominated sites averaged anywhere from 4.21 to 6.57. [research, 1, read; fetch summary]
- **Read together:** a 5-second test answers two different questions. "What is this, for whom, what next?" is a discovery question: a few people are enough to show a message nobody gets (`stop-line-statistics.md` §2: about 84% of problems with five, on average). Faulkner's resampling shows the spread: five users found 55% to nearly 100% of the problems the full 60 found; ten found at least 82% [research, 15, secondary]. "Do you like it / would you trust it?" is a measurement question, and the single answers are noisy (above). Five answers to it say little. [derived from 1, 2, 7, 9, 15]

## 3. First-click tests: a useful signal for navigation, with a smaller effect than often quoted

- Bailey's studies reported 87% eventual task success after a correct first click versus 46% after a wrong one [secondary, 14; original not found].
- MeasuringU's own data: on five live websites (21 tasks, 750 users) the gap was 78% vs 70%; in eight tree tests (navigation only, 137 tasks, 1,000+ users) it was 80% vs 14% [vendor, 14, read; fetch summary; method summarised only].
- So the first click predicts success strongly when navigation is the only route, and weakly when search and on-page links offer other routes. It tests findability ("where would you click to start a trial?"), not looks. No reliability study of first-click tests was found. [derived from 14]

## 4. Choosing between pages beats rating one, and needs enough judges

- **Picking is more precise than rating.** In image quality tests, forced-choice pairs had "the smallest measurement variance" of four methods and were the most time-efficient for a moderate number of options; for one comparison, 80% power took about 29 forced-choice measurements against 66 single-image ratings [research, 13, read; fetch summary; image distortion, which has a right answer; taste may differ].
- **Comparative judgement reaches good reliability with about 10 comparisons per item.** Reliability .70 needs 10–14 comparisons per item; .90 needs 26–37 [research, 11, abstract]. A later meta-analysis of 101 sessions: "collecting ten comparisons for every representation is generally sufficient" (median split-half reliability .79); adaptive pairing inflates the reported figure [research, 12, read; fetch summary]. Absolute scores on a Likert scale "may prove unreliable" because people use the scale differently; that meta-analysis did not test the two side by side. [research, 12, read; fetch summary]
- **Taste lowers agreement.** Consumer liking tests (coffee, ice cream, pizza) stayed below .8 even with over 100 comparisons per item [research, 12, read; fetch summary]. Designers comparing pairs of UI screens agreed at Krippendorff's α = 0.37 [research, 5, read; fetch summary; 12 designers, 10 shared pairs]. Liking a page is closer to these than to grading an essay. [derived]
- **What a founder's side-by-side can show.** With the founder's page and 3 competitors, a tester who sees all four as equal picks the founder's page 1 time in 4. "Nobody picked mine" then happens by luck 24% of the time with 5 testers, 6% with 10 and 3% with 12. Head to head against the strongest competitor, "1 or fewer of 8 picked mine" happens by luck 3.5% of the time. [derived; binomial arithmetic, computed here]. So 5 testers can catch a page that is far behind only by chance; 10 strangers can catch it reliably enough for a cheap fix.
- **Hide which page is the founder's.** Choice resists flattery only when no answer is the kind one (`choice-vs-rating.md` §1–2). Unlabelled screenshots, shuffled order, and strangers from the target group (not friends) keep it balanced. [derived from choice-vs-rating.md and founder-test-samples.md]

## 5. When design is likely the lever (no new page measure)

- None of the sources gives a page measure that says "design is the lever". The signals stay the ones in `design-and-marketing-in-crowded-markets.md` §6 item 1: a crowded category of lookalike products, a message that testers already understand, and traffic that arrives but does not act. [derived]
- The one computed measure with a strong, one-way link is very high visual complexity [research, 1, 2, 6]. It predicts lower first-impression ratings, not lower sign-ups. It also cannot be judged in isolation: what counts as typical (prototypicality) depends on the category [research, 6]. [derived]

## What this changes

1. **Drop the computed appeal trigger; don't build it.** Computed colourfulness and complexity plus demographics explained 7% of appeal ratings [research, 2; n=32,222], appeal itself has no shown link to small-product conversion (`design-quality-outcomes.md` §5), and the stronger models are aesthetic scores the stance rules out. No render-mode colourfulness or complexity flag.
2. **Build instead: a side-by-side kit, not a score (render mode).** Input: the page URL and up to 3 competitor URLs. Rule: render each at one desktop and one phone width, screenshot the first screen only, strip nothing, label them A–D in random order, and save a key file the founder keeps. Output text: "First-screen screenshots of 4 pages, labelled A–D in random order (key in key.txt). Show them to at least 10 people from your target group who don't know you, and ask: which would you try first, and why? This tool did not judge how any page looks." Every competitor fetch goes through `netguard.ts` like any other hop (security look needed; it widens what render mode fetches). Grade: [research; comparative judgement and forced-choice methods, mostly education and image quality; transfer to page choice untested].
3. **`knowledge/landing-pages-and-cro.md`, teardown checklist item 1.** Rename it "First-screen read" (it is the advisor reading the h1 and lead text, not a test). Add a step after it:
   - "Cheap human checks, if the message may be the problem: show the first screen for 5 seconds to 5 people from the target group and ask what it is, who it's for and what to do next. If 2 or more get it wrong, rewrite. Five people find most clarity problems; they cannot measure how much people like the page [research; Nielsen & Landauer 1993; Gronier 2016, one site, n=146]. On a busy page, show it longer: complex pages take longer to understand [research; Kuric et al. 2024, abstract]."
   - "If the page is clear but visitors don't act, and the category is crowded: put unlabelled first screens of your page and the top 3 competitors in random order, and ask at least 10 strangers from the target group which they would try first. Picking is more reliable than rating one page [research; image-quality and comparative-judgement studies, not page choice], and people disagree about looks, so 5 answers are too few: with 4 equal pages, 'nobody chose mine' happens by luck 1 time in 4 with 5 testers and 1 in 20 with 10 [derived; binomial]."
   - "For navigation, a first-click question ('where would you click to start?') predicts task success, strongly when navigation is the only route [vendor; MeasuringU, method summarised only]."
   - "The tool does not judge looks. AI models pick the better of two real app screens about as often as a coin [research; Wu et al., UIClip, 892 designer-rated pairs]."
4. **`src/prompts.ts` landing_page_teardown.** Step 1 says "The 5-second read"; rename to "First-screen read (yours, from the text)". The closing line "a smaller check (5 people shown the new page)" should split by question: "5 people for whether the message is understood; 10 or more strangers choosing among unlabelled pages for which one they'd try". Replace text rather than add, to stay under the word cap.
5. **Possible `check_answer` / `moveCheck.ts` rule (extends `design-quality-outcomes.md` §What this changes):** when a move's action is a visual change and its stop line is a preference count with fewer than 10 testers, add: "Fewer than 10 testers can't separate a weak preference from luck; use 10 or more, or count sign-ups." Test: "show 5 testers both pages; stop if fewer than 3 prefer the new one" returns the issue; the same with 12 testers does not. [derived; binomial arithmetic in §4]
6. **Tag change.** The crowded-markets note's "5-second test: show … to 5 target users … ask … whether they would trust it" should drop "whether they would trust it" from the 5-user version: trust and credibility judged at a glance were less consistent than appeal [research, 8, abstract], and 5 answers cannot measure a rating.

What the checks cannot see, and must say: whether the people asked match real buyers; whether liking a page leads to buying (no study shows it at founder scale); anything below the first screen; how the page behaves in use.

## Open questions

- No test-retest reliability study of the 5-second test, or of first-click tests, was found.
- No study compares a side-by-side choice against conversion: does the page strangers pick first convert better? A founder running both the kit and an A/B test could supply that.
- Comparative-judgement reliability comes mostly from essays and images with a right answer. Website preference agreement (α = 0.37 among designers) suggests 10 comparisons per item may not give .70 for pages. Unmeasured.
- Tractinsky 2006 and Papachristos 2011 were seen only as abstracts; the consistency coefficients are unchecked.
- Whether the 7% marginal R² would rise if the image measures were compared within a category (pages of one kind) rather than across 430 mixed sites.
- Bailey's original first-click studies were not found; the 87%/46% figures come only through MeasuringU.
