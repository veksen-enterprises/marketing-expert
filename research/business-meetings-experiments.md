## 1. Monthly meetings raised sales and partners, and the gains lasted

- In Nanchang, China, 2,800 firms set up in the previous 3 years, most run by their owners, were drawn at random: 1,480 went into groups of 10 that met monthly for a year (about half a day, with a tour of the host firm), 1,320 got no meetings. Firms averaged 36 employees; about half were in manufacturing and 48% in services. [research, 1, read; fetch summary; RCT, n=2,800; China; young SMEs, not solo] [corrected 2026-10-28: NBER w22951 gives 2,800 (1,480 + 1,320); the 2018 CEU working-paper abstract (econpapers.repec.org/paper/ceueconwp/2018_5f3.htm) and CEU's record of the QJE article (133(3):1229–1282) say "We randomized 2,820 firms"; use the published figure once its text is seen]
- Sales rose 8.1% just after the 12 meetings and 10.3% one year after they ended, against control firms. Profit, clients, suppliers, loans and a management score also rose. The authors say "essentially all treatment effects persisted one year after the meetings had concluded." [research, 1, read; fetch summary; RCT, n=2,800; measured at about 1 and 2 years; one study, government-organised]
- Firms in groups with larger, better-run peers grew more. [research, 1, read; fetch summary; RCT]

## 2. The gain came from both deals and information, and deals needed repeat meetings

- Partners: some managers also met once with a different random group. Counted per manager, the regular group (met 12 times) gave 2.18 referrers (a member who sent a supplier, client, partner or worker) and 1.44 direct partners (a member doing business with the firm). The one-time group gave 0.06 referrers and 0.29 partners. The survey wave for these counts is not stated in the summary seen. [research, 1, read; fetch summary; RCT, 439 managers in 43 one-time groups; China]
- Information: when some members were told about a grant or savings product, uninformed members applied more (about +31 to +33 points), less so among competitors. [research, 1, read; fetch summary; RCT; China]
- The paper does not split the sales gain between partnerships and learning: "these results do not yet conclusively identify the mechanisms." [research, 1, read; fetch summary; one study]
- The one-time meetings were run on purpose, to test how often owners must meet (Section 1). Each manager is compared with two random sets of peers, met 12 times or once, so the gap is a measured effect of repeat meetings, not a guess. The authors read it as trust: "repeated meetings created trust between managers" (Section 4.2), backed by a hypothetical trust game (3.52 vs 0.94). The trust reading is theirs, and suggestive. [research, 1, read; fetch summary; within-manager comparison, 439 managers; one study]
- Over time, effects held rather than grew: sales +8.1% then +10.3%, with no test that the two differ; the peer effect on management halved but stayed. Attendance and its link to effects are not reported. [research, 1, read; fetch summary]
- Fafchamps & Quinn's contact was one-off: panel peers talked more later, but reported no more business friends (§3). [research, 3, read; fetch summary] [corrected 2026-10-28: not one-off; each judging committee of five or six met three times to assess 12 applicants, and judges still reported no more business friends]
- So "keep going back to the same group" can be stated as research, with its qualifiers: one study, China, young firms. How often is not tested beyond monthly versus once. [derived]

## 3. Other experiments: ties form, sales effects are mixed and fade

- Africa (Ethiopia, Tanzania, Zambia): panel peers were 16 points more likely to have spoken since; few practices spread; sales were not measured. [research, 3, read; fetch summary; RCT, 337 participant firms of 1–100 employees] [corrected 2026-10-28: not one-off; each judging committee of five or six met three times to assess 12 applicants]
- Togo: owners paired with peers about 1 km closer kept the tie more often and earned about 10% more profit; a short social-skills module gave 50% more peers and about 20% more monthly profit. [research, 4 and 5, abstract-only; fetch summary; RCTs; Togo; sample sizes not seen]
- Ghana: four months of WhatsApp groups of eight women with weekly one-to-one calls raised profits (26% in the brief, 21% and no sales effect in the review). After three years, "only 3% of the treated entrepreneurs are still in touch", and effects on profit and collaboration were gone. [research, 6, read; fetch summary; RCT, n=1,771; Ghana; mostly micro firms]
- Kenya: a local mentor raised profits 20%, fading as pairs stopped meeting. [research, 7, abstract-only; fetch summary; RCT; female micro-owners]
- About 90% of randomised encounters among 112 aspiring entrepreneurs never became a tie. [research, 9, read; fetch summary; n=112]

## 4. Agreement and disagreement with `solo-first-clients.md` §6

- Agree: ties pass on work; source 1 adds measured referrals and sales among owners. [derived]
- Disagree, in part: §6 favours moderately weak ties. In these studies, deals came from people met again and again (§2), and gains faded when contact stopped (§3). The two fit if "stay in touch" means repeated contact with the same people, not more new people. [derived]
- Neither covers solo service providers. [derived]
