## 1. Google Business Profile: online-only is out

- To be eligible, a business "must make in-person contact with customers during its stated hours". "Online-only businesses" are listed as ineligible. [first-party, 1, read; fetch summary; checked 2026-10-28]
- A practice that only sees clients by video does not qualify. One that also sees clients at an office or clinic during stated hours, or visits clients' homes as a service-area business (`local-seo.md`), does. [derived from 1]
- I agree with `local-services.md` that a profile is the main local listing for practices people visit. Where I disagree with the catalog: today nothing stops the advisor from telling an online-only practitioner to make one.

## 2. Directories that exist for dietitians

- Dietitians of Canada runs a member directory. It filters by province, language, area of expertise and "private practice, primarily individual client counselling". It has no virtual-care or direct-billing filter that the summary saw, and no usage figures. [first-party, 10, read; fetch summary]
- Sun Life's Lumino Health lists dietitians with online booking requests, a "virtual appointments" mark and filters by language and rating. Cost figures come from Sun Life claims. [first-party, 9, read; fetch summary; one town's page; listing terms not read]
- Sun Life opened direct billing (eClaims) to registered dietitians from 1 Feb 2026. [association news, 8, read; fetch summary]
- No ODNQ public directory for finding a practitioner was found (see Not fetched). [derived]

## 3. Do directories bring clients? No usable data

- No source gives the share of a dietitian's or therapist's clients that come from a directory. [derived from 2–14]
- The best-documented analogue, Psychology Today (US, $29.95 a month), has only anecdotes. One therapist said she used to get "at least 5 month, 10 on a good month" and now about one every other month. [corrected 2026-10-28: that quote is from an anonymous Reddit commenter cited by the article, not an interviewed therapist] Another said her referrals from it were down 50%. The company says overall traffic "has remained strong". [press, 12; anecdotes; no survey; US]
- In three interviews with US counselors, one said the directory brought "approximately one to two clients a month". All three named doctors and word of mouth as better sources. [qualitative, 13; n=3; 2015; US] [corrected 2026-10-28: all three named working ties with psychiatrists (and primary care doctors) as their best source; only one named word of mouth; a second disliked paid directories]

## 4. Is dietitian care covered? Partly, with weak numbers

- 27 million people had supplementary health benefits in 2025. Group plans carried 92% of health insurance bought in Canada. [industry association via trade press, 2; fetch summary; CLHIA original not read] That is about two in three Canadians, taking a population of about 41 million. [derived; population figure not from a source read here]
- How many of those plans pay for a dietitian is not measured in anything read. One practitioner says "roughly 80 per cent" of plans now do, up from "20 to 30 per cent", with yearly limits of "$300 to $1,000", "$500 showing up most often". [practitioner, 3; one person's estimate; no dataset; sells services to plans]
- Against that: the dietitians' own association writes that "many Canadians don't have coverage" for their services. [advocacy, 6] The Quebec order writes that some plans pay for naturopathy but not dietitians. [advocacy, 7] One Manulife plan booklet seen covers only chiropractors and physiotherapists. [first-party, 11; one plan]
- Dietitians of Canada says "all major direct-claim platforms cover dietitian services". That is about claim systems, not about which plans pay. [advocacy, 5]

## 5. Does coverage drive bookings? No evidence found

- The only claim found is one US dietitian: "My practice has grown exponentially … primarily because I am in-network." [practitioner, 14; one person; US in-network billing, unlike Canadian reimbursement plans]
- The Quebec order says unequal coverage "may steer clients toward less safe or less effective options". That is a stated concern, not a measurement. [advocacy, 7]

## 6. Receipts and regulated titles

- One plan booklet defines a covered practitioner as one who practises "by virtue of a license or certificate issued by the appropriate authority". [first-party, 11; one plan]
- So a plan that lists dietitians pays for a licensed dietitian, and an unregulated nutrition coach cannot give a receipt such a plan accepts. [derived from 11; no insurer claim rule read; what the receipt must show not checked]

## What this does not settle

- What share of plans cover dietitians, from a measured source (a CLHIA or insurer plan-design survey).
- Whether listing on a directory or saying "covered by your plan" changes bookings. No study or practitioner survey was found.
- What a reimbursable receipt must show (registration number, title), per insurer.
- Whether the ODNQ runs a public directory under its new name.
- Rules for other professions (psychologists, physiotherapists) were not checked; the regex includes them on the strength of their being regulated, not of their directories.
