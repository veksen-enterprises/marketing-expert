# Does Spain's LSSI need prior consent for cold email to businesses, and what else binds outreach to Spanish firms? (checked 2026-10-28)

Question: a freelance designer in Argentina, with zero clients, wants to write to small firms in Spain. Does Spain's information-society law (Ley 34/2002, "LSSI") art. 21 need prior consent for commercial email to businesses? What exceptions, fines and enforcement by the data agency (AEPD)? How do LinkedIn messages and calls fit (Ley 11/2022 art. 66; the Robinson opt-out list)? Does it reach a sender outside the EU?

## 1. Consent is needed for B2B email too

- Art. 21.1 bans advertising or promotional messages "por correo electrónico u otro medio de comunicación electrónica equivalente que previamente no hubieran sido solicitadas o expresamente autorizadas por los destinatarios de las mismas" (by email or an equivalent electronic means, unless the recipients asked for them or expressly allowed them first). Art. 21 never says "consumidores". [law, 1, fetch summary; Spain; consolidated text of 23 Jan 2025]
- The Anexo defines the recipient as a "persona física o jurídica que utiliza, sea o no por motivos profesionales, un servicio de la sociedad de la información" (a person or a company, whether or not acting for work). [law, 3, 4, fetch summary; Anexo as quoted by the AEPD in 2014–15]
- The AEPD applied art. 21.1 to a firm's email sent to another firm's info@ address. It found a breach, with no prior consent or contract. It closed the file because the sender had already deleted the data. [first-party, 3, fetch summary; one decision; c. 2015; no fine]
- So Spain needs prior consent for cold commercial email to businesses. [derived from 1, 3] This puts Spain with Germany, not with the UK or France, in `knowledge/outbound-and-abm.md`.

## 2. The one exception: an existing customer

- Art. 21.2: the ban does not apply "cuando exista una relación contractual previa", if the contact data were obtained lawfully and the message is about the sender's own products "similares a los que inicialmente fueron objeto de contratación con el cliente" (similar to what the client first bought). [law, 1, fetch summary; art. 21.2 last amended by Ley 9/2014]
- Collection and every message must offer a simple, free way to object; art. 22.1 lets consent be withdrawn at any time. [law, 1, fetch summary] With zero clients, the exception covers nobody. [derived]

## 3. Enforcement and fine sizes

- Breaching art. 21 is a minor offence (art. 38.4.d), fined "hasta 30.000 euros". Mass, insistent or systematic sending is serious (art. 38.3.c): "30.001 hasta 150.000 euros" (art. 39.1). The AEPD imposes these (art. 43.1). [law, 1, fetch summary; Spain; 2025 text]
- Of six AEPD art. 21 files read (2014–2021), none ended in a fine: three warnings ("apercibimiento") with an order to stop, three closures (data deleted, or time-barred). [first-party, 3–6, fetch summary; hand-picked, not a sample]
- Fines seen, all with individual recipients: €800 (PS/00092/2022) [secondary, 9]; €5,000 for WhatsApp messages sent after a deletion request (2024) [secondary, 10]; €30,001, cut by the Audiencia Nacional to €6,000 as a minor offence (2011) [court, 8].
- So a first complaint often ends in a warning; fines seen run from hundreds to a few thousand euros. [derived; no outcome statistics found]

## 4. LinkedIn messages and phone calls

- Art. 21 covers "otro medio de comunicación electrónica equivalente". The AEPD has applied it to WhatsApp. [secondary, 10] No source on LinkedIn messages was found. Treat an unsolicited sales message on LinkedIn like an email. [derived; untested]
- Calls: Ley 11/2022 art. 66.1.b gives users the right "a no recibir llamadas no deseadas con fines de comunicación comercial, salvo que exista consentimiento previo del propio usuario" or another GDPR art. 6.1 basis. [law, 2, fetch summary; cited by the Circular; in force one year after the law's publication]
- Circular 1/2023 (in force 29 Jun 2023) allows legitimate interest if a balancing test is done and documented before calling. With no contract, request or contact in the last year, the AEPD will not presume the person expects the call. [first-party, 2, fetch summary]
- Art. 5 presumes lawful use of the contact data of people who work at a company, per LOPDGDD art. 19. Art. 4: "Deberán consultarse previamente los sistemas de exclusión publicitaria" (check the Robinson list first, per LOPDGDD art. 23). [first-party, 2, fetch summary]
- The Circular does not say whether companies hold the art. 66 right. [first-party, 2] So a call to a firm's business line about its work, with a written balancing test and a Robinson check, is the most open channel. [derived]

## 5. A sender outside the EU

- LSSI art. 4: providers outside the EU/EEA follow arts. 7.2 and 11.2. Those "que dirijan sus servicios específicamente al territorio español" also follow the rest of the law. [law, 1, fetch summary; 2025 text] Writing to Spanish firms to sell them a service reads as directing services to Spain. [derived; no AEPD decision seen on this]
- GDPR art. 3(2)(a) reaches non-EU controllers offering services "to such data subjects in the Union". The EDPB guidelines do not address offers made to a business through its staff. [first-party, 11, fetch summary; v2.0, 12 Nov 2019] So whether the GDPR applies here is open. [derived]
- The AEPD closed a Robinson-list call case against a Peruvian company with no EU representative, for lack of jurisdiction. [first-party, 7, fetch summary; one case; 2022] Weak enforcement is not permission. [derived]
